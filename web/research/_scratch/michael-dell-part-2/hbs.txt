Prologue: Dell Computer's Quick Delivery to NASDAQ

    On October 27, 1997, a financial crisis in Asia set off a frenzy of activity on the U.S. stock markets.
By the end of the day, the New York Stock Exchange had fallen by 554 points and the NASDAQ
Composite, representing stocks of small and medium-sized companies, had dropped 115.1 These
were unprecedented declines, fueled by record levels of trading activity in both markets.

    The NASDAQ was the world's first electronic stock market, and a substantial portion of the trades
made by individuals--in excess of 25%--were conducted on-line.2 On October 27, the NASDAQ
received more than twenty million hits at its Internet trading site, NASDAQ.com, straining its
information-processing infrastructure. To handle the huge increase in on-line traffic, the NASDAQ
ordered eight sophisticated new server systems from Dell Computer. Thirty-six hours later, Dell
delivered the customized, fully tested systems to NASDAQ. Within three days, the exchange was
using the Dell equipment to conduct on-line transactions.3

    Even in the rapid-fire pace of the Information Age, this was an extraordinarily fast delivery of
customized high-technology products. NASDAQ's director of interactive services was astonished
that the exchange had received the systems so quickly and without being charged extra for the rapid
turnaround. "Originally Dell got in with us on price," he explained, "but that's not the issue now.
Their customer support and service [are] what's driving our relationship now."4 In the ruthless
competition that prevailed among computer makers, such loyalty was a decided strategic advantage.

    The NASDAQ order of 1997 was an accomplishment Michael Dell could hardly have dreamed of
in 1984 when, at the age of nineteen, he founded a small business to assemble personal computers. In
the intervening years, his company's operating model, strategic position, and the broader PC
industry had undergone tremendous change, much of it at breakneck speed.

    The first microcomputer or personal computer was developed in 1974 by a small firm in New
Mexico that did not long survive. The next year, PC makers in the United States shipped 43,000
machines. By 1995, annual shipments totaled almost 45 million.5 Dell Computer's expansion was no
less striking. During the company's first sixteen years, sales climbed at a compound annual rate of
more than 50%.6 By the end of the twentieth century, Dell had yearly revenues of more than $18

    Like Ford, Michael Dell was fascinated by a new technology. From the time he discovered
personal computers as a teenager, he could not keep his hands off them, constantly taking the
machines apart and reassembling them to improve their performance. The more he learned about
how computers were made and what they could do, the more certain he became that the PC was a
revolutionary innovation with wide-ranging applications for business and household consumers. "I
knew," Dell said, remembering his initial perspective on the personal computer, "that if you took this
tool, previously in the hands of a select few, and made it available to every big business, small
business, individual, and student, it could become the most important device of this century."14 From
Dell's first experiences tinkering with computers, he wanted to exploit the technological, industrial,
and other supply-side possibilities of the Information Revolution.

    But Dell had also recognized from the outset that to achieve sustained success in the young PC
industry, he and his company would also have to compete effectively on the demand side of the
market. In 1984, he had realized that how a new product such as the PC was presented, sold, and
serviced to consumers was very important, and he used this insight to build strong relationships with
customers. These connections helped Dell anticipate what consumers wanted and earn their loyalty.
By the early twenty-first century, Dell Computer's business model, which was based not only on
efficient manufacturing and "mass customization," but also direct connections with buyers, had
become the industry standard.15 Millions of business and home users around the world associated the
Dell brand with quality information technology (IT) products, direct sales, customer control, and
responsive service and support.

Evolution of the Personal Computer Industry

    The technological foundation of the PC--the microprocessor--was invented in 1969. That year,
Marcian E. "Ted" Hoff, an engineer working for the newly formed Intel Corporation in what became
known as the Silicon Valley region of California, figured out how to create a silicon chip that would
serve as a kind of functional headquarters for small-scale computers. The microprocessor or
"computer on a chip" used software instructions to execute a wide range of operations for
calculators, computers, and other machines. Because it eliminated the need to build hard-wired
circuits for each individual operation, the microchip laid the groundwork for radically decentralized
computing capabilities.

    The development of the microprocessor also initiated an intense race to install ever more power
onto a silicon wafer. Gordon Moore, one of the founders of Intel, predicted that the number of
transistors that could be put onto the same size chip would double every year. (He later extended the
period to eighteen months).16 His prediction became known as "Moore's Law," and unlike most such
prophecies, it has proven remarkably accurate.17

    As productivity galloped ahead, the cost of any function that a microchip could perform declined
dramatically. Falling computing costs helped focus industry managers, engineers, and others'
attention on exploiting the latest and most efficient microprocessing innovations. Declining costs also
helped make hundreds of applications practicable that were once prohibitively expensive. In the last
quarter of the twentieth century, microprocessing chips that automatically controlled a myriad of
operations were incorporated into cars, cash registers, garage door openers, kitchen appliances, and
many other products.18

    The personal computer, of course, was the most important application of microchip technology. In
1974, Ed Roberts, an Albuquerque, New Mexico businessman, developed the first PC. He designed
the machine around the latest Intel microprocessor, the 8080, calling his invention the Altair after a
star in the Aquila constellation.19

    The Altair, a bulky box about the size of a case of soda or beer, had no keyboard, monitor, external
memory, or printer.20 Its internal memory was a scanty 256 bytes. (By contrast, most desktop PCs in
the late 1990s contained 64 million bytes of memory). Targeted at hobbyists, the Altair sold for $395
in kit form and $621 preassembled.21 To enter programs and data, users flipped switches on the front
of the computer. Results appeared as patterns of flashing lights above the switches. Roberts'
innovation proved very popular and within a few months, he had a backlog of 4000 orders.22 Most of
these customers were electronic hobbyists and programmers.23

    A host of start-up companies, many of them based in northern California garages, emerged to
produce machines similar to the Altair. Some of these enterprises, like Apple Computer, quickly
became major players in the PC industry. Larger, established companies, including Tandy Radio
Shack, Commodore, and Atari, also began producing PCs.

    The most popular of the early machines, the Apple II, was introduced in 1977. The brainchild of
Steve Wozniak, Steve Jobs, and Mike Markkula, it was the first PC to look and feel like a consumer
product. Unlike competing machines, the Apple II was aesthetically appealing. Housed in a sleek
case of beige plastic, the computer included a full-sized keyboard and supported color graphics as
well as text. A small television monitor and floppy disk drive were optional peripherals. Numerous
software programs, such as games and word processing, could run on the machine. The Apple II
enjoyed tremendous commercial success, opening a market that reached beyond hobbyists to include
business customers, initially in primarily engineering-related industries.24 At the end of 1980, more
than 120,000 units had been sold, and Apple had established a leading position in the young PC
industry.25

    Meanwhile, continued improvements in microprocessing capacity enhanced the PC's memory
and speed. Such efficiency gains, coupled with the PC's growing popularity, greatly stimulated
software production, as hundreds of hobbyists created programs to run on the new machines. Most
of the early software inventors were enthusiasts who created computer languages for their own use.
Harvard undergraduate Bill Gates and his high school friend Paul Allen, however, saw significant
commercial possibilities in manufacturing software.26 When they first discovered the Altair in 1975,
they decided to go into business adapting computer languages for the PC and perhaps other similar
machines. Convinced that there was no time to lose in exploiting this window of opportunity, Gates

Early Marketing and Distribution

    By early 1980, the worldwide installed base of personal computers totaled almost 610,000
machines, almost two-thirds of which were in the United States.28 On both the supply and demand
sides, the PC market remained a collection of dynamic, but disparate, cottage industries. Hardware
and software manufacturers designed their products for one particular computer; virtually every
model on the market was incompatible with competing models.29 In this scattered marketplace, early
PC consumers had few industry standards or generally recognized attributes on which to base
purchasing decisions. Most relied on word-of-mouth recommendations from colleagues, friends, and
salespeople.

    Individual manufacturers reached potential customers by advertising in specialty magazines,
trade shows, and computer clubs. The Altair and other early PCs were distributed principally by mail
order. But as the market expanded, new outlets developed, including retail stores dedicated to
personal computers. During the late 1970s and early 1980s, PC retailers multiplied, stimulated partly
by manufacturers' interest in distributing their products as efficiently as possible.30 In 1975, for
example, Roberts began to create a network of franchised distributors to sell the Altair.31 Tandy Radio
Shack, which began manufacturing personal computers in 1977, sold PCs through its national chain
of electronics stores and its newly created Radio Shack Computer Centers, dedicated PC stores that
offered both Tandy products and those of leading manufacturers. The company's sales of computer-
related products boomed, climbing from 1.8% of its North American revenues in 1978 to almost 13%
in 1980.32 ComputerLand, a retail franchise operation for PCs started in the late 1970s with the aid of
California-based PC manufacturer IMSAI, opened its first store in 1977. Two years later it had more
than 100 outlets, four years after that, in 1983, there were almost 460.33

    By 1980, the PC industry was well established, especially among hobbyists and business users
with engineering or computer experience.34 Over 490,000 units were shipped that year, totaling more
than $1 billion in sales.35 This was a small market relative to those for mainframes and
minicomputers, but it was growing at an annual rate of more than 30%.36 New and existing
companies wanted a piece of the action, and in the early 1980s, Osborne, Kaypro, Xerox, Hewlett-
Packard, DEC and Wang in the United States--along with European makers such as Olivetti and
Philips, and Japanese manufacturers NEC, Toshiba, and Fujitsu--began producing PCs.37

    The most important entrant to the PC market, however, was IBM. More than any other single
company in the 1980s, IBM transformed the personal computer industry, breaking open the business
market for the new machines by making them necessary equipment for a wide range of companies
and inadvertently attracting a host of new competitors, such as Dell Computer.

    The transformation unfolded quickly. In 1980, an IBM task force was given some 20 months to
develop a personal computer and build a national and global marketing organization for it.38 Pressed
for time, the task force decided that IBM would build its PC from components made by outside
suppliers rather than developing its own proprietary hardware and applications. The task force also
decided to leave the introduction of specific peripherals to third parties, basing the new computer on
an open architecture, meaning system specifications would be made public.39

    IBM contracted with Intel to produce the PC's microprocessor.40 For the machine's operating
system, task force members initially approached Gary Kildall, a Northern California software
developer who had designed a popular operating system compatible with Intel-based

microcomputers.41 After an early meeting with Kildall did not go well, IBM turned to Bill Gates and
Paul Allen of Microsoft. Now based in Seattle, the two entrepreneurs had already agreed to supply
IBM with a number of programming languages for the PC.42

    Microsoft did not have an operating system in 1980, and there was no time to develop one from
scratch. So Gates purchased the licensing rights to a viable product, QDOS (Quick and Dirty
Operating System), from Seattle Computer Products, a local company. He and his Microsoft
colleagues then made the system compatible with the PC that IBM was developing, renaming the
operating system MS-DOS (Microsoft Disk Operating System).43 In exchange for supplying IBM with
an operating system, Microsoft received a one-time payment of $80,000.44 Microsoft, however,
maintained control of the software, retaining the right to license it to other companies. Although
neither party realized it at the time, this was destined to be one of the most important business deals
of the twentieth century.

    The IBM PC was officially launched in August 1981, right on schedule. Its base price was $1,595,
which included one floppy drive, 64 kilobytes (K) of random-access memory (RAM), and a monitor.
With software applications, a printer, and other peripherals, the entire package cost close to $3,000.
The IBM PC was an extraordinary triumph. In the first full year of production, the company's
microcomputing revenues topped $500 million. Three years later, they had skyrocketed to $5.5
billion, the fastest recorded revenue growth in industrial history.45

    IBM's new product created the first truly mass market for the personal computer. U.S. shipments
of PCs, mostly IBM or IBM-compatible machines, soared from 760,000 in 1981 to 2.5 million the
following year and more than 6 million in 1985.46 But it was not individual consumers that fed the
exploding market.47 Less than one in six U.S. households owned a PC in 1985.48 The vast majority of
personal computers--about 80%--were purchased by corporate and other business customers.
(Exhibit 1).49

    IBM segregated the customer base for its new product, leveraging its powerful brand, sales force,
service organization, and established customer relationships to sell PCs to large corporate clients and
relying on retailers such as Sears and ComputerLand to distribute the product to small and medium-
sized businesses. To promote its PC to these latter customers, IBM ran a massive print and television
advertising campaign featuring a Charlie-Chaplin-like tramp who used the PC to solve business
problems.50

    This two-pronged marketing strategy proved effective both in legitimizing the personal computer
in the minds of data-processing managers and in accommodating the scattered small business
customer base that generated average purchases insufficient to support the cost of the company's
sales force.51 IBM's effort to build a large market for PCs among smaller companies was extremely
successful, exceeding all expectations.52

    The success of the IBM PC created a tremendous opportunity. In the early and mid-1980s,
hundreds of companies, mainly start-ups such as Compaq, Dell, and AST, began to build IBM-
compatible machines (also termed "clones") using IBM's open architecture as their blueprint and
buying Intel chips and Microsoft's operating system. At the same time, countless software engineers
and amateurs hurried to develop applications for IBM-compatible machines. As the market exploded
in the 1980s, common standards emerged for software and hardware. By 1985, most PC-related
development and production efforts were aimed at IBM-compatible PCs. (Apple Computer, which
kept both its hardware and software proprietary, remained an important exception.)

    Without Big Blue's corporate overhead and sales costs, most of these companies, or "clones" as
they came to be called, could undersell IBM. Price and, thus, manufacturing costs rapidly became the

most important drivers of competition among clone makers. It was fierce rivalry, driven principally
by supply-side factors, and price wars erupted frequently. In this environment, few companies

    This was the stage on which Michael Dell chose to make his debut. The intensity of the
competition in the personal computer industry did not intimidate him, even as a college freshman.
Nor did IBM's size or remarkable success with the PC. Dell believed that he could capitalize on the
company's triumph by assembling IBM-compatible computers and providing consumers better
service, more choice, and a superior buying experience than was currently available.

Dell's Beginnings

    Michael was born in Houston on February 23, 1965, the second of three sons of Alexander and
Lorraine Dell. Alexander, an orthodontist, and Lorraine, a stockbroker, had met and married in
Brooklyn, New York, before moving to Houston in the early 1960s.

    The Dell household was a lively one, filled with the activities of three smart boys. Michael and his
two brothers, Steven and Adam, competed at ping-pong and other sports. When the games grew
rowdy, Alexander Dell would admonish his sons to play civilly together. Then, he would take each
child aside and say, "But win."55 Family dinner table discussions centered on economic subjects, such
as the Federal Reserve's policies, the oil crisis, inflation, and promising stocks. "In our household,"
Michael Dell explained, "you couldn't help being aware of commercial opportunities."56

    By the time Michael was age 9, he had a checking account. At age 12, he became interested in
stamp collecting, taking a job as a bus boy in a nearby Chinese restaurant to finance his purchases.
Rather than buy stamps from retail dealers, who earned a fee, Michael decided to create his own
small business buying and selling. "Then I could learn even more about stamps," he remembered,
"and collect a commission in the process."57 He obtained a reseller's license and lobbied neighbors to
consign stamps from their collections to him.58 Michael then advertised "Dell's Stamps" in a national
trade journal, typing and mailing out a twelve-page catalogue to support the ad. "Much to my
surprise, I made $2,000," he said. "And I learned an early powerful lesson about the rewards of
eliminating the middleman."59

    At age 16, he took a part-time job as assistant subscription coordinator for the Houston Post. The
position involved cold-calling names from listings provided by the telephone company. Michael
quickly realized that two groups of people were especially interested in buying a newspaper
subscription: newlyweds and those who had recently moved into a new residence. In his first effort at
customer segmentation, Michael devised ways of reaching these groups, hiring friends to collect
names and addresses of couples who had recently applied for a marriage license. The teenagers also
obtained lists of applicants from specialized home mortgage companies. Michael contacted those
with the highest mortgages first, using his Apple II computer to write each customer a personalized
letter and offer them a newspaper subscription. Subscriptions came in by the thousands.60 By the time
he was 17, Michael had become the Post's top salesman, earning commissions of $18,000 in a single
year.61

Dell's Passion for Computers

    Michael had bought his first electronic calculator at age seven, "fascinated by the idea of a
machine that could compute things."62 As he grew older, computers absorbed more and more of his
attention. He constantly sought opportunities to learn how they worked and what they could do.
When his junior high school installed its first teletype terminal, Michael was hooked. "If you stayed
after school," he recalled, "you could play around with it and write programs or input equations and
get back answers. It was the most amazing thing I'd ever seen."63

    His computer interests expanded. In high school, Michael worked as a student assistant to the
principal, writing administrative programs to track student absences and plan school budgets.64 He
logged long hours on the school's mainframe terminals.65 After classes and on weekends, he went to a
local Radio Shack to use the PCs there. Michael also devoured computer magazines and became
fascinated by the electronic architecture of the Apple II and other popular machines. He begged his
parents to let him have his own PC, and for his fifteenth birthday, they consented. As soon as he got
his hands on the new, expensive Apple II, he took it apart to see how it was made. His parents were
furious.66

    A short time after Michael had acquired his Apple II, IBM introduced its PC. The teenager was
immediately struck by the new computer's commercial possibilities and soon switched machines.
"While the Apple had lots of games," he said, "at the time, the IBM was more powerful. It had
software and programs for business usage, and although I didn't have a lot of business experience, I
had enough to know that this PC was going to be the choice for business in the future."67

    In June 1982, unbeknownst to his parents, Michael skipped school for several days to attend a
national computer conference that was being held in the Houston Astrodome. For the first time, the
sixteen-year-old got a real sense of the burgeoning PC industry. The conference opened his eyes to a
world of component makers, PC manufacturers, related equipment suppliers, and distributors. The
conference intensified his interest in understanding the economics of making and selling PCs, and
Michael set up a computerized bulletin board--a forerunner of email--to exchange notes with others
interested in the business.

    At the same time, he began purchasing components and peripherals, such as memory, disk drives,
and monitors, to improve his own PC's performance. Then he began to buy and improve used
machines. "I would enhance a PC the way another guy would soup up a car, and then I would sell it
for a profit and do it again," Michael Dell explained.68

    Although enrolled at the University of Texas at Austin in the fall of 1983, Dell devoted less time
and energy to his premed classes than to computers. Working from his dorm room, he continued to
upgrade IBM PCs, buying machines from local dealers with excess inventory and enhancing their
performance before selling them to Austin business people at 10 to 15% below retail.69

    Supply was not a problem as rapidly escalating demand for IBM PCs occasioned inefficiencies in
the distribution system that often left retailers with too many or too few machines. Monthly quotas
imposed by IBM frequently exceeded what retailers could sell.70 The reverse was also the case. "A
dealer," Dell explained, "would order 100 computers and receive only 10. So the next time, to get
what he wanted, he would order 1,000. Maybe then he'd get 633, when he'd needed only 100."71
Retail dealers often bought more PCs than they needed in order to qualify for discounts on large
orders.

    A "gray market" outside the established channels quickly developed to sell machines that dealers
were left with. Rather than carry excess inventories, distributors sold extra PCs at a reduced markup

over wholesale to small resellers such as Dell. This tactic, explained an authorized IBM dealer,
allowed an individual distributor to "show IBM that he was a `high volume seller'" and entitled to
"favorable treatment from the company when, in fact, he was a major conduit for the gray market."72
These informal outlets fueled the industry's expansion. "There was no discipline at all in the
marketplace," the IBM dealer stated, "and so the gray market in the machines just took off."73 Michael
Dell was one of many aspiring entrepreneurs operating in this dynamic shadow market.

    The PC industry was too young in the early 1980s to have generated extensive market research.
What little evidence there was indicated that price mattered a great deal to potential consumers. Two-
thirds of business buyers and 61% of home users polled in 1981 rated price as the most important
criterion in selecting a personal computer. Ease of expansion, programming possibilities, and
reliability were among other relevant considerations.74

    Dell had little access to consumer survey research, which was too costly for any but the largest
manufacturers to afford. But he had considerable firsthand experience with computers and their
users. His years of informally studying the industry revealed several important business
opportunities.

    Some of these related to the price of PCs. Dell figured out, for example, that although an IBM PC
typically retailed for about $3,000, its components--which were not proprietary to IBM--wholesaled
for approximately $600 or $700. This meant that retailers typically paid $2,000 for an IBM machine
and marked it up about $1,000, a figure that Dell considered unreasonably high, given the limited
functions retailers were performing.75 He believed that retailers could and should be answering
consumers' questions and allaying their anxieties about PCs, demonstrating how the complicated
new product operated, and feeding back to manufacturers valuable information about consumer
priorities, complaints, and reactions that could be incorporated in production, marketing, and other
supply-side decisions.76

    But as the 18-year-old Dell saw it in 1983, PC distributors were falling far short of their potential.
Most offered PC consumers little or no product support. "The people operating the computer stores
didn't even know much about PCs," he recalled. "For the most part, they had previously sold stereos
or cars and thought that computers represented the next `big ticket' fad, so they figured, `Why not?'"77

    Meanwhile, the rapid proliferation of these new products posed numerous problems for users. In
the early 1980s, few secretaries and administrative assistants knew how to set up a personal
computer. Few business managers understood what "system error" and other messages flashing
across their PC monitors meant, and few medium-sized and virtually no small companies had an
information technology (IT) department with a dedicated help desk.

    Not only business consumers, but distributors as well, were openly clamoring for help. In the
months after introducing its PC, IBM received thousands of calls on toll-free lines that it had installed
for quality complaints. Most callers were not trying to resolve quality problems, but rather obtain
operating information or technical support that retailers were not providing.78 Frustrated dealers
themselves contacted IBM to learn how to respond to the queries they were receiving from end-users.
The situation was even worse with companies that made IBM-compatible machines. Competing
principally on price, most of the clones generally offered little or no customer support.

    In the emerging chaos, Dell perceived another important opportunity related to how PCs were
sold. He became convinced that many business consumers cared about more than price and
computing power. They also wanted, he believed, knowledgeable, responsive service, after-sales
support, and some say in how their machines were configured. But in 1983, few manufacturers or
retailers were providing these benefits, particularly to small and medium-sized companies. Dell's

informal, one-person PC assembly shop was a striking exception. Working from his dorm room, the

    Dell initially targeted experienced users in small and medium-sized businesses such as legal and
medical offices, banks, manufacturing operations, and government agencies. Most of these
consumers, he found, understood the power of the emerging standard for personal computers,
wanted higher performance at less cost than they could get from PCs available through IBM-
authorized dealers, and frequently desired some control over the configuration of their machines. He
also realized that these users valued informed, responsive service, yet required less information and
product support than most first-time buyers. Dell believed that this consumer segment was
underserved by most PC makers and distributors.

Getting Started

    In the fall of 1983, Dell began approaching potential customers. He acquired a vendor's license,
which enabled him to bid on computing contracts for the state of Texas, and subsequently landed
several government orders. He also placed ads in local newspapers and sold door-to-door at Austin
law offices and other small businesses.79 As word of Dell's enterprise spread, sales escalated. By early
1984 Dell was selling $50,000 to $80,000 a month in upgraded PCs and components.80

    Michael's parents grew increasingly worried that he was neglecting his academic work for
business interests. In the fall, Alexander and Lorraine Dell flew to Austin for a surprise visit to their
son, calling him from the airport. Michael barely had time to hide the computers in his roommate's
shower before they arrived.81 Arguments erupted between the son and his parents. During one of
their discussions, Alexander Dell asked Michael what he wanted to do with his life. "I want to
compete with IBM!" the son replied. As Michael remembered it, his father "was not amused."82

On His Own

    Over his parents' objections, Dell moved his operation into a two-bedroom condominium. In
January 1984, he registered the name PC's Limited with the State of Texas and, four months later,
incorporated the company as Dell Computer Corporation with an initial investment of $1,000. After
hiring several people to take and fill phone orders, he moved his operation again--this time to a
1,000-square-foot office. In May 1984, Dell decided to devote all his energies to his growing business.
The decision to leave school was not an easy one, and he arranged to take a leave of absence from the
university. "Where I come from," he recalled, "not going to college is not an acceptable option.
Convincing my parents to allow me to leave school would have been impossible. So I just went ahead
and did it, whatever the consequences. I finished my freshman year and left. After a while, my
parents forgave me. A little bit after that, I forgave them, too."83

    By the summer of 1984, monthly sales of Dell's upgraded IBM PCs had reached $180,000 and
continued to rise at dizzying speeds.84 The company moved three times between June and late
December 1984, outgrowing not only its headquarters, but its phone system, electronics

    Like most entrepreneurs running a new venture, Dell had his hands in everything: working the
phone lines, planning product features, managing finances, and hiring employees. He logged 18-hour
days, often sleeping on a cot in the office.86 Mundane operating issues followed hard on the heels of
broader strategic and organizational problems. What time should the office open? How should
phone orders be taken, compiled, and relayed to employees assembling PCs?87 By what criteria
should the organization evaluate prospective and existing workers? How much inventory was
needed when sales were doubling monthly? Who were the most important competitors and what
were they doing?

    Much of what Dell and his colleagues accomplished early on was a result of learning by doing, of
inventing solutions to problems as they occurred and trying not to make the same mistake twice.
Rapid growth meant constant change--in staff, financial management, supplier agreements, office
infrastructure, and operating policies. Dell remembered how he and his first employees approached
issues related to upgrading and, later, manufacturing PCs:

        We'd say "What's the best way to do this?" and come up with an answer. The resulting
    process would work for a while, then it would stop working and we'd have to adjust it and try
    something else. For example, we had started by writing orders by hand and pinning them up
    on a clothesline. After a while it became clear that this system couldn't handle the volume of
    orders coming in. So we hired a guy to write an order-entry program. But because our
    computers weren't networked, the salespeople would enter orders on their PCs and I would go
    around collecting the orders on diskettes and compile all of them into an order database. The
    whole thing was one big experiment.88

A New Model

    Although he had gotten started in the PC business by upgrading IBM machines, in 1985 Dell
decided that his company should make, market, and distribute its own brand of IBM-compatible
machines. In July, the company's Turbo PC appeared under the PC's Limited name. Base price was
$795, less than half the price of IBM's PC.89 Two months later, the firm introduced its first product
based on the leading-edge technology at the time, Intel's 286 microprocessor.90 New models followed
in April and August of 1986.91 Priced aggressively below IBM personal computers and other
compatibles, each new line outsold the previous one.92 At the close of the 1985 business year, Dell
sales topped $30 million.93

    Making his own products eliminated Dell's dependence on PC distributors for supplies of
stripped-down machines to meet growing demand. Manufacturing his own machines also increased
control over product development and manufacturing, enabling Dell to better exploit the rapidly
expanding capabilities and declining costs of computing technology. The decision helped the young
company communicate with consumers more effectively by exploiting its direct sales model. For
several years, Michael Dell had been skeptical of PC retailers' ability to meet customer's changing
needs for a new product. Most third-party sellers, he believed, were not well informed about the PCs
they distributed. Many were also unsuccessful in relaying consumers' priorities back to
manufacturers. This indirect channel, according to Dell, "was based on a marriage of the unknowing
buyer and the unknowledgeable seller." As the PC market developed and users grew more familiar
and demanding with the technology, Dell was certain that such a "marriage could not last."94

    Producing his own machines also afforded Dell a means to differentiate his company's offerings
from those of other clone makers. In 1985, hundreds of companies were making IBM-compatible
machines. Most were small start-ups, but a few were enjoying tremendous success simply by
undercutting IBM's prices.95 Prominent among these was Compaq Computer. Within three years of
its founding in 1982, the Houston-based company's annual sales topped $500 million, earning it a
place in the Fortune 500.96

    Compaq sold its products through third-party distribution networks. Developed by a former IBM
executive, the company's retail strategy relied on outlets such as ComputerLand and Sears, many of
which were already selling IBM PCs, but willing to add Compaq products because of their slightly
higher profit margins. Compaq, moreover, had no direct sales force to compete with retailers' stores
and its promised steady stream of inventory would be a welcome respite from IBM's frequent supply
problems.97

    Although he viewed Compaq's rapid growth as evidence that a huge market existed for high-
performance IBM-compatible machines, Dell was unimpressed with Compaq's strategy for putting
its products in front of consumers. Selling through independent dealers forced Compaq to share
retail space and salespeople with some of its strongest competitors and prevented it from ensuring
that third-party representatives thoroughly understood its products and their distinctive attributes.
More important, it was an impediment to tailoring product development, manufacturing, marketing,
and distribution efforts to consumers' changing preferences.

    In the mid-1980s, Dell began to formalize a different business model based on personal interaction
with consumers. Individual customers typically placed orders via toll-free calls. Institutional
consumers were initially cold-called by Dell sales representatives trained to work with particular
customer segments. Order information was relayed to the company's nearby factory, which
assembled computers according to customer specifications. Completed orders were shipped directly
to customers within one to three weeks.98

    Dell's "made-to-order" sales and manufacturing model grew out of its founder's insights into the
demand side of the personal computer market. But it yielded important supply-side benefits as well.
By eliminating third-party salespeople (and their customarily high retail margins), Dell's company's
could reduce its distribution costs substantially below those of its rivals. Many of these savings were
passed onto customers, enabling Dell Computer to profitably undersell IBM and others. In 1986, for
example, a line of Dell's personal computers based on the 286 microprocessor chip sold for between
$2,500 and $3,900. Comparable machines from IBM were priced between $4,600 and $5,800; those
from Compaq between $4,300 and $5,000.99

    The direct model also incurred lower inventory costs. Because it built computers after customers
ordered them, Dell Computer carried almost no inventory of finished machines. PC makers such as
IBM and Compaq had to maintain substantial product inventories to stock reseller and retail
channels. Building to consumer order, rather than to demand forecasts, also kept Dell's materials
inventories relatively low, a boon in an industry characterized by rapid technological innovation.
"Component prices," Dell explained:

    are always falling as suppliers introduce faster chips, bigger disk drives, and modems with
    ever-greater bandwidth. Let's say Dell has eight days of inventory. Compare that to an indirect
    competitor who has twenty-five days of inventory with another thirty in their distribution
    channel. That's a difference of forty-seven days, and in forty-seven days the cost of materials
    will decline about 6%.100

    In its broad outlines, Dell's model of direct selling was not entirely new. Corporate sales forces
such as IBM's, had long dealt face-to-face with institutional customers and consumer products
companies such as Fuller Brush and Avon Cosmetics employed door-to-door salespeople to market
goods to individuals. Dell's model was perhaps most akin to mail-order or catalogue distribution. By
eliminating wholesalers and other related costs, catalogue retailers such as L. L. Bean and Spiegel had
realized economies similar to those enjoyed by Dell. But none of these other companies were
positioned to offer products "made to order" for individual customers, as Dell was able to do.

    Dell was not the only PC maker to harness the possibilities of direct selling by phone and mail. In
the mid-1980s, a score of clones such as TIPC Network (later renamed Gateway) distributed IBM-
compatible machines by mail order. But virtually all of these players competed solely on price. Only a
handful offered reliable service or support and even fewer manufactured computers to customer
specifications. None invested the resources Dell did to build strong connections with, and understand
the priorities and earn the trust of, consumers.

Building the Brand and Organization

    Dell Computer's sales increased rapidly in the mid-1980s, climbing from $6 million in fiscal 1985
to $70 million two years later.101 Even as its market presence expanded, industry experts were openly
skeptical of its strategy. Some argued that businesses and individual consumers would not continue
to buy costly new products they could not see from a manufacturer they did not know; others
alluded to the dubious public reputation of other direct sellers. "Most mail-order houses," wrote one
industry reporter in 1987, "were fly-by-night outfits of marginal reputation that imported cheap PC
clones from the Far East and resold them at bargain basement prices."102 Still others criticized Dell's
underlying business model, asserting that direct marketing to PC users would devolve into a price
war in which profit margins for manufacturers, and in some cases distributors, would be competed
away.

    Michael Dell's response to these challenges was to build a powerful brand based on high-quality
machines and outstanding service. He did not have many compelling examples to follow. Only a
handful of computer companies such as IBM, Hewlett-Packard, and Apple had earned widespread
consumer recognition and trust for their brands. Both IBM and Hewlett-Packard enjoyed the benefits
of longstanding reputations and customer relationships that predated the personal computer. Apple,
although a relatively young company, had invested heavily in forging a strong brand based on user
accessibility, elegant design, and the creative possibilities of technology. Its name, logo, and products
were well known to both business and home consumers.103

    But most other PC makers in the mid-1980s had not succeeded in establishing a meaningful name,
symbol, or identity that consistently differentiated their offerings and commanded buyer loyalty.
Major IBM clone makers, such as Compaq and Epson, and large mail-order firms, such as Northgate
and Compuadd, based their marketing appeals primarily on cost, pricing their products higher than
those of smaller PC makers, but lower than those of IBM and Apple.104

    Some of the clones emphasized specific functional attributes such as processing speed.
"Megahertz wars" broke out often as individual manufacturers scrambled to offer the fastest
machine. In 1987, for example, many clones produced machines that ran at 8, 10, 12, and even 16
megahertz (millions of operations per second). Compaq, Leading Edge, and other producers also
advertised their dealer networks and product support. But price remained the overriding emphasis of
IBM-clone makers.105

    Dell, however, believed that consumers wanted more than good technical performance at low
prices. He was convinced that buyers wanted a satisfying buying experience with informed, helpful
sales people and effective product support and service after the sale. He suspected that buyers also
desired a strong voice in configuring their computers and peripheral equipment. All of these, Dell
reasoned, would only grow more important as the market matured and consumers' familiarity with
PCs increased. He intended to develop an appealing brand that customers would associate with
current technology, customer control, and responsive service.106 "I'd much rather design the winning
system to sell and support customers," he insisted, "than design an incredibly technically proficient
microprocessor that nobody wants to buy. I mean, that's the problem with our industry: There are a
lot of technologists who will create wonderful things and then go try to find people who want to buy
them. We started with the customer and then worked our way back."107

Segmenting the Market

    Dell's strategy was neither easy to implement nor inexpensive. Training sales representatives,
offering effective customer service and support, and configuring machines to buyer specifications
increased operating costs and, potentially, PC prices. Dell's solution to creating a distinctive brand
and manufacturing personal PCs that rivaled other IBM-compatibles in price was to segment the
market.

    This was hardly new territory for Dell, who, at age sixteen, had enjoyed considerable success
identifying specific newspaper customers. Several years later working in the young PC market, Dell
based his segmentation strategy on potential consumers' familiarity with PC technology rather than
on their address, marital status, income, or other frequently used criteria. He decided to focus the
company's strategic efforts directly on knowledgeable users--people, who, like Dell, envisioned a
growing number of applications for the PC, who desired a say in configuring the machines they
bought, and who wanted to work with expert salespeople and responsive service technicians.

    Dell Computer began introducing products, services, and other brand attributes that appealed
specifically to these users. Most of them were men who enjoyed keeping tabs on practical hi-tech
developments--pragmatic individuals who read computer magazines and tended to be interested in
superior PC performance.108 This segment's understanding of and facility with new technologies
made these customers an influential source of information for additional purchasers. Colleagues,
family members, and friends often took their high-tech cues from such knowledgeable people,
watching what they bought and asking their advice.109

    Also represented in this customer segment were managers in small and medium-sized companies.
Too small to be served by the likes of IBM's sales force, most of these firms bought PCs from third-
party retailers or value-added resellers (VARs), that typically offered little in the way of sales
information, service, or specialized configurations. But these kinds of benefits, Dell reasoned, could
be provided to targeted users on a cost-effective basis. Telephone ordering, for example, was ideally
suited to knowledgeable business customers who did not require hands-on product demonstrations
or extensive explanations and tended to have fewer product questions than less experienced users.

    To serve this segment, Dell Computer hired and trained a growing sales force. For the company's
first three years, sales personnel worked primarily off toll-free telephone lines, making cold calls to,
taking orders from, and answering questions for potential business customers, initially in Texas and
the Southwest and later around the country. By 1987, the telemarketing group was fielding more than
1,700 calls a day from around the country.110 A 30-day money-back guarantee was offered to allay
consumer fears about buying a PC sight unseen. Dell also offered a one-year limited warranty with

Listening to Customers

    As sales climbed, Dell's direct relationship with customers provided important demand-side
intelligence that affected a host of strategic and operating decisions.122 Human resource executives
and department heads based staffing plans and production levels partly on market estimates
gathered through thousands of daily customer interactions. Purchasing managers used these same
estimates when ordering from suppliers to reduce inventory costs.

     Sales and marketing executives used customer information to evaluate evolving consumer
preferences and measured response rates from newspaper ads, catalogues, and other communication
initiatives. Equally important, sales representatives relied on customers' order histories to help
configure new purchases.123 Because the sales division had access to buyers' previous purchases,
explained Hershel Hochman, vice president of manufacturing in the late 1980s, "we can determine
what equipment they already have and their complete repair history. This gives us the opportunity to
ensure that customers are ordering equipment appropriate for their needs. You have a sort of

inventory control that you never have on the retail level. You can save yourself a bundle. . . [by not]

Computer tailor its product innovation efforts and actual offerings to changing industry conditions.
In 1987, for example, Dell decided not to invest in IBM's Personal System/2 (PS/2) technology. Most

PCs; it was slower than other machines; and it was incompatible with some existing components and
peripheral equipment. Relying on its own market information, Dell Computer chose instead to
expand and upgrade its own line of PCs based on Intel's existing 286 and 386 microprocessors. In

compatible machines. The new design proved not only to be more efficient than the one in the PS/2

industry standard, and Dell produced several successful lines of computers based on it.125

defects in individual customers' equipment and to rapidly improve Dell's overall product line. In
responding to calls, technicians logged customer complaints into a database. If an individual
customer called the support hot line again, a Dell technician could instantly call up a record of the
consumer's previous difficulties. Executives and engineers then used all of this accumulated
information to improve computer assembly. When "we get more than a few complaints," commented
a Dell marketing manager in the late 1980s, "I go about 130 feet to the people who design the thing.
Within five or six hours, engineering has fixed the design, and within two or three days, the factory's
got that change incorporated on the line."126

    Consumer feedback also helped Dell's manufacturing managers evaluate suppliers and
subcontractors. If, for example, a significant number of machines purchased by an institutional
customer exhibited a particular problem, Dell engineers not only tried to solve the problem, but also
alerted the vendor of the offending part.127

performance. Within six months of its opening in late 1986, an 82,000-square-foot factory in north

had lent the firm $500,000 to finance its initial operations--a loan that the company repaid by the end

placement was completed in 1987 in the wake of the October stock market crash. But investors were
enthusiastic, and the firm raised more than $21 million.132 As sales and net income grew in the late

Targeting Large Corporations

    First, they enlarged the company's field of target customers to include Fortune 2,000 and other
large corporations. In 1987, Dell already had some big accounts, such as Dow Chemical and Arthur
Andersen. These companies had been attracted initially by Dell's customization capabilities as well as
its competitive pricing.138 But the bulk of its revenues still came from small and medium-sized
companies.

    Dell executives appreciated the advantages of selling to large institutions. Corporate customers
often bought 50 or more computers at a time, creating potential economies of scale in marketing and
support. These large customers also upgraded their information technology systems more frequently
than household and small business buyers, opening the door to the possibility of repeat sales with
relatively high payoffs. Big companies and government agencies also often purchased computer-
related products such as modems, disk drives, printers, and software packages, which tended to have
high profit margins and low distribution costs. Successful relationships with greater numbers of
Fortune 2,000 customers, moreover, would eventually make Dell a safer choice for chief information
officers, an advantage exploited by IBM and its powerful brand for decades.

    In targeting large institutions, Dell Computer was effectively taking on acknowledged industry
leader IBM, which had built its strategy, brand, and organization on selling to the large institutional
market.139 Since introducing the PC in 1981, IBM's share of the office personal computer market had
climbed to nearly 40%.140 But this did not dissuade Dell, Walker and their colleagues. Michael had
long wanted to beat IBM at its own game at least in the PC market, and he was optimistic about his
chances. He had predicted that as the young PC market matured, the number of sophisticated
corporate buyers would grow. These customers, in turn, would be receptive to reliable, high-
performance machines configured to user specifications, well supported, and priced below
comparable IBM products.141

    Dell and his senior executives planned to pursue both established and newer routes to large
institutional consumers. Proven methods included supplementing print ads in computer magazines
with direct-mail initiatives that targeted administrators and purchasing agents in specific
organizations. Dell also planned to assemble a team of initially about 25 people to specialize in direct
sales to major customers. These sales representatives would call potential corporate customers by
phone from their homes, a practice Dell senior management had concluded would be much less
expensive than supporting a traditional sales force in the field.142 Even using such cost-saving
measures, getting Dell products in front of large institutional buyers would be an expensive
proposition. In addition to hiring and training a larger sales group, Dell had to augment its on-site
service and product-support efforts.

    Dell also had to convince large corporations that purchasing computers through the mail was as
safe as buying them from an IBM representative or authorized dealer. Toward this end, Dell sales
people were trained to provide an abundance of relevant information to prospective customers. Each
representative was required to complete a five-week program covering a range of subjects from the
latest PC technology to Dell product lines. As they advanced in the organization, sales reps received
additional training in specific skills such as creating new sales territories and handling large
accounts.143 Dell sales people were also expected to participate in a variety of hands-on exercises
designed to enhance their customer service skills. They were required, for example, to unpack their
own PCs from cartons and set their machines up. Most sales people, Dell speculated, "probably
didn't enjoy it, but it gave them and us a real sense of what the uneducated customer would go
through to set up his system and it helped them develop a more intimate understanding of the
products they were selling."144

Moving Abroad

    Dell and his executive team made the equally important strategic decision to seek out
international markets. By the late 1980s, nearly half of worldwide sales of personal computers were
outside the United States.147 Most of this demand was concentrated in Europe and Asia, particularly
in Japan. Excepting the United States, Japan was the single largest national market for personal
computers.148 But Dell realized that competing there "was more of a longer-term dream. The
investment we'd need to enter a market that was then dominated by entrenched Japanese companies
was more than we could handle."149

    British and other European markets were more open and expanding rapidly, and total PC sales in
Europe were expected to exceed $7.5 billion by 1990.150 A number of companies were selling PCs in
European markets in the late 1980s at prices competitive with Dell's. But Michael Dell perceived most
of these products to be neither as well made nor as reliable as those his company made. Moreover,
few of the players in European markets effectively serviced their machines, and none provided free
after-sales support. Dell was confident that his company's products, services, and, eventually, brand
would appeal to European consumers.

    Dell had become interested in the British market in 1984 when he and his family had traveled to
London during his spring break from university. "I observed the same high markup/lousy service
phenomenon in the United Kingdom as I had in the United States," he recalled.151 These
shortcomings, as Dell recognized, presented strategic opportunities, and three years later, he and his
colleagues decided to exploit them.

    Among other advantages to starting in Britain and Ireland were the absence of a language barrier
and the presence of large numbers of knowledgeable business users who might become interested in
the Dell brand. The United Kingdom, moreover, looked to be an excellent beachhead for future
European expansion.

same broad strategies as its parent company. Telemarketing sales reps backed by a national

small sales force in the field targeted large institutional buyers in Britain and Ireland.152

    Dell's overseas sales grew rapidly and the subsidiary was profitable within seven months. By

Sustaining Growth

    By early 1988, Dell's annual sales had climbed to $159 million, a 129% increase over the previous
fiscal year.157 To keep pace with this kind of expansion, the company needed additional
manufacturing, warehouse, and office facilities as well as more people. Senior management expected
employment, which stood at 650 in 1988, to increase by at least 60% over the coming year.158

    Collectively, these requirements occasioned a need for a considerable infusion of capital. To fund
new investments and satisfy working capital needs, senior management hoped to raise $38 million in
1988.159 In May of that year, 23-year-old Michael Dell announced that he and his colleagues would
take the company public. Reactions among financial analysts and business reporters were mixed.
Most were impressed with Dell's meteoric rise, but many doubted that the company could continue
to grow rapidly and profitably. "Dell has just about tapped out the mail-order business," asserted the
editor of Technologic PC Letter.160 Other writers predicted that the company's recent initiative to offer
Dell computers through value-added resellers would force it to change its entire operating strategy.161
Moreover, despite the involvement of Walker and other experienced executives, some questioned
Dell's ability to share control, citing the recent departures of several top managers.162

    Dell nevertheless raised in excess of $34 million when it went public in June 1988. The company
sold about a quarter of its outstanding stock, some 4 million shares, at an average price of $8.50 per
share. In its first six months on the NASDAQ, the company's stock price fluctuated between 7 3/4
and 12 5/8.163 Dell employees were eligible to receive incentive stock options, but Dell himself owned
more than half the company's equity.164

    Dell Computer's expansion accelerated in the late 1980s and early 1990s. Sales in fiscal 1991
exceeded $546 million, and net income totaled $27 million.165 During the preceding four years, sales
and profits had each grown at compound annual rates of more than 65%. Measured by market share,
Dell Computer was now the sixth largest player in the American PC industry, up from number 22
just two years earlier.166 But it was not a leader in world markets. In 1991, the largest shares of the
global market were held by IBM, Apple, NEC, Compaq, and Fujitsu, which together accounted for
52% of worldwide sales.167

    But Dell's market presence and brand recognition continued to develop as its strategy of targeting
large institutional consumers bore fruit. Nearly half of Dell's sales in fiscal year 1991 were to major
corporate, government, and educational customers. Small and medium-sized business accounted for
40% of sales, individual consumers for the remaining 12%.168 Moreover, more than two-thirds of
Dell's customers were repeat purchasers.169 Dell earned the top ranking for customer satisfaction
among business users in J. D. Powers and Associates' first annual survey of personal computer
consumers in early 1991. Other organizations, such as PCWeek, also awarded the company high
marks for pleasing customers.170

Learning from Mistakes

    Dell Computer had enjoyed tremendous growth during its first seven years. But in the intensely
competitive PC market, characterized by rapid-fire technological advances, past achievements hardly
guaranteed future success. In the late 1980s and early 1990s, graphics and network applications as
well as microprocessing capacity continued to expand quickly. PC manufacturers strove to assimilate
all this innovation. Leading companies such as IBM, Compaq, Apple, Tandy, and Zenith relied on
their research and experience to assess the operating efficiencies, commercial viability, user appeal,
and profitability of a never-ending stream of new product possibilities, many of which displaced or
rendered obsolete earlier offerings.171

    PC makers competed not only on how well they brokered technological innovation coming from
all sides, but also on product quality, brand equity, and, of course, price, which fell precipitously
during this period, driven down by technological advances and a nationwide recession. As sales
dampened, a price war broke out. Between 1987 and 1992, the average selling price of a personal
computer declined from $2,760 to $2,309, a 32% decrease (adjusted for price changes).172 Falling prices
squeezed industry profitability, motivated most players to reduce operating costs, and forced some
companies, such as Wang, out of the PC business altogether. One industry consultant described
participation in the PC industry as akin to operating "inside a tornado," a metaphor that captured the
speed and ruthlessness of rivalry in the business.173 (Exhibit 4 traces leading manufacturers' PC sales.)
Dell and other low-cost manufacturers such as Packard Bell and AST more than survived the price
war. For most of the period, Dell's sales and net income growth outpaced industry averages.174

    Dell's generally strong financial performance and increasing market share in desktop PCs were
hard won. Senior managers made two significant missteps as they strove to expand the firm's
product lines, customer base, and market presence as swiftly as possible. Both mistakes posed
potentially serious threats to the company's reputation and relationship with consumers and had
lasting consequences for the brand, organization, and Michael Dell himself.

Managing Inventory

    Between 1984 and 1988, Dell's sales rose at a compound annual rate of more than 250%.175 To keep
pace with this dizzying growth, company managers invested in large quantities of semiconductors,
PC disk drives, memory chips, and other components. When memory chips were projected to be in
short supply by late 1989, the company bought as many as possible.

    Dell and his colleagues discovered that they had purchased many more memory chips than
needed--at high prices. "And then prices plunged," Dell recalled. "To make matters worse, we also
got caught `crossing the street' technologically, as memory chip capacity went from 256K to 1
megabyte almost overnight."176 The company found itself holding millions of dollars of memory chips
that were rapidly becoming obsolete.177 "Inventory," Dell continued, "is the worst thing to own in an
industry in which the value of the materials or information declines quickly. . . . As one of my
colleagues in this industry likes to say, inventory has the shelf life of lettuce."178

    It took Dell Computer nearly a year to sell off its inventory of memory chips at prices much lower
than it had paid for them. The resulting losses prompted the company to postpone the launch of new
international market initiatives and contributed to a drop in its net income, which fell to $5 million in
fiscal 1990 from $14 million a year earlier.179 To preserve overall profit margins, Dell executives raised
product prices, which, in turn, dampened consumer demand. Dell's troubles led some journalists and
financial analysts to express doubts about the viability of its direct model.180 "For the first time in the

20
Michael Dell: Winning on the Demand Side of the Information Revolution  801-363

company's history," Dell recalled "we didn't deliver. To our stunned disbelief, we had quickly
become known as the company with the inventory problem."181

    It was the company's biggest mistake to date, and its lessons were not lost on Dell and his
colleagues. Senior managers recommitted their efforts and resources to one of the firm's original
operating principles--to minimize inventory. They devised new methods to monitor the speed with
which inputs flowed through the factory. As the company grew in the early and mid-1990s and its
influence with vendors increased, Dell was able to negotiate agreements that called for components
to be delivered on the day they were to be used. The company also improved its ability to predict
future demand. By the late-1990s, Dell had achieved lower inventories--an average of about 11
days--than most rival firms. Leaner inventories not only freed up financial resources for other uses,
but also helped the company respond quickly to changes on both the demand and supply side of
product markets. "If I've got 11 days of inventory," Dell explained, "and my competitor has 80, and
Intel comes out with a new 450-megahertz chip, that means I am going to get to market 69 days
sooner."182

Recommitting to Direct Sales

    In 1990, Dell executives stumbled again. They decided to begin selling PCs in traditional retail
outlets, a controversial decision for a business born of its founder's skepticism of third-party dealers
and their role in the personal computer market. Some managers, echoing Dell's own earlier thoughts,
doubted that mass merchandisers could deliver the service and support consumers had come to
associate with the Dell brand. Others worried that retailers' markups would force the company to
either raise prices or watch its profit margins shrink.

    Senior managers weighed these potential problems against the possibility of reaching new
consumer segments such as small office and household users and thereby expanding overall demand
for Dell products. In particular, they hoped to capitalize on the recent growth in PC sales through
discount retailers and superstores such as Sam's Club and PriceClub. Sales at such stores had climbed
from $600 million in 1987 to $1.6 billion in 1990.183 Reluctant to ignore these opportunities for growth,
Dell executives signed an agreement with CompUSA to sell PCs and peripheral equipment. Over the
next two years Dell products were also made available in Staples stores and PriceClub warehouse
outlets. Brisk sales through these retailers helped fuel record increases in Dell's gross revenues, which
climbed from $546 million in fiscal 1991 to more than $2 billion two years later.184

    Yet, as Michael Dell and his senior team came to realize, selling in the traditional retail channel
was not very profitable for the company. Dealers' markups, returns of unsold products, and the
administrative costs of dealing with additional actors sandwiched between the manufacturer and its
customers reduced Dell's income. Equally important, the PC maker had no direct line of
communication with buyers who purchased computers through retailers, and this prevented Dell
from gathering market intelligence about consumers' preferences and user feedback about product
performance. Company executives became increasingly concerned that they were diluting the firm's
brand equity by relying on sales people and technicians whom it did not hire, train, or manage.

    Dell stopped distributing its products through mass merchandisers and superstores in late 1993.
Both internally and externally the company was criticized for strategic vacillation. Some financial
analysts and journalists expressed skepticism about the company's future prospects.185 It could not
have been easy for Michael Dell to reverse course in 1993. At age 28, his track record in
understanding consumer wants and needs and how to meet them had been a stream of virtually
uninterrupted successes. Yet on this important issue, he and his top executives had made another
serious mistake.

    But Michael Dell was a fast learner. During the mid-1990s, with renewed appreciation for the
direct sales model, he and his colleagues recommitted the company to building and maintaining
strong, unfiltered connections with consumers. Senior managers also began investigating ways to
broaden its customer base without resorting to third parties.

    One of these avenues was the Internet. Dell had been attracted to the possibilities of reaching
consumers over the Internet in the early 1990s, envisioning such communication as a powerful
extension of the company's direct model. In 1995, he charged a small team of managers to explore
how Dell might use the Internet to connect with customers, deliver information, and sell products.186
Impressed with the growth of Internet access--especially among business users--in the United
States, its potential synergies with Dell's existing customer relationships, and the projected increase
in electronic commerce, team members began in late 1995 to plan an online technical interface and
retail store.187

    The following year Dell launched its online website, www.dell.com. Industry analysts and
journalists applauded the initiative, emphasizing the advantages of the Internet over third-part
distribution networks. "Dell has been positioned for something like this since its beginning,"
observed one reporter. "They can migrate what they do in the real world to the Web easily without
upsetting any of these existing sales channels."188 In its first four years, Dell's online initiative proved
highly successful, helping the company better understand user priorities and further customize
service and product support offerings. WWW.dell.com quickly became a dynamic marketplace; by
the early twenty-first century the company's web sales averaged more than $40 million a day.189

Conclusion

    By the beginning of the twenty-first century, Dell Computer's yearly sales exceeded $25 billion.
Measured by annual revenues, Dell was the second largest personal computer-systems company in
the world. Its PC sales exceeded those of Apple and IBM, two of the industry's early leaders.190 Many
industry observers credited Dell's success to a range of supply-side initiatives: including rigorous
quality control, lean inventories, close relationships with suppliers, and efficient cash-flow
management. Michael Dell had long recognized that producing quality goods at reasonable prices
was essential to competing effectively in the Information Revolution.

    But he also realized that such success depended on more than well-organized manufacturing.
Sustained business achievement also rested on a number of important demand-side capabilities.
Since 1984, he had worked to understand what consumers wanted and needed in IT products. He
used this understanding to build strong connections with consumers, a powerful brand and business
model, and the organizational capabilities to support these critical resources.
