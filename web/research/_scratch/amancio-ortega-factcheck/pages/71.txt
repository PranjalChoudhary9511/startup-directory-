 This is Amancio Ortega, The Man Who Created Zara Ricardo’s Notepad Subscribe Sign in This is Amancio Ortega, The Man Who Created Zara by Covadonga O'Shea Ricardo Vice Santos Jan 12, 2025 Share I heard about this book in David Senra’s Founders Podcast, and thought it would be interesting to read a Spanish that hasn’t been translated to English. While I’m fluent in conversational Spanish, reading and writing is a different challenge altogether. This was not a well written book. It often felt like a puff piece lacking depth. There was also barely any chronological order, and the chapters made no sense whatsoever. That said, there were a few good insights on how Zara operates, which are worth sharing. As is often the case, Zara’s success can be summarized in one word: clarity. Translated to English via ChatGPT, enjoy. Highlights Chapter I: HOW I MET AMANCIO ORTEGA "From pricing to the entire process, everything is done in-house without intermediaries or agents. [...] We prefer to make a small profit on each garment but sell many." (p.22) As a clear example, I recount something I sadly experienced around the tragic 9/11 attacks in New York. [...] Designers and producers of a colorful collection saw their business grind to a halt the following season. When such a deep shock occurs, bright tones amplify the pain by contrast. Shortly after, around Christmas, I spent a few days in New York and confirmed that in the preceding two months, only Zara had managed to sell. Without disregarding the world's sadness and suffering, Zara's rapid production system filled stores with dark, somber tones appropriate to the tragic circumstances in the West, particularly near the epicenter of such horror, which shook not only the towers’ foundations but much of humanity itself. (p.24) Paul Poiret said in 1890: "Fashion is an industry whose reason for being is novelty." (p.23) Making distributed novelty, delivered at lightning speed through the just-in-time formula, a hallmark of the business and a new model. (p.25) "I spend my days moving from one part of the factory to another to check how everything is going. If I'm not in the warehouse, I'm in design. I'm interested in the entire process, but I especially enjoy seeing what our artists produce. It's my favorite part of this complex." (p.29) "What are you doing? How are you going to greet the new millennium?" He replied: "What am I going to do? Work. If I want everything to run smoothly, I have to stay on the front line, no matter what happens in life." (p.31) "I want to be recognized on the street only by my family, friends, and colleagues. I want to live peacefully, be one of the crowd, go anywhere, have coffee on the terrace in Plaza de María Pita, or stroll with my wife along the Promenade without people knowing who I am." (p.35) Chapter II: FIRST STEPS IN A STORY "I heard something I’ve never forgotten despite the time that has passed: ‘Señora Josefa, I’m very sorry, but I can’t give you any more credit.’ That crushed me. I was barely twelve years old." Amancio explained that he was very sensitive yet proud. After rebelling against those words, he made an irrevocable decision: "This will never happen to my mother again. From that day forward, I resolved to work, earn money, and help at home. I left school, put down the books, and got a job as a clerk in a shirt shop." (p.41) "I’ve never raised my voice because I don’t like to make others suffer. But I was very proud. Later, when working at La Maja—a more upscale clothing store than my previous job—I went for a walk in La Coruña with the daughter of one of the store's wealthy clients. Apparently, the girl liked the boy working at the store—that is, me. I also found her amusing. One day, her mother came to the shop and asked the owner, ‘Where’s your son, Amancín?’ When the owner said I wasn’t his son, the mother’s expression changed. She clearly didn’t like her daughter associating with me anymore, simply because I was a mere clerk and not the owner's son. That deeply marked me. Reflecting on that time, I realize the wealthy back then were different—exclusive, closed off, and very possessive of their modest fortunes. I suffered, but it made me empathetic, determined to prevent others from experiencing similar pain." (p.44) "I didn’t have time to study because I worked 24 hours a day. I saw more clearly what I wanted to do and didn’t stop until I could achieve it, with the help of many others." (p.45) Chapter III: ZARA - A NEW FASHION CULTURE FOR THE 21ST CENTURY "A visitor to our headquarters once said that what I started was a groundbreaking and avant-garde textile distribution company." This idea perfectly encapsulated why the flagship company in the sector was named Inditex. (p.55) The new role of women in society and globalization bring profound changes in thinking and living. Fashion must adapt to this reality, not the other way around. In contrast to the materialism of the 1980s, the early 2000s saw the rise of comfortable, minimalist, and practical styles. (p.58) Critics who claim Zara brings nothing new to fashion miss the point. What Zara has truly innovated is a new way of understanding fashion and a different business model. (p.59) Jesús Vega, former HR Director of Inditex, shared that Ortega advised him: "Love people." This simple yet profound advice embodies Ortega’s philosophy, alongside his physical closeness to his teams. (p.67) "We don’t design for 80 different countries but rather a single type of clothing for 80 countries." (p.67) "Always aim for the best because the store is the heart of the company—the protagonist of this business." (p.69) Amancio Ortega created a business model that is now studied in universities and academic institutions he himself never attended. (p.72) Inditex has outpaced traditional development models that require six to nine months for a collection, responding instead to a market where brands no longer dictate to consumers but adapt to their preferences. (p.72) There are no mature sectors where everything has already been discovered, only companies or executives with closed minds resisting innovation. (p.73) Shoppers know they will always find new items, but they also understand that what they tried on seven days ago will likely no longer be available. This encourages a sense of urgency—customers buy immediately if they like something because it might not be there next time. For example, in Spain, Inditex customers visit stores an average of 17 times a year, compared to 3.5 visits for other fashion chains. (p.74) Chapter IV: ORTEGA ALWAYS PURSUED EXCELLENCE "I manufacture what I believe customers will demand." (p.81) Ortega has always appreciated simple ideas and preferred to clarify problems and decisions directly and efficiently. He is not a man of lengthy technical speeches. (p.83) When Ortega sold products to El Corte Inglés, buyers requested items that, upon closer observation, did not align with customer preferences. This led him to consider opening his own stores. (p.86) "You need to stay balanced and clear about your goals to avoid deviating from your business's core. This doesn’t mean you shouldn’t strive for daily improvement, but it’s essential not to lose sight of your company’s DNA. One of Amancio’s primary objectives was ensuring that everyone could dress well. Why venture into unrelated businesses?" (p.91) "A shop owner chooses what they like. This distinguishes them from a store clerk, whose sole aim is to sell products they did not select themselves." (p.100) "At first, I had no say in what was purchased or stocked in the store. My only option was to observe what customers liked. This was my education: learning to identify changes in tastes, preferences, and the styles women, men, and young people preferred. [...] This seemingly simple approach was revolutionary: realizing that customers have expectations they don’t want frustrated, committing to meet those expectations, and proving you can deliver." (p.100) Chapter V: INDITEX’S INTERNATIONAL EXPANSION AND STOCK MARKET DEBUT Ortega had a clear vision—his genius lay in integrating manufacturing, retail, logistics, and design, bridging two worlds traditionally seen as incompatible. A critical factor in Inditex’s success is Ortega’s unwavering belief that more can be sold at lower prices. Customers delight in finding silk and cashmere garments for €30, which would cost €100 in other stores. (p.109) Chapter VIII: HOW AMANCIO ORTEGA VIEWS HIS COMPANY AND LIFE "I increasingly value other people’s time. Making someone wait is disrespectful—it shows a lack of appreciation for the importance of their time, which is equally valuable to them as yours is to you." (p.158) Chapter IX: THE GREAT "WHY" OF HIS LIFE "I used to voice opinions on everything, but not anymore. When I delegate, I fully trust the person. It was hard for me to let go of the commercial side, but I had to. It’s essential to grant people independence and responsibility while ensuring they know they’re never alone." (p.185) When asked about Ortega’s aesthetic sense, a colleague explained: "He’s a practical man who applies logic. It’s not about special inspiration. If he sees women are comfortable in a suit and black sweater, he’ll make a suit and black sweater. [...] As he says himself, ‘The secret is to use logic to decide and lead. Life doesn’t need to be overly complicated.’" (p.194) Chapter X: "WE ALL HAVE GOOD AND BAD TRAITS" "The truth is, I sleep very well," he said. "To function properly, I need at least eight hours of sleep." (p.197) "Most people aren’t willing to spend a lot on clothing. Even wealthy individuals often spend the least on branded clothes. When customers get used to good value for money—whether in our stores or similar ones—they feel more satisfied with themselves. These are people who value their money and prefer to spend it on other things." (p.201) "Brand names should ideally be one word—short, memorable, and easy to pronounce in any language." (p.202) "Education is the greatest right everyone has." (p.203) "My advice to anyone wanting to do something groundbreaking is to observe the streets—they’re the ultimate runway." (p.206) "The mother is the cornerstone of a family. No matter how old you are, when she’s gone, something collapses." (p.207) Chapter XII: INDITEX - A BUSINESS MODEL Amancio Ortega’s obsession was delivering what the customer wanted quickly and at an attractive price. The traditional textile process, in contrast, involved long lead times: collections were designed over a year in advance, manufactured within three months, and then sent to distributors, who delivered them to stores one or two times per season. This system posed significant risks, including inventory overstock, poor-performing collections, and uncompetitive prices due to high markups at each step of the chain. (p.223) Inditex revolutionized this approach. Stores communicate preferences to factories, which respond in the shortest possible time. Inventory is kept low, with enough stock for a month, or two weeks during peak seasons. Approximately three out of ten customer visits result in a purchase. Inditex has created a sense of urgency—customers know that what’s available today might not be tomorrow and that new items might arrive later. (p.231) Epilogue Simplicity is the hallmark of geniuses.
