TITLE
Code for a Machine He'd Never Seen
From a school teletype in Seattle to Microsoft, a $118 billion fortune, and a promise to give almost all of it away by 2045.
HOOK
Bill Gates has put an end date on his own fortune. On ==8 May 2025== he wrote that he will give away virtually all his wealth through the Gates Foundation, which expects to spend **more than $200 billion** and then close its doors for good on ==31 December 2045==. His reason fits in one line: "People will say a lot of things about me when I die," but __he is determined that "he died rich" will not be one of them__.

0 Born: 28 October 1955, Seattle, Washington, USA [4,1]
1 Grew up: Seattle, the second of three children. Father William H. Gates Sr. was a lawyer and civic leader; mother Mary Gates was a schoolteacher, University of Washington regent and chair of United Way International [3,4]

CONTACT
Bill Gates has no public personal email or phone, so every real route runs through his organisations, and none of them hands out money to individuals. If you work at a non-profit, university or startup in global health, farming, sanitation or education, the honest way in is the Gates Foundation's published Requests for Proposals; the foundation says it mostly invites proposals itself and will not review pitches sent through its general contact form. Journalists should use the foundation's media line or email, or the press contacts of Microsoft and TerraPower; shareholders should use Microsoft Investor Relations. To hear what he is thinking, follow Gates Notes and his verified LinkedIn, X and Instagram accounts, where he posts about books, AI, health and climate. Never pay a 'grant fee' or share bank details with anyone promising Gates money: the foundation says it never asks for these, and you can check a suspicious message by forwarding it to phishing@gatesfoundation.org.

=== CH0 origins | Ages 0–12 | The Boy Who Was Thinking
[0.0 p] Bill Gates was born in Seattle on ==28 October 1955==. His full name was William H. Gates III, and the family called him "Trey", a card player's word for a three. He was the second of three children and the only son. [27,26]
[0.1 p] The Gates family was not new to Seattle. Bill's great-grandfather, also called William Henry Gates, moved there in the 1880s. On his mother's side, great-grandparents came from Nebraska to South Bend, Washington, in 1892. Trey was born into a family that had put down deep roots in the Pacific Northwest. [27]
[0.2 p] His parents met as students at the University of Washington. His father, William H. Gates Sr., was studying law. His mother, Mary Maxwell, earned an education degree in 1950. In the early 1950s they lived in Bremerton, where he worked as an assistant city attorney and she taught school. [28]
[0.3 note] Two parents who worked hard at their jobs and for their city. 
[0.4 h] The banker's daughter 
[0.5 p] Mary Maxwell was a banker's daughter from a wealthy banking family. At Roosevelt High School she was valedictorian and a star forward on the girls' basketball team. She was socially brilliant, the kind of person who put strangers at ease. [26,33,28]
[0.6 p] As her children grew, Mary built a huge civic career. She became the first woman to lead King County's United Way and the first woman to chair the national United Way's executive committee. She was the first woman director of First Interstate Bank of Washington, and in 1975 the governor appointed her to the University of Washington's Board of Regents, where she served for more than 18 years. [28,26]
[0.7 p] Bill's father became a successful Seattle lawyer and later a partner in a law firm. Berger describes him as tall, civil and quietly respected. Years later he still lived in the Lake Washington house where Trey grew up. [33,31,26]
[0.8.0 facts] 1954: Older sister Kristianne (Kristi) born [28]
[0.8.1 facts] 1955: William H. Gates III born in Seattle [27]
[0.8.2 facts] 1964: Younger sister Elizabeth (Libby) born [28]
[0.8.3 facts] 18+ yrs: Mary Gates's service as a UW regent [28]
[0.9 p] The family lived in north Seattle, in the View Ridge and Laurelhurst neighbourhoods, which were mostly white and well-off. Gates does not pretend otherwise. In his 2025 memoir, Source Code, he wrote openly about luck: being born white, male and American in a society that favoured exactly those things. [33,31]
[0.10 quote] "It's impossible to overstate the unearned privilege I enjoyed" -- Bill Gates, Source Code (2025), quoted by Cascade PBS [33]
[0.11 p] So this is not a rags-to-riches story. The Seattle journalist Knute Berger put it bluntly: Gates grew up with every advantage and was no bootstrap success. __Knowing your head start is not weakness; it is the first step to using it well.__ [33]
[0.12 h] Gami and the card table 
[0.13 p] A big figure in Trey's early life was his grandmother, Mary's mother, whom he called "Gami". She fed him Ritz crackers with peanut butter. Even more important, she and Mary loved card games, and they taught him how to play to win. [31,26]
[0.14 p] Sunday nights in the Gates house meant a big family dinner. Afterwards came bridge, the word game Password and trivia contests. Nobody played just for fun. [26]
[0.15 quote] "Winning mattered." -- Bill Gates Sr., on the family games, to TIME (1997) [26]
[0.16 p] Summers were even more organised. Every year the family rented cabins at a place called Cheerio on Hood Canal, about two hours from Seattle. There were nightly campfires, family skits, a Saturday tennis tournament and a Sunday "Olympics". Trey liked solo sports best, such as water skiing. [26]
[0.17 note] He learned competition at the dinner table, long before business. 
[0.18 p] Mary ran the household with rules and rituals. Every Christmas the whole family got, and wore, matching pyjamas. When Trey was 7, she used her IBM Selectric typewriter to make a travel log for a family trip to Disneyland, for him and his older sister to fill in, with headings such as "land forms" and "population distribution". [33,31]
[0.19 example] At 8, Trey helped raise money for the Cub Scouts by selling ==179 pounds of nuts==. His memoir uses the number as an early sign of how hard he competed. [31]
[0.20 h] Rocking, and the fair of the future 
[0.21 p] His father remembered something odd from Trey's babyhood: he rocked back and forth in his cradle. He never really stopped. As an adult, Gates still rocked when he was thinking hard, and Microsoft staff picked up the habit. [26]
[0.22 p] In 1962, when Trey was six, Seattle hosted a World's Fair. The Gates family went to every pavilion. At the General Electric pavilion, Trey probably saw a vision of the future: electronic libraries and simulated home computers. Historians of his life see it as an early turning point. [27]
[0.23 quote] "It was a huge event, a neat deal. We went to every pavilion" -- Bill Gates, recalling the 1962 World's Fair (quoted by HistoryLink) [27]
[0.24 photo] {"url":"https://upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/0/0d/Space_Needle_at_World%27s_Fair%2C_1962.jpg","page":"https://commons.wikimedia.org/wiki/File:Space_Needle_at_World%27s_Fair,_1962.jpg","caption":"The Space Needle at Seattle's 1962 World's Fair. Six-year-old Trey's family went to every pavilion.","author":"Seattle Municipal Archives","license":"Public domain","year":1962}
[0.25 p] At home he loved board games, especially Risk and Monopoly. He liked sports where he competed alone: roller skating, tennis, skiing. He was a keen Boy Scout and ended up only a few badges short of Eagle Scout. [27]
[0.26 h] "Have you ever tried thinking?" 
[0.27 p] Around age 9, something changed. Trey found he was very good at maths, and that gave him a belief that the world was rational and could be worked out by reasoning. It also made him, in his own words, a "bratty wiseass" who talked back to adults. [31]
[0.28 quote] "I became an adult overnight — an argumentative, intellectually forceful and sometimes not very nice adult." -- Bill Gates, Source Code (2025), quoted in The New York Times; he adds he was about 9 [31]
[0.29 p] By sixth grade he and his mother were in open conflict. He had a bedroom in the basement and often ignored her calls to dinner. One evening she called him over the house intercom and asked what he was doing. [26]
[0.30 p] "Yes, Mom, I'm thinking," he answered fiercely. Then came the line his family would never forget: "Have you ever tried thinking?" He was in sixth grade. [26]
[0.31 note] Every parent of a gifted, difficult kid will wince here. 
[0.32 p] His parents did something unusual for the 1960s. They took him to a psychologist in Seattle's University District, hoping to bring peace to the family and to understand their son. The counsellor gave Trey books by Freud to read between sessions, and the boy loved them. [33,26]
[0.33 quote] "He gave me books to read after each session, Freud stuff, and I really got into psychology theory." -- Bill Gates, to TIME (1997) [26]
[0.34 p] After about a year of sessions and tests, the counsellor gave Mary his verdict: "You're going to lose." His advice, in effect, was to let Trey be himself. Bill Sr. said his strong-willed wife eventually accepted that competing with her son was pointless. [26,33]
[0.35 quote] "You had better just adjust to it because there's no use trying to beat him." -- The family's counsellor, to Mary Gates, as reported by TIME (1997) [26]
[0.36 note] Read this twice. His parents chose to understand him, not crush him. 
[0.37 p] Today Gates describes himself as neurodivergent. When The Wall Street Journal printed an excerpt from his memoir, its headline asked if he was on the autism spectrum, and answered: "Probably." In the 1960s, almost nobody raised children with that idea in mind, which helps explain the clashes at home. [32,33]
[0.38 p] The next big decision was school. His father later said Trey was small and shy and needed protecting, and his parents thought a smaller class would suit a boy they saw as immature. Mary told Berger they did not want him bullied in a public school locker room, so they chose Lakeside, an elite private school across town. [26,27,33]

[0.40 lesson] Turn your fights into fuel: Trey's arguing could have wrecked his family. Instead, a counsellor and two patient parents pointed it at books, puzzles and goals. If you are the stubborn one, find adults who will aim your energy instead of fighting it. If you lead people, do the same for your stubborn ones.

=== CH1 growing-up | Ages 12–17 | The Night Shift
[1.0 p] Lakeside sat on a 33-acre, New England-style campus in north Seattle. In 1968 it did something almost no school in the world had done. Its Mothers' Club used money from a rummage sale to buy a teletype terminal for the students. [39,26,27]
[1.1 p] The terminal lived in one small room, next to an electric typewriter. It was linked by phone line to a General Electric GE-635 mainframe somewhere else in the city. Students typed commands, and a big remote computer typed back. [27,30]
[1.2 photo] {"url":"https://upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/d/df/ASR-33_at_CHM.agr.jpg","page":"https://commons.wikimedia.org/wiki/File:ASR-33_at_CHM.agr.jpg","caption":"A Teletype ASR-33 at the Computer History Museum: a common terminal of the era for talking to faraway mainframes over a phone line.","author":"ArnoldReinhold","license":"CC BY-SA 3.0","year":2014}
[1.3 p] Computer time cost real money, and the Mothers' Club budget ran out fast. Parents helped pay the bills. Trey was about 13, and he was hooked. [30,33]
[1.4 p] A teacher named Bill Dougall ran the lab. He said Gates took just a week to learn more than he knew. Dougall also remembered the boy's favourite phrase, used on ideas he disliked: "That's the stupidest thing I've ever heard of!" [27]
[1.5 note] One week to overtake his teacher. Then he never stopped. 
[1.6 h] The older boy in the lab 
[1.7 p] In the autumn of 1968, Trey met a student nearly three years older named Paul Allen. Allen's father was associate director of the University of Washington libraries and his mother was a fourth-grade teacher. Trey quickly joined Allen's circle in the computer room. [29,27]
[1.8 photo] {"url":"https://upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/0/03/Paul_Allen_and_Bill_Gates_at_Lakeside_School_in_1970.jpg","page":"https://commons.wikimedia.org/wiki/File:Paul_Allen_and_Bill_Gates_at_Lakeside_School_in_1970.jpg","caption":"Paul Allen (left) and Bill Gates at Lakeside School in 1970. Gates was about 14 here.","author":"Bruce Burgess","license":"Public domain","year":1970}
[1.9 p] The two learned the BASIC programming language from a manual. In eighth grade Gates wrote a program that converted numbers between bases, then a tic-tac-toe game. He also built a computer version of Risk, inspired by Napoleon's battle plans. [26]
[1.10 quote] "Left to ourselves, Bill and I would program until we were starving" -- Paul Allen, in his memoir Idea Man (quoted by HistoryLink) [29]
[1.11 h] Out the basement door 
[1.12 p] School computer time was too short and too expensive. So the boys made a deal with a local company: they could use its computer for free if they hunted for bugs that made the machine crash. They were being paid, in effect, to break things. [26,27]
[1.13 p] Trey took this job seriously, maybe too seriously. His father later learned that he would slip out of the basement door after bedtime and spend most of the night at the computer. At home he was the difficult son; at the terminal he was a professional. [26]
[1.14 example] A neighbour of the Allens remembered seeing the lights on in Paul's room at 3 a.m. Allen, he said, "would be up there with that Gates kid, working on computer stuff." [29]
[1.15 p] Not everyone at Lakeside loved Trey. One classmate called him "an extremely annoying person", and some students enjoyed giving him a hard time. He was small, fast-talking and very sure he was right. [27]
[1.16 p] In ninth grade he invented his own kind of rebellion. He decided to get straight A's without ever taking a book home. He skipped maths class because he had already read ahead, and he still placed in the top ten nationally on a maths aptitude exam. [26]
[1.17 quote] "I came up with a new form of rebellion." -- Bill Gates, to TIME (1997) [26]
[1.18 note] Rebellion, Gates-style: win so hard nobody can argue. 
[1.19 h] Kent 
[1.20 p] His best friend in these years was Kent Evans, the son of a Unitarian minister. The two boys read Fortune magazine together, the way other kids read comics. Decades later, Gates said he could still remember Kent's phone number. [26]
[1.21 quote] "We read Fortune together; we were going to conquer the world." -- Bill Gates, on Kent Evans, to TIME (1997) [26]
[1.22 p] Gates, Allen, Kent and another friend, Ric, spent all their free time writing more and more ambitious programs. On hiking trips Gates was a follower. In the computer room, he wrote later, he was the leader. [32]
[1.23 quote] "Unlike in hiking, among that group of friends, I was the leader." -- Bill Gates, Source Code (2025), WSJ excerpt [32]
[1.24 h] The pencil résumé 
[1.25 p] The boys called themselves the Lakeside Programmers Group, and they wanted real work. In ==November 1970==, a time-sharing company in Portland, Oregon, offered them a contract to write a payroll program. The payment was free time on its mainframe. [26,29]
[1.26 example] Gates had just turned 16. His résumé for the payroll job was written in pencil on lined notebook paper. As Allen later wrote: "We got the job." [29]
[1.27 p] The project nearly split the friends. Allen tried to take the work over, then found he needed Gates's coding help. Gates agreed to come back, but only on one condition. [26]
[1.28 quote] "O.K., but I'm in charge, and I'll get used to being in charge, and it'll be hard to deal with me from now on unless I'm in charge." -- Bill Gates, recalling what he told Paul Allen, to TIME (1997) [26]
[1.29 note] Sixteen, and already fighting for control. Watch this pattern. 
[1.30 p] That sentence is a preview of the next 30 years. __Gates learned early that whoever writes the hardest code can set the terms.__ It made him powerful, and it also made him hard to work with. [26,29]
[1.31 h] Fifty miles in the snow 
[1.32 p] Computers were not his only world. From about 13, Gates joined a breakaway group of five Boy Scouts who took long, unsupervised hikes. Over roughly three years they covered hundreds of miles in the Olympic Mountains, the Glacier Peak Wilderness and along the Pacific coast, living on Spam, Hamburger Helper and instant breakfast. [32]
[1.33 quote] "We wanted more freedom and more risk than the trips the Scouts offered." -- Bill Gates, Source Code (2025), WSJ excerpt [32]
[1.34 p] He was not the star of these trips. He was the skinniest and usually the coldest, and he felt weaker than the others. But he loved being far from help, making real decisions about food, routes and risk. [32]
[1.35 p] In ==June 1971==, at the end of his sophomore year, the group tried a roughly 50-mile route through the Olympic Mountains, over the Low Divide and down to the Quinault River. Heavy snow made the going brutal. When they reached a snowed-in shelter, the leader called a vote on whether to go on, and Gates was on the losing side. [32,33]
[1.36 p] So he kept walking, and he kept coding in his head. He was writing a version of BASIC for a small PDP-8 computer that a teacher friend could use, and it had only six kilobytes of memory, so every instruction had to count. Step by step through the snow, he worked out the part of the program that evaluates formulas. [32]
[1.37 quote] "I'd picture the code and then try to trace how the computer would follow my commands." -- Bill Gates, Source Code (2025), WSJ excerpt [32]
[1.38 note] Stuck on a problem? Walk. He debugged code on a mountain. 
[1.39 h] Pages, girls and traffic 
[1.40 p] Gates's parents knew powerful people, and doors opened. In spring 1971 a family friend, Washington governor Daniel Evans, arranged for him to serve as a page in the state legislature in Olympia. In summer 1972 Congressman Brock Adams arranged a paid job as a congressional page in Washington, D.C. [27]
[1.41 p] Back at Lakeside, the school hired him to write a program that scheduled classes, and paid him ==$4,200==. He built in a secret feature. He arranged his own timetable so that his history class had only one other boy and, as he put it, "all the good girls in the school". [27,26]
[1.42 p] He and his friends also started a business that analysed and graphed traffic data for local governments. One history of Microsoft says the traffic program earned Gates and Allen $20,000 in one summer. For two teenagers in the early 1970s, that was serious money. [26,30]
[1.43.0 facts] 16: Gates's age at the first paid contract (payroll, Nov 1970) [29]
[1.43.1 facts] $4,200: Paid by Lakeside for the class-scheduling program [27]
[1.43.2 facts] $20,000: Reported earnings from the traffic-data program in one summer [30]
[1.43.3 facts] 6 KB: Memory he had to squeeze his PDP-8 BASIC into [32]
[1.44 p] His father watched him change. As a teenager Trey grew more confident and funnier, a good storyteller who could imitate people's voices, and he made peace with his mother. He even acted, playing a lead role in Peter Shaffer's comedy Black Comedy in the school chapel. [26]
[1.45 p] Some things stayed hard. Girls, in particular, were a different kind of problem from code. His father remembered this scene clearly. [26]
[1.46 quote] "I remember him fretting for two weeks before asking a girl to the prom, then getting turned down." -- Bill Gates Sr., to TIME (1997) [26]
[1.47 h] The friend who did not come back 
[1.48 p] Then Kent Evans died in a mountain-climbing fall. Gates was asked to speak at the funeral, but he could not do it. For about two weeks afterwards, he could barely function at all. [26]
[1.49 quote] "I had never thought of people dying." -- Bill Gates, on Kent Evans's death, to TIME (1997) [26]
[1.50 p] Grief pushed him closer to Paul Allen. The two learned an artificial-intelligence programming language together and took on odd programming jobs. They became the partnership that would build Microsoft. [26]
[1.51 quote] "We were true partners. We'd talk for hours every day." -- Bill Gates, on Paul Allen, to TIME (1997) [26]
[1.52 note] Grief can redirect a life. It redirected his. 
[1.53 h] A real job at 17 
[1.54 p] In his senior year, the defence contractor TRW came calling. It was behind schedule on software for the Bonneville Power Administration, which ran the electric grid of the Pacific Northwest, and it wanted Gates's help debugging it. Gates phoned Allen, who was by then at Washington State University. [27,29,30]
[1.55 p] Both were offered jobs at ==$165 a week==. Gates took leave from Lakeside, Allen took leave from college, and the two rented an apartment together in Vancouver, Washington. Gates was 17 and working on software for a real power grid. [27,29]
[1.56 p] He went back to Seattle to finish high school, already accepted to Harvard. The plan, on paper, was a normal elite education. In reality he had already spent five years programming, much of it for pay. [27]

[1.58 lesson] Get paid to learn: Gates never waited for a degree to start working. He traded bug-hunting for computer time, pencilled a résumé at 16 and took a grown-up contract at 17. Find someone with a problem you can almost solve, and offer to solve it for whatever they can pay, even if that is only access.

=== CH2 first-steps | Ages 17–19 | Dorm Room, Phone Call, Desert
[2.0 p] Gates arrived at Harvard in the autumn of 1973, just short of his 18th birthday. Allen had warned him that Harvard would be harder than Lakeside and that some students would be better at maths. Gates's reply was two words: "No way." [27]
[2.1 p] Allen was right. Gates met people who were as good at maths as he was, or better, and that pushed him to look harder at other interests. Being the smartest person in the room had been his identity, and now it was not guaranteed. [27]
[2.2 note] Being humbled at 18 is cheap. Being humbled at 40 costs more. 
[2.3 p] He lived in Currier House, at the Radcliffe end of campus. His room became a late-night hangout, partly because everyone knew he did not care about getting up in the morning. [5]
[2.4 quote] "There were always lots of people in my dorm room late at night discussing things" -- Bill Gates, Harvard Commencement speech (2007) [5]
[2.5 p] His study method was unusual. He skipped lectures for courses he was enrolled in, sat in on lectures for courses he was not taking, and crammed before exams. He later admitted he had "this terrible habit of not ever attending classes". [26,35]
[2.6 quote] "I used to sit in on lots of classes I hadn't even signed up for. That's how I came to be the leader of the anti-social group." -- Bill Gates, Harvard Commencement speech (2007) [5]
[2.7 note] Copy the curiosity, not the skipping. 
[2.8 h] Poker until six 
[2.9 p] In his sophomore year, a student named Steve Ballmer lived down the hall. Ballmer, who would one day succeed Gates as Microsoft's chief executive, watched his new friend's nights closely. [26,27]
[2.10 quote] "He'd play poker until 6 in the morning, then I'd run into him at breakfast and discuss applied mathematics." -- Steve Ballmer, to TIME (1997) [26]
[2.11 p] Ballmer pulled Gates into a social life. He got him into an eating club, where Gates gave a drunken initiation speech about an artificial-intelligence machine. They played Pong in hamburger joints and went to Studio 54 on trips to New York. Ballmer called him "the smartest guy I've ever met". [26]
[2.12 h] The pancake problem 
[2.13 p] In a combinatorics class, professor Harry Lewis described a puzzle that was easy to state but unsolved. You have a messy stack of pancakes of different sizes, and the only move allowed is to slide a spatula under some of them and flip that top part over. What is the smallest number of flips that will always sort the stack? [36]
[2.14 quote] "this smart sophomore comes into my office and explains that he's got a five-thirds N algorithm" -- Harvard professor Harry Lewis, to NPR (2008), on Gates returning a day or two later [36]
[2.15 example] The simple method takes about two flips per pancake. Gates's method needed about five-thirds of a flip per pancake, roughly 1.67. With a young Harvard assistant professor, Christos Papadimitriou, he published it in the journal Discrete Mathematics in ==1979==. [36,37]
[2.16 p] It was real mathematics, not a party trick. Lewis called the approach "quite clever", and it needed a complicated case analysis. When researchers finally beat it, decades later, their strategy was only about 1 percent faster, and they needed powerful computers to find it. [36]
[2.17 note] Billionaire later; published mathematician first. 
[2.18 h] The Aiken lab 
[2.19 p] Harvard's computer centre was the Aiken Computer Laboratory. Professor Thomas Cheatham of the Aiken lab saw both sides of the young programmer. He called Gates "a hell of a good programmer", but said he also "put people down when it was not necessary". [27]
[2.20 p] Allen did not want to lose his partner to Boston. In 1974, at Gates's urging, Allen left Washington State to take a programming job at Honeywell in Massachusetts, driving his Chrysler across the country. Gates had also been offered a Honeywell job; he stayed in college but worked there in the summer of 1974. [27,29,26]
[2.21 h] The magazine cover 
[2.22 p] In December 1974, Allen was walking through Harvard Square when he saw the new issue of Popular Electronics. Its cover story was the Altair 8800, a computer kit built around Intel's 8080 chip. Allen bought it and ran to Gates's dorm. [27,34]
[2.23 quote] "I saw a cover of Popular Electronics with this thing that looked like what I had been imagining" -- Paul Allen, in PBS's Triumph of the Nerds (1996) [34]
[2.24 p] The Altair kit cost ==$397== and came with just 256 bytes of memory. You programmed it by flipping switches on the front panel. It had no language that normal people could use, and Gates saw the gap at once. [30,34,29]
[2.25 quote] "Paul showed that to me and then okay, here was a company that would be needing software." -- Bill Gates, in PBS's Triumph of the Nerds (1996) [34]

[2.27 h] Selling what did not exist 
[2.28 p] In January 1975, Gates picked up the phone in Currier House and called the company in Albuquerque, New Mexico, that made the Altair, called MITS. He offered to sell them software for it. There was one problem. [5,27]
[2.29 quote] "Instead they said: "We're not quite ready, come see us in a month," which was a good thing, because we hadn't written the software yet." -- Bill Gates, Harvard Commencement speech (2007) [5]
[2.30 note] He sold it before it existed. Then he built it, fast. 
[2.31 p] For the next eight weeks or so, Gates and Allen wrote a version of BASIC for the Altair, using Harvard's computers. A Harvard student named Monte Davidoff joined them to help. Their code had never run on a real Altair, so they were writing software for a machine they could only read about. [30,27,29,34]
[2.32 p] Gates was proud of that code all his life. In 1996 he told a documentary crew that it had been a labour of love. [34]
[2.33 quote] "that was er, er, a work of love, you know, we just kept tuning and tuning that thing." -- Bill Gates, in PBS's Triumph of the Nerds (1996) [34]
[2.34 p] Then came the question of who would fly to Albuquerque to show it off. Allen went. In his telling, he looked like an adult, while Gates could pass for a high-school sophomore. [27]
[2.35 p] Allen flew with the program on paper tape and loaded it into an Altair at MITS. It had never run on a real machine before. He was terrified it would fail in front of the customer. [34]
[2.36 quote] "I was just I was so nervous....this is just....it's not going to work and it worked." -- Paul Allen, on the first demo at MITS, in PBS's Triumph of the Nerds (1996) [34]
[2.37.0 facts] $397: Price of an Altair 8800 kit [30]
[2.37.1 facts] 256 bytes: Memory in the basic Altair [30]
[2.37.2 facts] ~8 weeks: Time to write Altair BASIC at Harvard [30,27]
[2.37.3 facts] $3,000: Paid by MITS on signing, plus royalties [27]
[2.38 p] MITS signed a contract. Gates and Allen got ==$3,000== on signing, plus a royalty on each copy shipped with the hardware. MITS also hired Allen as its director of software, and in April 1975 he moved to Albuquerque. [27,29]
[2.39 h] Sixty–forty 
[2.40 p] In the summer of 1975, Gates joined Allen in Albuquerque. They formed a partnership they first called "Micro-Soft". Allen had assumed they would split it 50-50. [27,29,26]
[2.41 p] Gates disagreed. Allen was drawing a salary from MITS, he argued, while Gates had written BASIC at Harvard for nothing. He insisted on 60-40 in his favour, and later the split moved to 64-36. [27,29]
[2.42 quote] "It's not right for you to get half" -- Bill Gates to Paul Allen, as recalled in Allen's Idea Man (quoted by HistoryLink) [29]
[2.43 p] Gates won that argument, but it had a cost. The two fought often in the years that followed, and the friendship that began over a teletype was later badly strained. __Winning a negotiation with your co-founder is not the same as winning.__ [26,29]
[2.44 note] Very important for first-time founders! Talk about equity early and fairly. 
[2.45 p] Life in Albuquerque was scrappy. The young partners lived across the street from MITS at the Sundowner Motel, and a MITS colleague remembered days when Gates slept on the floor of the software lab. Gates never used his family inheritance to fund the company. [34,30]
[2.46 p] Harvard faded away. The fall term of 1975 was, by one account, his last real term, and he left against his parents' wishes; another account says he formally left in January 1977, technically on extended leave. Gates once summed up the start simply. [27,30]
[2.47 quote] "We started when I was 19." -- Bill Gates, in PBS's Triumph of the Nerds (1996) [34]
[2.48 p] The bedroom-basement rebel had become a company founder. He had the tools: years of paid programming, a partner he trusted and a product that worked. What came next, from a motel in New Mexico to the biggest software company on Earth, is the story of the next chapters. [26,27,34]

=== CH3 every-company | Every venture, win or lose | Everything He Ever Built
[3.0 p] Most people can name one Bill Gates company. The full list runs from a teenage payroll job to a nuclear plant going up in Wyoming. Along the way it takes in a satellite internet that was never built, a cartoon house guided by a dog, and a wristwatch that picked up news over FM radio. [26,40,41,42,43]
[3.1 p] Watch one pattern as you read. After the 1980s, almost every bet below was paid for by Microsoft, either as a company project or from Gates's own Microsoft wealth. __A flop cost him pride and money, never survival. Most founders have no such cushion, so copy his thinking, not his risk level.__ [30,44,45]
[3.2 note] Read the failures as carefully as the wins. 
[3.3 h] Before Microsoft: two teenage businesses 
[3.4 venture] Lakeside Programmers Group (1970–1972) shut
 IDEA A small band of Lakeside School students sold programming work to grown-up companies. Their big job was a payroll program for a time-sharing firm in Portland, Oregon.
 DECISION They wanted computer time, which cost real money. When the Portland firm said free time had to be earned, they agreed to write payroll software. Gates negotiated the deal himself. Paul Allen and another older boy first pushed him out, then needed him back.
 STARTED No office, just a school computer room. The group was four friends: Gates, Allen, Kent Evans and Ric Weiland. Their first customer was Information Sciences in Portland.
 FUNDING None. The payment ran the other way, in free computer time rather than cash.
 WHAT Gates returned on his own terms: he would be in charge from then on. In 1972 Lakeside paid him $4,200 for a class-scheduling program. The group faded as its members moved on to college and paid jobs.
 LESSON Your first customer can pay in something other than cash. Access, tools and experience can be worth more at 15. [26,4,27]
[3.5 venture] Traf-O-Data (about 1972 to the late 1970s) failed
 IDEA Towns counted traffic with a rubber hose across the road. A box punched holes in paper tape every 15 minutes, and clerks read the tape by hand. Gates and Allen wanted to turn that tape into clean charts, cheaply.
 DECISION Gates had seen the dull work on a summer job. The pair thought software could do it faster, and later that a small computer could do it automatically. Allen admitted they did no market research first.
 STARTED Gates hired younger Lakeside students at 50 cents a tape to copy the data onto cards. A third partner, Paul Gilbert, then built a machine to read the tapes. The first customers included the Washington state roads department, small counties near Seattle and a district in British Columbia.
 FUNDING Their own programming earnings. They scraped together $360 to buy a special-order Intel 8008 chip.
 WHAT They charged about $2 a day for data. Reports say the work netted close to $20,000. Then Washington and other states began offering the same service free, and local officials did not want to buy machines from students. The business died while Microsoft was taking off.
 LESSON Check who else could give your product away free, and whether your buyer is allowed to buy from you. [46,30,26]
[3.6 p] Traf-O-Data lost, yet it taught them how a microprocessor really worked. Allen later wrote that without it, and without all those hours on University of Washington computers, Microsoft might never have happened. __The failure paid off years later, in skill rather than cash.__ [46]
[3.7 h] The one that worked 
[3.8 venture] Microsoft (1975–present) public
 IDEA Cheap chips would put a computer on every desk, and every one of those computers would need software. Gates and Allen chose to write that software and sell it to the people making the machines.
 DECISION Allen kept bringing ideas that involved building hardware. Gates kept shooting them down, saying that they were not hardware experts and that software was what they knew. The Altair kit in 1975 gave them a customer for exactly that skill.
 STARTED First home: Albuquerque, New Mexico, to be near MITS, which made the Altair. In 1976 they rented eighth-floor offices in Two Park Central Tower. In 1975 the team was Gates, Allen and Ric Weiland. MITS came first, then GE, NCR and Citibank.
 FUNDING Almost nothing. One account puts the start at a $1,500 shoestring. MITS paid $3,000 on signing plus royalties. Gates never used his family inheritance on the company, and it did not sell any stock to an outsider until 1981.
 WHAT Sales went from $16,005 in 1975 to over $1 million in 1978 and $16 million in 1981. The IBM PC deal in 1980 made its operating system the world standard. It went public in 1986. In the three months to September 2026 it made $92.3 billion in revenue.
 LESSON A business that lives on its own cash keeps its founder in charge. Take outside money only when it buys something you cannot build. [47,48,49,50,27,30,51,52,53]
[3.9 example] For example: at $92.3 billion a quarter, Microsoft now takes in roughly $11,600 every second. Its entire first year of sales, $16,005, would be earned in under two seconds. That sum is our own arithmetic from the two published figures. [53,51]
[3.10 h] Inside Microsoft: the products that flopped, or nearly did 
[3.11 p] Gates ran Microsoft as chief executive until January 2000, then stayed on as chairman and chief software architect until mid-2008. So the company's flops from those years are his flops too. He has never pretended otherwise, as you will see with the phone. [51,54,55,56]
[3.12 venture] Multiplan and the first Word (1982 to about 1990) pivoted
 IDEA Microsoft's first attempts at office software for ordinary business users: a spreadsheet and a word processor.
 DECISION Gates decided a platform company should also own the programs people used all day. Controlling the operating system alone did not make people buy Microsoft's applications.
 STARTED Built inside Microsoft. A January 1982 deal with Apple also had Microsoft writing programs, Multiplan among them, for the coming Macintosh.
 FUNDING Microsoft's own profits.
 WHAT They got crushed. Gates has said Lotus 1-2-3 owned spreadsheets, while WordPerfect and others owned word processing and Word sat at about number four. His answer was to stop fighting on the old ground. Microsoft bet on graphics, built Excel for the Mac in 1985, and arrived on Windows first when rivals hesitated. By the time Windows 95 shipped, Office was beating them all.
 LESSON If you are losing on today's platform, do not try harder there. Be first on the next one. [57,58,59,60,61]
[3.13 venture] Windows 1.0 and 2.0 (1983–1990) pivoted
 IDEA A graphical layer on top of MS-DOS, so that people could click on pictures with a mouse instead of typing commands.
 DECISION Rivals such as VisiCorp were close, and Gates wanted to claim the ground early. So Microsoft announced Windows on 10 November 1983, long before it was ready. Two years later it also signed with IBM for a different system, keeping two bets alive.
 STARTED A small team inside Microsoft. The $99 first version came with a notepad, a calendar, a paint program and the board game Reversi.
 FUNDING Microsoft's DOS profits.
 WHAT It shipped two years late, on 20 November 1985. The industry called it vaporware. Microsoft held a roast for itself in Las Vegas, where a magazine editor handed Gates a Golden Vaporware award. The early versions were not successful. Version 3.0 in May 1990 finally took off and sold over 10 million copies.
 LESSON A big idea can take three versions. Fund it from a business that is already paying the bills, so you can survive versions one and two. [62,63,57,64,30]
[3.14 venture] OS/2 (with IBM) (1985–1992) shut
 IDEA The official successor to DOS, built jointly by the biggest computer company on Earth and its small software partner.
 DECISION Microsoft could not afford to walk away from IBM. Ballmer has recalled the fear that if they let go of IBM, it might trample them. But Gates also kept Windows running quietly as insurance, and staff on each team complained about the other.
 STARTED A joint development agreement signed in 1985. The first version came out in spring 1987.
 FUNDING The sources we read do not give the money terms of the 1985 deal. In the 1992 settlement, IBM was reported to owe Microsoft about $23 per copy, and Microsoft paid a lump sum for IBM patents.
 WHAT OS/2 needed costly hardware and never caught on, while Windows 3.0 became a hit. In September 1990 the two firms agreed that IBM would take over most OS/2 work. In June 1992 they settled the last of it, and from then on they were open rivals.
 LESSON Keep a Plan B alive when your biggest partner holds your future. Gates's small insurance project became the company. [64,65,66,57,67]
[3.15 venture] Encarta (1993–2009) shut
 IDEA A multimedia encyclopedia on a single cheap CD-ROM, sold for home computers.
 DECISION Microsoft was already moving into CD-ROM reference works. CIO reports it tried to buy Britannica and was refused, so it built its own.
 STARTED A Microsoft team working on CD-ROM reference products, starting from earlier disks such as Bookshelf.
 FUNDING Microsoft's own budget.
 WHAT It worked brutally well at first. Britannica's book sales fell by half in three years, and the old company was sold for $130 million after putting itself up for sale in 1995. Then the same thing happened to Encarta. Free online sources, led by Wikipedia, overtook it. Microsoft closed the software in June 2009 and the websites that October.
 LESSON If you killed a giant with a cheaper model, assume someone will do it to you with a free one. [68,69,70,30]
[3.16 venture] Microsoft Bob (1995–1996) shut
 IDEA Computers scared people in the 1990s. Bob turned the screen into a cartoon house. You clicked a pen to write a letter or a calendar on the wall to check the date, and a dog named Rover guided you.
 DECISION Researchers had watched beginners struggle, even in tears, with ordinary menus. The team thought a friendly character could solve that. Melinda French, later Melinda Gates, led on the marketing side. Colleague Tandy Trower warned Gates about the design, and Gates left the call to the team.
 STARTED A Microsoft team, working with academic advisers.
 FUNDING Microsoft's own budget.
 WHAT It needed 8 megabytes of memory, which few beginners had. Critics called it too cute and childish. Within a year it was gone, and TIME later listed it among the worst inventions. Its code lived on in Office helpers, including the much-mocked paperclip Clippy.
 LESSON Beginners do not stay beginners. Do not design only for somebody's first day. [71,41,72]
[3.17 venture] The Microsoft Network (first version of MSN) (1995–late 1990s) pivoted
 IDEA A private online service in the style of America Online, built into Windows 95, with Microsoft acting as gatekeeper and collecting the tolls.
 DECISION In 1994 the question inside Microsoft was how anyone could make money from an internet where everything seemed free. A private network looked like a safer answer.
 STARTED Built in-house and timed to launch alongside Windows 95.
 FUNDING Microsoft's own budget, part of a much bigger internet spend of an extra $1.5 billion set aside for web work.
 WHAT The open web arrived too fast. Gates's May 1995 memo swung the whole company towards the internet before the network had even launched. Web projects followed, such as the magazine Slate and the news site MSNBC in 1996, and Microsoft's attention moved to the open web.
 LESSON Do not build a walled garden while everyone is leaving for the open field. [73,74,75]
[3.18 venture] Xbox (2000–present) running
 IDEA A Microsoft game console to stop Sony's PlayStation from taking over the living room.
 DECISION The first plan was a cheap PC pretending to be a console, which is what the team pitched to Gates. Then the costs came out. Rick Thompson warned Gates that the project could lose $900 million over eight years, and $3.3 billion in a price war. Gates pushed back and asked for better numbers.
 STARTED About 60 people by December 1999, inside Microsoft. On 14 February 2000 an all-day meeting, later nicknamed the St. Valentine's Day Massacre, gave the final go-ahead.
 FUNDING Microsoft's own cash. Thompson figured over $1 billion would go out before any revenue came in.
 WHAT It launched on 15 November 2001. Each console sold at a loss, and early games chief Ed Fries estimates that the project lost about $1 billion a year in his time. He still calls it a bet worth making. Xbox is still a Microsoft business today, though its content and services revenue slipped 2% in the September 2026 quarter.
 LESSON A rich company's patience is a weapon. Know exactly how much you can lose before you start. [76,77,78,79,53]
[3.19 venture] Tablet PC (2001–2010s) failed
 IDEA A full Windows computer the size of a legal notepad, used with a pen.
 DECISION Gates loved the idea and personally used one every day. At the COMDEX trade show in November 2001 he predicted that within five years it would be the most popular kind of PC sold in America.
 STARTED Prototypes came from partners such as Acer, Compaq, Fujitsu and Toshiba. Microsoft supplied a special Windows edition. Machines went on sale in late 2002.
 FUNDING Microsoft's own budget, plus partners paying for their hardware.
 WHAT The prediction was badly wrong. The interface treated the tablet as an afterthought, the hardware was weak, and Windows tablets stayed a tiny niche for years. NBC later judged that Microsoft had simply turned up too early.
 LESSON Being early and being right are different things. A good idea on the wrong interface still loses. [80,81]
[3.20 venture] SPOT smart watches (2003–2012) shut
 IDEA Everyday objects that could receive tiny bits of news, weather and messages. The star product was a smart watch, long before smart watches became common.
 DECISION Microsoft built its own wireless network over FM radio signals, rather than using phone networks. Gates himself showed off the watches on stage at the Consumer Electronics Show in January 2003.
 STARTED Watch partners were Fossil, Suunto and Citizen. Custom chips came from National Semiconductor.
 FUNDING Microsoft's own budget, with buyers paying a subscription of about $59 a year.
 WHAT Phones did the same job far better over cellular and Wi-Fi. Watch sales stopped in 2008, and the radio service went dark on 1 January 2012.
 LESSON A correct vision of the future is not a product. Ask what else could deliver the same benefit. [82,83,42,81]
[3.21 p] The same years brought Zune, a music player launched in 2006 to take on the iPod. It had interesting features and heavy investment behind it, but it never took off, and Microsoft ended it in 2011. Reviewers praised the later versions, yet the audience stayed with Apple. [81,69]
[3.22 venture] Windows Mobile, then Windows Phone (2000–2010s) failed
 IDEA Put Windows on pocket devices and do to phones what Microsoft had done to PCs.
 DECISION Microsoft came early, with Windows Mobile in 2000. It did not spot how touch would change everything, and it did not stop a small rival. Google bought Android in 2005 for about $50 million.
 STARTED Built in-house and licensed to phone makers. In a December 2008 emergency meeting, after months of argument, Microsoft agreed to scrap Windows Mobile and rebuild it as Windows Phone.
 FUNDING Microsoft's own budget, and later about $1 billion paid to Nokia to adopt Windows Phone.
 WHAT Android became the standard non-Apple phone system. Steve Ballmer was chief executive for much of this, and many assumed it was his miss. Yet in 2019 Gates called it his own greatest mistake, valued the lost prize at $400 billion, and named the antitrust lawsuit as part of the backdrop.
 LESSON In a winner-takes-all market, second place is worth close to nothing. When your own rule says so, act on it. [84,56,85,81]
[3.23 h] The side bets 
[3.24 p] From about 1989, Gates also began putting personal money into ideas that had nothing to do with Microsoft's main business. Some were a rich man's experiments, and a few were simply good investing. He often stayed out of the day-to-day running, as the photo company shows. [86,40,87]
[3.25 venture] Corbis (first called Interactive Home Systems) (1989–2016) sold
 IDEA People would one day hang digital art on flat screens at home. So someone should buy the digital rights to the world's great images.
 DECISION Gates built his own house to display art this way, and he bet others would follow. When home screens did not take off, Corbis changed course. It began licensing images to publishers, news outlets and advertisers, and fought Getty Images for the top spot.
 STARTED A tiny Gates-owned start-up, set up in 1989. In October 1995 it bought the Bettmann Archive, one of the world's great photo collections.
 FUNDING All Gates. He was its sole shareholder, and he funded tens of millions of dollars of purchases.
 WHAT By 2007 it had over 1,000 staff and about $250 million in sales, but no profit. To save the fragile Bettmann negatives, it moved them 220 feet down a Pennsylvania limestone mine. In January 2016 it sold its image business to an affiliate of Visual China Group for an undisclosed sum, and its old rival Getty took over distribution outside China. The sale included the famous Tiananmen Square pictures, which raised concern. Its entertainment arm was kept and rebranded.
 LESSON Your own money can keep a weak business alive for 27 years. That is not the same as making it work. [86,88,45,89,90,91,92,93]
[3.26 venture] ICOS (biotech investment) (1990–2007) sold
 IDEA A drug company fighting inflammatory disease, set up by the biotech veteran George Rathmann, who had earlier built Amgen, and two other pioneers. It was not Gates's idea, but he was its star backer.
 DECISION Gates joined a dream-team group of investors and served on the board until 2005. Rathmann's rule was to raise lots of cash early, while he could.
 STARTED Offices in the Seattle area, later in Bothell. The first round in 1990 was reported at about $32–33 million, a record size for a biotech start-up.
 FUNDING Gates put in about one-tenth of the first round; the Seattle Times puts it at $5 million. In late 1994, with cash running out, he and Rathmann together bought half of a $23 million share sale. Gates's holding doubled to 15%.
 WHAT Its early drugs failed trials. Its fortune came from Cialis, an impotence drug it developed with Eli Lilly. In January 2007 Lilly bought ICOS for $34 a share, about $2.3 billion, and most of the 700 staff lost their jobs. Gates still held about 8% in late 2006.
 LESSON In long-horizon fields, cash is time. Raise when you can, not when you must. [94,87,95,96,97,98]
[3.27 venture] Teledesic (1994–2002) shut
 IDEA Internet from the sky: 840 low-orbit satellites giving fast broadband anywhere on Earth, including remote places with no phone lines.
 DECISION The project already existed when the mobile-phone pioneer Craig McCaw and Gates each put in $5 million in 1994. For Gates this was pocket money and a passive stake, but the headlines treated it as a mighty alliance.
 STARTED Offices at Carillon Point in Kirkland, then a Bellevue warehouse, with over 100 staff at its peak. Boeing came in as prime contractor in 1997.
 FUNDING McCaw and Gates each put in $5 million. Saudi Prince Alwaleed bin Talal added $200 million, Boeing up to $100 million. The planned total was $9 billion.
 WHAT The design shrank from 840 satellites to about 288, then to 30. Contractors changed from Boeing to Motorola to Alenia Spazio, and chief executives came and went. Iridium's and ICO's bankruptcies scared off money. In October 2002 it laid off all but 10 people and stopped work. A company spokesman said $500 million had been invested.
 LESSON A famous name raises money and attention, not satellites. Watch the build, not the headlines. [40,99,100,101,102]
[3.28 note] He put in 0.05% of his wealth. The press called it a giant bet. 
[3.29 venture] Cascade Investment (1995–present) running
 IDEA A private firm to move Gates's fortune out of a single stock, so that it would no longer rise and fall with Microsoft alone.
 DECISION His old money manager, a longtime friend, had served six months in prison for bank fraud years before. A 1993 Wall Street Journal story brought that to light, so Gates went looking for someone new. A recruiter found Michael Larson, a Putnam fund manager.
 STARTED Larson started in 1994. Cascade was registered in Washington state in 1995, with a deliberately dull name, and is based in Kirkland.
 FUNDING Gates's own wealth, then around $5 billion.
 WHAT Larson bought unfashionable things: railways, waste collection, tractors, hotels and farmland. He told Fortune he was no risk taker. It worked: the fortune he looked after kept growing. In May 2021 the New York Times reported ten former staff describing misconduct and a culture of fear. Larson and his spokesman denied some of the claims, though not all, and said complaints had been taken seriously. One outside inquiry found one complaint could not be substantiated.
 LESSON Great founders hire someone for what they do not know, then step back. Stepping back too far has its own price. [103,104,102,105]
[3.30 venture] Four Seasons Hotels (1997–present) running
 IDEA An ownership stake in one of the world's best-known luxury hotel brands.
 DECISION Cascade first bought shares in 1997, when the company was public. In 2006 it teamed up with Prince Alwaleed and founder Isadore Sharp to take the company private, drawn by a trusted brand and steady long-term income.
 STARTED This one was bought rather than built. Founder Isadore Sharp stayed on as chairman and kept a stake.
 FUNDING Cascade's money. The 2006 group offered about $3.7 billion for the company, including $750 million of borrowed money. In 2021 Cascade paid $2.21 billion in cash for a further 23.75%.
 WHAT Cascade rose from 47.5% to 71.25% after the 2021 deal, which valued the chain at $10 billion including debt. The chain managed 121 properties at the time.
 LESSON Patience in a boring, beloved brand can beat cleverness in a hot one. [106,107,102]
[3.31 venture] Farmland and Belmont, Arizona (2010s–present) running
 IDEA Own land: farmland in many US states, and 24,800 acres near Phoenix that could one day become a brand-new high-tech city.
 DECISION Larson wanted assets that do not move with technology stocks. Belmont's backers said the plan was a city built from the start around fast data networks, autonomous vehicles and logistics hubs.
 STARTED Bought through dozens of small companies with plain names, so the owner was hard to see. A largest block came from a Canadian pension fund in 2017.
 FUNDING Cascade put $80 million into Belmont, and over $690 million into two big farm purchases in 2017–18.
 WHAT In January 2021 The Land Report named Gates America's largest private farmland owner, at about 242,000 acres across 18 states. Belmont has moved slowly: as of 2021, no large-scale building had begun.
 LESSON Land is the opposite of software, and that is why a software fortune buys it. [108,109,110,111,103]
[3.32 h] Giving it away, and the bets after Microsoft 
[3.33 venture] The Gates Foundation (1994–present (closing on 31 December 2045)) running
 IDEA Spend a software fortune on problems markets ignore, above all diseases that kill children in poor countries.
 DECISION Gates was being flooded with charity requests. His father offered to sort through them. Then a newspaper article showed Gates that millions of children were dying of preventable illnesses, and he sent it to his dad with a short note. He later chose to spend it all down rather than keep it forever.
 STARTED A basement. Bill Gates Sr. ran the first foundation from his home office, near his son's old bedroom, and used to bring boxes of proposals over to his son's house.
 FUNDING A first stock gift of about $94 million in 1994. A $20 billion endowment by 2000. Warren Buffett pledged more than $30 billion in 2006, and has given about $43 billion so far.
 WHAT It spent $100 billion in its first 25 years, about half on global health. Gates has given it over $63.9 billion. Melinda French Gates left in 2024 with $12.5 billion for her own giving. It now plans to spend more than $200 billion and close for good in 2045.
 LESSON Treat a giving problem like a business problem: find where a small push changes a big outcome. [112,113,114,115,116,117,118,1,119,120]
[3.34 venture] Giving Pledge (2010–present) running
 IDEA A public promise by very rich families to give away more than half of their wealth.
 DECISION Warren Buffett and Bill and Melinda Gates spent the year before it launched holding dinners with US billionaires, asking them to think out loud about what their money was for. They chose a moral promise, not a contract.
 STARTED Launched in June 2010. The first 40 families and individuals were announced on 4 August 2010.
 FUNDING None needed. The pledge pools no money and backs no particular cause.
 WHAT It gives peer pressure a formal shape. Nobody can be forced to pay, so its strength rests on reputation alone.
 LESSON Sometimes the most powerful thing you can launch has no budget at all. [121,122]
[3.35 venture] TerraPower (2008–present) running
 IDEA A new kind of nuclear reactor that is simpler, cheaper and safer. The first design hoped to burn depleted uranium; the current Natrium design is cooled by liquid sodium and stores heat in molten salt.
 DECISION The work grew from brainstorming run by Nathan Myhrvold, Microsoft's former chief technology officer. Gates wanted reliable clean power for a warming, poorer world. In 2017 the company signed with China National Nuclear Corp to test its reactor. When new US rules blocked that around the end of 2018, it had to regroup and change designs.
 STARTED A private company in Bellevue, Washington, with Gates as chairman and Myhrvold as vice chairman.
 FUNDING Gates, who says he has put in over a billion dollars and is prepared to add billions more. Later backers include SK of South Korea ($250 million in 2022), and Nvidia's venture arm in a $650 million round in 2025. The US government covers half of the first plant's project costs, up to $2 billion.
 WHAT On 4 March 2026 US regulators approved construction of its first plant at Kemmerer, Wyoming, the first such permit for this kind of reactor. The site sits beside an ageing coal plant. Completion is aimed at 2030, and it still needs an operating licence.
 LESSON Hard problems need owners who can wait 20 years and survive a government killing their plan. [123,8,124,125,126,127,128,129,130,131,132,43,133]
[3.36 venture] bgC3, now Gates Ventures (2008–present) running
 IDEA A private office to coordinate Gates's business, science and charity work in one place, and to chase ideas that fit nowhere else.
 DECISION He left his full-time Microsoft job in June 2008 and wanted a third base, separate from Microsoft and the foundation. An insider said the C stood for catalyst, and the 3 for that third place.
 STARTED A Kirkland office fitted out with Microsoft gear. It was formed quietly in March 2008 as Carillon Holdings and took the new name days after he left Microsoft.
 FUNDING Gates himself.
 WHAT The office now runs under the name Gates Ventures, next to Cascade. An insider said in 2008 that it was not a commercial venture, and it has stayed a personal base rather than a profit-chasing company.
 LESSON After a founder leaves, structure decides what they do next. Build the small team first. [123,103]
[3.37 venture] Aquion Energy (battery investment) (investor to 2017) failed
 IDEA Cheap saltwater batteries for power grids, started by Carnegie Mellon professor Jay Whitacre.
 DECISION Grids that use wind and solar need storage, and Aquion promised to build batteries for less than $300 per kilowatt-hour. Gates joined Kleiner Perkins and Shell among its backers.
 STARTED Whitacre's start-up, which built its own factory and grew past 100 staff.
 FUNDING Nearly $200 million from investors including Gates. His own share has not been made public.
 WHAT The technology worked, but the factory ate cash faster than venture investors would refill it. Then lithium-ion batteries fell below Aquion's price target, and investors lost faith. It filed for bankruptcy in March 2017. Its assets sold for $9.16 million, a small fraction of what had gone in.
 LESSON Winning on technology does not matter if a rival's price falls past your target first. [134,135,136]
[3.38 venture] Breakthrough Energy (2015–present) running
 IDEA Patient money for clean-energy companies too risky and too slow for normal venture funds.
 DECISION Government labs were producing ideas, but few crossed what investors call the valley of death between lab and market. Gates decided to gather rich backers around the 2015 Paris climate talks. He also pressed governments to double research budgets at the same time.
 STARTED Launched on 29 November 2015 with about 28 investors from 10 countries.
 FUNDING In December 2016 the Breakthrough Energy Ventures fund opened with over $1 billion, with Gates as chair. Backers included Jeff Bezos, Jack Ma, Michael Bloomberg, Vinod Khosla and John Doerr. It planned to operate for 20 years.
 WHAT It backs start-ups at any stage, from seed to commercial scale. In March 2025 Bloomberg reported dozens of layoffs, including all staff in Europe and the US public policy team, as the group pulled back from advocacy.
 LESSON Match the money's patience to the problem's timeline. A 20-year problem needs 20-year money. [137,138,139,140,141]
[3.39 p] Count them up and the record looks like this: one giant win, a handful of smart side investments, and many losses. __Gates's real edge was never perfect judgement. It was that Microsoft's cash let him stay in the game after each wrong call.__ The next chapters show how that cash machine was built, and how close it came to breaking. [44,40,56]

=== CH4 the-big-bets | 1977–1986 | The $75,000 Operating System
[4.0 p] Here is the oddest fact about how Microsoft was funded: it hardly was. Before going public in 1986, the company sold a stake to just one venture investor. Gates later said that money was not even needed. __Microsoft grew on what customers paid it, and that one choice shaped everything that came later.__ [52,44]
[4.1 p] Gates also had a private rule, and he explained it in a 1998 interview. He wanted enough cash in the bank to pay a full year of salaries, even with no money coming in. His staff were older than he was and had children, and he worried about what would happen if a customer did not pay. He has said he kept to that rule almost the whole time. [142]
[4.2 note] A year of payroll in the bank. Very important for first-time founders! 
[4.3 h] The bad deal that taught him pricing 
[4.4 p] Early Microsoft sold licences for a flat fee, and some of those deals look painful now. It licensed BASIC for the Apple II for ==$21,000==, one time only. Apple went on to sell over a million of those machines, which works out to about two cents per copy. [47,49]
[4.5 p] Paul Allen pushed for charging per copy. Gates and Ballmer disagreed, saying low flat fees were how Microsoft had won so much of the market. Both had a point. Flat fees spread Microsoft's software everywhere in the early years, and the later switch to per-copy pricing brought in billions. [47,67]
[4.6 example] For example: take one early NCR licence for BASIC, at $175,000. Even after half went to MITS, Allen wrote, it could pay five or six programmers for a year. One big customer could keep this tiny company going. [47]
[4.7 h] The billionaire who said it was too expensive 
[4.8 p] In 1979, Ross Perot tried to buy Microsoft. Perot ran the computer services giant EDS and was already very rich. Microsoft was a 28-person partnership with about $2 million in sales, and Gates was 23. He got a rare haircut before meeting Perot in Dallas. [50]
[4.9 p] The two remember the price differently. Perot recalled Gates asking ==$40–60 million==. Gates recalled ==$6–15 million==. Either way, Perot thought it was far too high, and Gates was never keen. Gates's mother did want him to think harder about selling, since she still hoped he might go back to Harvard. He sent Perot a polite letter saying no. [50]
[4.10 p] Years later, when Microsoft was worth over $21 billion, Perot told the authors of a Gates biography that this was one of the biggest business mistakes of his life. He said he should have let the young man name his price. __A founder's refusal to sell cheaply can be the most valuable decision they ever make, and it usually looks foolish when they make it.__ [50]
[4.11 note] He was 23. The offer was a fortune. He sent a polite no. 
[4.12 h] Why IBM knocked on the door 
[4.13 p] In July 1980, an IBM executive named Jack Sams arrived at Microsoft's Bellevue office. IBM was secretly building a personal computer, under the code name Project Chess. When a young man came out to fetch him, Sams took him for the office boy. It was Gates, and Sams soon changed his mind about him entirely. [27,48]
[4.14 p] IBM needed programming languages, which was Microsoft's business, and also an operating system, which was not. An operating system is the basic software that makes a computer run. Ballmer says IBM may have been confused by a Microsoft product called the SoftCard, a plug-in that let Apple II machines run another company's system, CP/M. So Gates sent IBM to Digital Research, the company behind CP/M. [67,30]
[4.15 p] Those talks failed, and people still argue about why. Ballmer and one history say Digital Research would not sign IBM's secrecy agreement. Another account says the reasons remain unclear. Either way, IBM came back, and Microsoft did something bold. It promised to deliver an operating system it did not have. [67,143,27]
[4.16 p] Microsoft knew where to get one. Across town, a young programmer named Tim Paterson at Seattle Computer Products had written a system in about four months that was nicknamed QDOS, for Quick and Dirty Operating System. Microsoft licensed it for ==$25,000==, without telling the seller that IBM was the customer. Paterson had his suspicions. [144,145,143]
[4.17 p] On 27 July 1981, just two weeks before the IBM PC launched, Microsoft bought the system outright for another ==$50,000==. That made the total $75,000. It became MS-DOS. By 1991 it was bringing Microsoft about $200 million a year. [145,146,27]
[4.18 h] The clause that made the fortune 
[4.19 p] The contract was signed on 6 November 1980. Reports put IBM's total payment at about ==$430,000==, which was not life-changing even then. The money was not the point. __Microsoft kept the right to sell the operating system to anyone else.__ IBM accepted this partly because it wanted standard parts and because it was already deep in antitrust trouble. [147,67]
[4.20 p] Then IBM's machine turned out to be easy to copy, since it was built from standard parts. Rival makers rushed out clones, and they all needed the same software. By December 1982, 50 other manufacturers had licensed DOS. At the moment of the deal, Microsoft had about $7 million in sales and fewer than 40 staff, while IBM had some 325,000 workers. [30]
[4.21 p] Did family connections help? The New York Times reported in 1994 that Gates's mother, Mary, sat on the national United Way committee with IBM's chairman John Opel, and that she spoke with him in 1980. Her role was believed to have helped at a crucial time, though we should not overstate it. Ballmer's own version is plainer: he calls this deal their big piece of luck, and he says neither he nor Gates foresaw how large the PC market would become. [148,67]
[4.22 note] Read this twice. They kept the rights. IBM got the machine. 
[4.23 example] For example: the people who sold DOS did not stay happy. In 1986 Seattle Computer Products sued Microsoft for $60 million over its licence. With the jury already deliberating, Microsoft paid $925,000 to end the case and buy the licence back. It also paid over $1 million that year for Paterson's own company, to recover another royalty-free licence. [149,146]
[4.24 h] The only VC, and the money that never left the bank 
[4.25 p] In 1981 Microsoft stopped being a partnership and became a corporation, with Gates at 53%, Allen 31%, Ballmer 8% and Vern Raburn 4%. David Marquardt of Technology Venture Investors had been talking to them since late 1980. A venture capitalist, or VC, is an investor who buys part of a young company hoping it grows. [143]
[4.26 p] In September 1981 his firm paid ==$1 million for 5%==, which valued Microsoft at $20 million. Gates has said the money was never needed and simply sat in the bank. What he wanted was a seasoned adviser on the board, and he says Marquardt did a fantastic job. __Most founders raise money and then look for advice. Gates wanted the advice, and took the money as the price of getting it.__ [143,52]
[4.27.0 facts] $75,000: Total price Microsoft paid for the system that became MS-DOS (1980–81) [145,144]
[4.27.1 facts] $430,000: Reported IBM contract with Microsoft, 6 Nov 1980 [147]
[4.27.2 facts] $1 million for 5%: The only pre-IPO venture investment, Sept 1981 [143,52]
[4.27.3 facts] $0: Years of annual losses Gates recalls, as of his 2013 remarks [52]
[4.28 h] The IPO he did not want 
[4.29 p] An IPO, or initial public offering, is when a private company first sells its shares to anyone on the stock market. Most firms do it for cash. Microsoft had pre-tax profit margins as high as 34% and no long-term debt, so it did not need any. Gates also dreaded the distraction, and told Fortune that traders ringing the chief executive was like ball bearings asking the driver about the grease. [44]
[4.30 p] The reason was a legal one. Microsoft paid staff partly in shares and stock options, and by 1987 over 500 people would own stock. That would force the firm to register with US regulators anyway. So Gates decided to go public on his own timing rather than be pushed. In October 1985, a day after a roller-skating party for his 30th birthday, the board agreed. [44]
[4.31 p] The bankers came courting. CFO Frank Gaudette ranked them on a scale of one to five in 19 categories. At an awkward dinner at the Rainier Club in Seattle, Gates sat bored and tired until the talk turned to pricing. Then he folded his arms and began rocking, his well-known sign of interest. Later, in the parking lot, he joked that the Goldman Sachs team had not spilled their food and seemed nice, so they might as well go with them. [44]
[4.32 p] Then came the strangest part. The bankers suggested a price of $17 to $20 a share, and __Gates insisted on a lower one, $16 to $19__. He feared a $20 price would push Microsoft's value past half a billion dollars, which he thought uncomfortably high. A Goldman banker said few companies ever fight for a lower range. [44]
[4.33 p] Investor demand overruled him. The final price, agreed on 12 March 1986, was ==$21==. Gates haggled over the bankers' fee too, ordering his team to hold it down to $1.28 a share. It settled at $1.31 after a stand-off. Each penny there was worth about $31,000 to Microsoft. [44]
[4.34 p] Trading opened on 13 March at $25.75 and closed the first day at $27.75. The shares reached $35.50 soon after. The company and its shareholders raised $61 million. Gates kept 45% of the company, worth about $350 million on paper within months. [44]
[4.35 example] For example: Gates sold only about $1.6 million of his own shares in the IPO, and used part of it to pay off a $150,000 mortgage. A programmer on the same deal made nearly $200,000. He had also ruled that nobody should sell more than 10% of their holdings. Fortune reported all this from inside the process. [44]
[4.36.0 timeline] 1975: Gates and Allen sign with MITS and earn $16,005 in the first year. [27,150]
[4.36.1 timeline] 1978: Sales pass $1 million. The team has 13 people. [48]
[4.36.2 timeline] 1979: Moves from Albuquerque to Bellevue, Washington. Turns down Ross Perot. [48,50]
[4.36.3 timeline] Nov 1980: Signs the IBM contract, keeping the right to sell DOS to others. [147,48]
[4.36.4 timeline] Jun–Sep 1981: Becomes a corporation and sells 5% to Technology Venture Investors. [143]

[4.37 lesson] Keep the rights, not just the cheque: The IBM payment was small. The right to sell to everyone else was worth billions. In any contract, ask first what you get to keep, and only then ask how much you get paid.

=== CH5 near-death | 1977–2004 | The Years It Nearly Broke
[5.0 p] Microsoft never faced a long bankruptcy battle. Gates says it never had a losing year. Its danger came in other forms: a frozen product, a broken partnership, a lawsuit worth billions, a technology it nearly missed, and a government trying to split it in two. [52,151,30]
[5.1 h] 1977: the one time the money ran out 
[5.2 p] MITS was struggling, and its founder Ed Roberts agreed to sell it to a larger firm, Pertec, for about $6.5 million. Pertec believed it was buying the rights to Microsoft's BASIC. It stopped the sublicensing deals that were Microsoft's main income, and a judge barred new sales until an arbitrator ruled. An arbitrator is a private judge whose decision both sides agree to accept. [151,152,153]
[5.3 p] The company was starving. They struggled to pay salaries and rent, and they had to borrow from one of their own programmers, Bob Greenberg. Allen told Gates over dinner that they were nearly out of cash and should think about settling. __Gates refused, after talking to his father, a lawyer, who thought they could win.__ It was, Allen wrote later, the best advice Gates never took from him. [153,152,151]
[5.4 p] In November 1977 the ruling came, and it was a total win. The contract had said MITS must use its best efforts to sell licences, and blocking them broke that promise. The arbitrator called it corporate piracy. Microsoft got back full rights, and within weeks it had signed deals with companies that had been waiting. It ended 1977 with about $381,000 in sales, after almost nothing for most of the year. [153,152,154,151]
[5.5 note] One clause in a contract saved the whole company. Read yours. 
[5.6 h] 1982–83: losing the co-founder 
[5.7 p] Paul Allen's own account of this period is painful to read, and it is his side of the story. In 1980, he says, Gates offered Ballmer 8.75% of the company while Allen was away, when they had agreed on no more than 5%. Allen called it a major breach of faith. He was also carrying the sting of two earlier reworkings of their split, from 50-50 to 60-40 to 64-36. [47,155]
[5.8 p] In 1982 Allen was diagnosed with Hodgkin's lymphoma, and he has written about that publicly. One evening that December, while still weak from treatment, he says he overheard Gates and Ballmer talking about diluting his stake by issuing options. Dilution means creating new shares, so your slice of the company shrinks. He burst into the office and told them it showed their true character. [47,155]
[5.9 p] Ballmer came to his house that night to apologise, and Gates sent a six-page handwritten apology dated 31 December. Allen left anyway, on 18 February 1983. In their final talk, by his telling, Gates offered him $5 a share for his stock, and Allen said he would not discuss less than $10. Gates said no. __That refusal made Allen one of the richest people in the world.__ [47,30]
[5.10 example] For example: in 1999 Forbes ranked Gates first in the world at about $90 billion, Allen third at about $30 billion and Ballmer fourth at $19.5 billion. Allen's stock, the stake Gates had offered to buy at $5 a share, made that possible. Another early executive who left for a rival, Vern Raburn, had his shares bought back at $3 and lost out, Allen says, on billions. [30,47]
[5.11 p] Gates spoke warmly of Allen when he died in 2018, calling him a true partner and dear friend, and saying that personal computing would not have existed without him. Both things are true of them: a brutal negotiation, and a lifelong tie. [46]
[5.12 h] 1985–94: a $5.5 billion fight with Apple 
[5.13 p] Microsoft had been writing programs for the Macintosh under a 1982 deal. Then Windows arrived looking a lot like it, and Apple objected in the summer of 1985. Talks that October produced a licence, signed on 22 November, two days after Windows 1.0 shipped. In it, Microsoft agreed that the displays were derived from Apple's work, and Apple licensed them for present and future programs. [156,157,60]
[5.14 p] Here is the detail most people miss. Apple got something back: Microsoft promised to hold back an IBM-compatible Excel for a while and to improve Word for the Mac. Apple needed those programs to sell Macs to businesses. In exchange, it signed a document that would later undercut its own lawsuit. [157,156]
[5.15 p] In March 1988 Apple sued over Windows 2.03, and its damages claim grew to ==$5.5 billion==. Judges ruled that the 1985 licence covered most of what Apple complained about. By June 1993 the case had shrunk until there was almost nothing left to try, and in September 1994 the appeals court upheld the result. Asked about all this, Gates reportedly joked that both he and Jobs had a rich neighbour called Xerox, whose lab had pioneered the ideas. [158,156,157,30]
[5.16 h] 1990: betting against your biggest partner 
[5.17 p] By the late 1980s, Microsoft was building OS/2 with IBM and pushing Windows at the same time. Staff on each side resented the other. Gates has said he kept Windows alive as a kind of necessary strategy. In May 1990 Windows 3.0 came out and sold over 10 million copies, and the partnership collapsed into what the press called a bitter divorce. [57,64,66]
[5.18 p] __Walking away from the customer that made you is about the riskiest move a young company can make.__ Microsoft could do it because every PC maker was now its customer, not just IBM. Its revenue topped $1 billion in 1990, and the IBM contract's old clause had quietly become its protection. [74,30]
[5.19 p] It also needed to win on applications, where it was losing badly. Gates's internal message was that there was no spreadsheet market, only a Lotus 1-2-3 market, so Microsoft had to build something clearly better. Lotus's boss Mitch Kapor once told a panel that Gates was wrong about graphics but worked so hard he might succeed anyway. Gates says he took that as a compliment. [57]
[5.20 h] 1995: the tidal wave he nearly missed 
[5.21 p] The internet is the most famous near-miss in Microsoft's history. An internal memo in April 1994 had said Microsoft would invest, while admitting that this might be a mistake. Then AOL paid $30 million for a browser company in November 1994, which stunned Microsoft's managers. When Microsoft tried to license Netscape's browser, Netscape turned it down flat. [73]
[5.22 p] On ==26 May 1995==, Gates sent senior staff a memo called The Internet Tidal Wave. He set the internet at the highest priority for every part of the business, and admitted Netscape's browser already had about 70% of usage. One senior manager on Windows 95 later said it felt as if Gates was the last executive to come around. [75,73]
[5.23 p] Microsoft then did what rich latecomers do. It licensed browser code from a small firm, Spyglass, for $2 million, and put a small team on it. It gave Internet Explorer away free, and placed it on every Windows machine. Its share rose from almost nothing in 1995 to about 20% in 1996 and 40% in 1997, closing fast on Netscape. [73]
[5.24 example] For example: Windows 95 launched on 24 August 1995 with a marketing push of about $300 million. Reports said the Rolling Stones were paid $14 million for the song in the ads. It sold 1 million copies in four days. Stores opened at midnight for an operating system. [73,159]
[5.25 p] That free browser would nearly cost Microsoft everything. __The move that won the browser war also started the biggest legal fight of its life.__ [30]
[5.26 h] 1997: rescuing a rival 
[5.27 p] In August 1997, Steve Jobs stood on stage at Macworld in Boston while Gates appeared on a screen by satellite link. Microsoft agreed to invest ==$150 million== in Apple's non-voting stock and to keep making Office for the Mac. Apple agreed to make Internet Explorer its default browser, and the two signed a broad patent cross-licence. [59,160]
[5.28 p] It looked like generosity, and Apple really was struggling. Yet it served Microsoft too. The Computer History Museum notes it may have helped to deflect criticism in the coming antitrust case, and it put Explorer in front of every new Mac buyer. __A smart deal can help your rival and yourself at once.__ [161,59]
[5.29 h] 1998–2002: the government tries to split Microsoft 
[5.30 p] Antitrust law is the set of rules meant to stop a dominant company abusing its power. Regulators had looked at Microsoft since 1990, and it had already settled one case. On ==18 May 1998==, the US Justice Department and 19 states sued. They said Microsoft had used its monopoly on PC operating systems to choke off competition in browsers. [30,27,162]
[5.31 p] On 3 April 2000, Judge Thomas Penfield Jackson ruled against Microsoft in harsh language, and investors drove the share price to a 52-week low. In June he went further and ordered the company split in two. Gates had already handed the chief executive job to Ballmer that January, becoming chairman and chief software architect. [30,51,54]
[5.32 quote] "placed an oppressive thumb on the scale of competitive fortune" -- Judge Thomas Penfield Jackson, on Microsoft, in his ruling of 3 April 2000 [30]
[5.33 p] Then came a dramatic turn. On 28 June 2001, a federal appeals court upheld the finding that Microsoft had illegally kept its operating-system monopoly. But it threw out the breakup order, rejected the claim that Microsoft had tried to monopolise browsers, and took the judge off the case over his comments to the press. In November 2001 the government settled. [163,162,164]
[5.34 p] Under the settlement, Microsoft had to share some technical information with rivals, let PC makers install competing software, and not punish anyone for doing so. Nine states and the District of Columbia held out for more. The new judge approved the deal in November 2002, and warned the company that she would be watching closely. It was widely seen as a win for Microsoft, and Gates called it fair. [164,30]
[5.35 quote] "a prince never lacks legitimate reasons to break his promise" -- Judge Colleen Kollar-Kotelly, quoting Machiavelli as a warning to Microsoft, November 2002 [30]
[5.36 p] Europe took its own turn. On 24 March 2004, the European Commission found that Microsoft had abused its near monopoly and fined it ==€497 million==. It also ordered Microsoft to share interface information and to offer a version of Windows without its media player. [165]
[5.37.0 timeline] Nov 1977: Arbitrator frees Microsoft from MITS and Pertec. [154,151]

[5.38 h] The miss he still talks about 
[5.39 p] Gates has connected the legal fight to his biggest regret, though he stops short of blaming it entirely. Speaking to founders in 2019, he said platforms are winner-take-all markets, with room for exactly one non-Apple phone system. Google took that slot with Android, a prize he values at $400 billion. [56,85]
[5.40 quote] "the greatest mistake ever is whatever mismanagement I engaged in" -- Bill Gates, on Microsoft losing the phone market to Android, 2019 [56]
[5.41 note] A billionaire publicly blaming himself. Very rare. Very useful. 
[5.42 lesson] Being paranoid is a strategy: Microsoft kept a year of cash, kept a backup operating system alive and kept rewriting its plans when it looked wrong. It still missed mobile. Plan for your own blind spots, because the giant killer usually starts out looking like a toy.

=== CH6 the-fortune | 1986–2026 | Where the Money Went
[6.0 p] Bill Gates did not get rich by drawing a big salary. He got rich by owning a large slice of one company as it grew for decades. Then he did something unusual: he spent the next 25 years moving that slice out of his own name. [44,1,113]
[6.1 h] Step one: hold the stock 
[6.2 p] In March 1986, Gates owned 45% of Microsoft, a stake of about $350 million. By 1987, with the stock over $90, he was a billionaire at 31. Microsoft's own history says its stock rose more than a hundredfold between 1986 and 1996. [44,27,166]
[6.3 p] Then the 1990s boom took over. On 16 July 1999 his holdings brushed ==$100 billion== for the first time, in a single day of trading that added about $5 billion. A month earlier he had given away about that same amount, $5 billion, in the largest gift then made by a living person to a foundation. Microsoft had also made many of its own staff rich, an estimated 10,000 of them millionaires through stock options. [167,30]
[6.4 example] For example: one $21 IPO share has become 288 shares through nine stock splits. A split gives you more shares of the same company without changing what you own. At about $530 a share in late October 2026, that one $21 share is worth about $152,000, before counting dividends. [168,169]
[6.5 h] Step two: spread it around 
[6.6 p] In 1994 Gates hired Michael Larson, and the job was to diversify. Larson put the money into things with no link to software. Over time, Cascade took stakes in Canadian National Railway, Republic Services, Deere, Ecolab and others, along with bonds, hotels and land. Fortune once reported that he kept most of it in bonds. [104,102,103]
[6.7 p] __Notice how boring the list is. A software billionaire's money now sits in trains, rubbish trucks and tractors.__ The reasoning was simple: when Microsoft fell, as it did hard in 2000, these holdings would not necessarily fall with it. The New York Times reported that this strategy paid off when the dot-com bubble burst. [103,102]
[6.8 example] For example: by May 2021 Gates controlled, directly or through his foundation's trust, about 14% of Canada's largest railway. That stake was worth about C$13.6 billion, enough on its own to rank him among the richest people in Canada. As the divorce began, Cascade moved about 14 million CN shares to Melinda French Gates. [105]
[6.9 h] Step three: give it away 
[6.10 p] Gates says the 2000 merger of his foundations started with an endowment of $20 billion. He gave over $16 billion in a single stretch in 1999, and a $3.3 billion Microsoft dividend in 2004 went straight to the foundation. Forbes now puts his lifetime donations to it at more than ==$63.9 billion==. Gates's own website gives a lower figure, $59.5 billion. [113,117,170,1]
[6.11 p] In May 2026 a filing revealed that the foundation's trust had sold its last 7.7 million Microsoft shares, worth about $3.2 billion. That ended a holding of decades. The money is needed because the foundation plans to spend about $200 billion by 2045, and a charity winding down its endowment has to sell. [171,120]
[6.12 p] The trust is a separate pot from Gates's personal fortune, though he is its trustee and Cascade manages it. On 30 June 2026 it reported about $34.4 billion in US stocks, led by Berkshire Hathaway, Caterpillar, Canadian National and Waste Management. Much of that Berkshire stock came from Warren Buffett, whose gifts have reached about $43 billion. Gates sat on Berkshire's board from 2004 until 2020. [172,173,112,102,174]
[6.13 h] Where his own fortune sits now 
[6.14 p] As of the start of November 2026, Forbes puts Gates at ==$120.4 billion==, number 16 in the world. Bloomberg, using its own method, says $121 billion and number 17. Both are estimates, since much of the wealth sits in private holdings. Bloomberg itself cut its estimate by about $50 billion in July 2025, after reassessing how much he had given away. [1,175,170]
[6.15.0 facts] $33.8B: Microsoft shares, Bloomberg estimate, 31 Oct 2026 [175]
[6.15.1 facts] $26.8B: Republic Services, a waste company, Bloomberg, 31 Oct 2026 [175]
[6.15.2 facts] $13.4B: Deere, the tractor maker, Bloomberg, 31 Oct 2026 [175]
[6.15.3 facts] $8.84B: Ecolab, a disinfectant and hygiene firm, Bloomberg, 31 Oct 2026 [175,104]
[6.16 p] Bloomberg also lists Givaudan at $5.35 billion, Berkshire Hathaway at $3.55 billion, FEMSA at $3.52 billion and AutoNation at $1.27 billion. His waste-collection stake is worth about four-fifths of his holding in the company he founded. Forbes estimates that he now owns less than 1% of Microsoft. [175,1]
[6.17 note] A garbage company is his second-biggest listed holding. Yes, really. 
[6.18 example] For example: at the IPO he owned 45% of Microsoft's 24.7 million shares, about 11.1 million. Multiply by 288 for the splits and $530 for the price, and you get about $1.7 trillion. That is our rough arithmetic. It shows the size of what he chose to sell and give away, compared with a fortune near $120 billion today. [27,168,169,1]
[6.19 p] Private assets are harder to price. Cascade controls 71.25% of Four Seasons since the 2021 deal, and TerraPower is still privately held, with Gates as its largest investor. The Land Report put his US farmland near 242,000 acres in 2021. [107,129,127,108]
[6.20 h] Stepping away, and what came after 
[6.21 p] His exit from Microsoft took 20 years. He gave up the chief executive job in January 2000, his full-time role on 27 June 2008, the chair on 4 February 2014, and his board seat on 13 March 2020. He stayed on as a technology adviser to chief executive Satya Nadella. [51,54,55]
[6.22 p] Even in later years, his instincts were not always right, which is oddly comforting. Nadella has recalled that when Microsoft put $1 billion into OpenAI in 2019, Gates warned him he would burn the money. Fortune reported in 2026 that the investment had grown into a 27% stake, worth about $135 billion. __Even the man who spotted the PC could not always spot the next one.__ [176]
[6.23 note] He doubted the OpenAI bet. It became a $135 billion stake. 
[6.24 p] The last chapter also has hard parts, and they should be told plainly. In May 2021 The Wall Street Journal reported that Microsoft board members had investigated a past relationship between Gates and an employee, and that this lay behind his 2020 exit. Microsoft confirmed it had reviewed a concern dating to the year 2000. Gates's spokeswoman said the departure was not related. [177,178]
[6.25 p] Gates also met the convicted sex offender Jeffrey Epstein many times from 2011. An external review for the foundation, reported in July 2026, counted about 30 meetings involving him and foundation staff through 2014, focused on a proposed health fund that never came about and a grant to a peace institute. The foundation says it paid Epstein nothing and never worked with him. In February 2026 Gates apologised to staff, called the meetings a big mistake, and said he took no part in any crime. In June 2026 he gave a closed-door interview to a US House committee. [179,180,181,182,183]
[6.26 p] Divorce changed the money too. After he and Melinda French Gates split in 2021, she left the foundation in 2024 with a $12.5 billion agreement for her own giving. Nairametrics reported in January 2026 that nearly $8 billion of it had moved to her nonprofit. [119,170]

[6.29 p] Put the whole money story together and the arc is plain. A small, cash-rich company kept its founder in control. One clause turned a modest contract into a global standard. __And the fortune that followed is now being deliberately taken apart, by the man who built it.__ [44,147,112]
[6.30 lesson] Ownership is what compounds: Gates never needed a huge salary, since 45% of a growing company did the work. Protect your equity early, because every percent you give away at the start is the most expensive percent you will ever sell.

=== CH7 how-they-work | Inside the machine | How Bill Works
[7.0 h] The man who read the whole thing 
[7.1 p] Picture Redmond on ==30 June 1992==. Joel Spolsky, a young programme manager on the Excel team, has written a plan for a new Excel programming language. By his own memory it runs to about 500 pages. In those days every major feature at Microsoft went through a “BillG review”, a meeting with Bill Gates himself. Spolsky sent the printed stack to Gates's office about 24 hours before the meeting. [184]
[7.2 p] The room was packed with his whole chain of bosses. One colleague had a single job: to keep count of how many times Gates swore. A low count meant a good meeting. Gates sat down, turned to Spolsky, and started flipping through the plan. There were handwritten notes in the margins of every page. [184]
[7.3 note] Five hundred pages. Read overnight. For one meeting. 
[7.4 p] The questions got harder and more detailed. Then came the killer. Would the new language have the same date and time functions as Excel, and would they all work the same way? Spolsky had happened to study exactly that the afternoon before. Yes, he said, except for January and February of 1900. The room went silent. Gates said good work and walked out with the marked-up copy. The swear counter announced four, the lowest anyone could remember. Gates was 36. [184]
[7.5 example] Why January and February 1900? The year 1900 was not a leap year, but Excel treated it as one so it could open Lotus 1-2-3 files, which had the same quirk. Basic started counting from a day earlier, so the two programs agreed on every date except those two months. Nobody in that room expected a junior employee to know this. [184]
[7.6 p] Afterwards a colleague explained the game to Spolsky. Gates was not really checking the document. He was checking whether you had the work under control. __He asked harder and harder questions until you admitted you did not know, and only then did the yelling start.__ He also left alone the software of people he trusted. But you could not bluff him, Spolsky says, because he was an actual programmer. [184]
[7.7 p] Chris Williams saw the same thing that very summer. Microsoft had just bought his employer, Fox Software, for ==$170 million==, and six newcomers were called to Redmond. Gates skipped the pleasantries. He wanted to know why their FoxPro database ran faster than Microsoft's own project. He found the right engineer and questioned him down to single bits and the size of a chip's instruction cache. Williams, who later became Microsoft's vice president of HR, says he watched Gates repeat this for years. [185,186]
[7.8 p] Williams noticed that people gave one of two answers under that pressure. Some said “I don't know” to the richest man on the planet. Others started guessing. The guessers got the swearing, and were told they had said the stupidest thing he had ever heard. In later years, Williams says, a look of disappointment did the same job. He also says Gates could take in a messy debate within seconds and pick out the two or three things that really mattered. [185,186]
[7.9 h] Fighting as a way of working 
[7.10 p] Paul Allen, who ran Microsoft with him, gives the rawest account. By 1978 Gates liked to settle things in intense one-on-one arguments, and he started many of them. Allen says a few people cringed at how Gates would demean colleagues and make them defend themselves. When someone ran late, Gates had a stock reply: he could write that code himself in a weekend. [187]
[7.11 note] This is the boss everyone feared. Now read the twist. 
[7.12 p] Here is the twist. Allen says Gates liked it when people pushed back. He would not pull rank to end an argument. If you stood your ground with the data behind you, he would look down, mutter that he saw your point, and try to make up. He hated losing talented people. Even quiet programmers learned to shout back at their boss. [187]
[7.13 note] Copy the standard. Do not copy the shouting. 
[7.14 p] Allen himself did not think the shouting helped. He asked why civil discussion would not have worked better, and he stayed angry for weeks after their fights. One employee, Bob Greenberg, once put in ==81 hours in four days== to finish a job. When Gates heard he planned to take Friday off, he asked why anyone would want to do that. Allen says he truly could not understand it. [187]
[7.15 h] The parking lot and the empty calendar 
[7.16 p] Gates has confessed to this himself. On BBC Radio 4's Desert Island Discs in 2016, he said he knew every employee's licence plate, so he could look out at the parking lot and see when people came in. He said he eventually had to loosen up once the company grew. Allen remembers him prowling the lot on weekends. [188,187]
[7.17 p] He told students at Northern Arizona University in 2023 that at their age he did not believe in vacations or weekends, or that his colleagues should either. CNBC notes he has admitted to being a difficult boss who sent requests at 2 a.m. His diary was packed to the minute. What cracked that belief was a small paper date book. Warren Buffett showed him one, and whole days in it were blank. [189]
[7.18 note] Busy is not the same as serious. It took him decades to see it. 
[7.19 p] Gates told Charlie Rose in 2017 that he had thought a packed schedule was the only way to do things. __Buffett taught him that a full calendar proves nothing about how serious you are.__ In 2024 he wrote on Threads that it took him far too long to learn this, and that he could have learned it sooner. Today's routine sounds almost ordinary. He has said he aims for about seven hours of sleep, reads for an hour before bed, and washes the dinner dishes himself every night. [189,190]
[7.20 h] Think Week 
[7.21 p] In 2005 a Wall Street Journal reporter became the first journalist allowed into Gates's secret. Twice a year he flew by helicopter or seaplane to a plain two-storey cottage by the water, for seven days alone. Family and staff were banned. A caretaker slipped him two simple meals a day. The ritual began in the 1980s as quiet reading time during visits to his grandmother. [191]
[7.22 p] Two months before each trip, his technical assistant put out an open call across Microsoft. Anyone at any level could send a paper, and the database that year held nearly 300. He read in bed, at the kitchen table and at his desk upstairs, up to 18 hours straight on some days, drinking Diet Orange Crush. A small fridge and a bathroom had been added near the study so he would not lose time going downstairs. His breaks were five minutes for a daily online bridge puzzle and, that week, one 30-minute walk on the beach. [191]
[7.23.0 facts] ~300: papers in the Think Week database in 2005 [191]
[7.23.1 facts] 112: his record for papers read in one week [191]
[7.23.2 facts] 18 hours: straight reading on some days [191]
[7.23.3 facts] 6 inches: thickness of one executive’s printout of his comments [191]
[7.24 p] Then came the flood. By week's end he had read 100 papers and emailed hundreds of people. One executive carried a six-inch printout of the comments on a business trip. One Office team changed direction because of them. A general manager called the whole thing the coolest suggestion box in the world. A week in 1995 inspired “The Internet Tidal Wave”, and plans for the Tablet PC, more secure software and online gaming also grew out of these retreats. [191]
[7.25 example] In 2005 he read a paper from Microsoft's research group in England on stopping internet worms. He thought it might be too good to be true, and worked into the small hours emailing executives about it. By morning, replies had come back from as far away as Cambridge, England. An idea from a researcher had reached the top and travelled around the world in one night. [191]
[7.26 note] Any employee could reach the boss with one good paper. Build that. 
[7.27 h] Memos with no padding 
[7.28 quote] "Winning Internet browser share is a very very important goal for us." -- Bill Gates, email to executive Joachim Kempin, 6 January 1996, quoted in the US court’s Findings of Fact [192]
[7.29 p] His writing is blunt, and we can read it because a court made it public. The same email named the problem in the next line: PC makers were displaying rival browsers and internet services more prominently than Microsoft's. It left no doubt about what he wanted. Gates told government lawyers in 1998 that he deleted ==98 percent== of his email, though he said nobody had intentionally deleted anything the government was seeking. [192,193]
[7.30 h] Who he hired 
[7.31 p] In 1989, at 33, he told NBC's Today show what he wanted. Age and experience did not matter much. He wanted people who were smart before they arrived and full of energy, and Microsoft would train them on the job. Most came straight from college or business school. He kept the company near Seattle partly, he said, because in Silicon Valley rumours spread and staff kept switching employers. [194]
[7.32 p] He was also scared of hiring too fast. When Steve Ballmer joined around 1980, Microsoft had about 30 people. By Ballmer's memory he soon said it needed 50 or 60 more. Gates replied that he was going to bankrupt the company. In that 1997 Newsweek talk, they recalled that Gates had tracked headcount, salaries and incoming cash by hand. He wanted bad news fast, and he went straight to the red numbers in a report. [195]
[7.33 p] He fought to keep good people, sometimes clumsily. In 1996 David Risher told Microsoft he was leaving for a tiny online bookstore. Gates called him in and told him it was the stupidest decision he had ever heard. Risher went anyway and became Amazon's 37th employee. He is now the CEO of Lyft. Gates, famous for being right, missed that one. [196]
[7.34 note] Even a genius can call the next Amazon a stupid career move. 
[7.35 h] The hard part: letting go 
[7.36 p] In January 2000 Gates handed the CEO job to Ballmer and became chief software architect. Ballmer had asked whether he wanted a real CEO or a figurehead, and Gates said a real one. It went badly anyway. Ballmer has said the two did not speak for about a year, because neither knew how to work under this new arrangement. Their wives arranged an awkward dinner to repair things. [197,198]
[7.37 p] Later they split over hardware and phones. Ballmer pushed Microsoft to buy Nokia's phone business for $7.2 billion, and Microsoft later wrote down $8.4 billion on it. Ballmer has said the disagreement with Gates peaked around that phone decision. __If you built the thing, stepping aside takes more skill than building it did.__ [199]
[7.38 p] The intensity followed him into philanthropy, if one book is right. In 2024 the journalist Anupreeta Das wrote that Gates Foundation staff prepared frantically for his reviews and that one former executive compared them to a king holding court. A former employee told her he would catch a footnote on page 9 that did not match one on page 28. Gates's office rejected the book as relying on hearsay and anonymous sources. Treat it as one contested view, not settled fact. [200]

=== CH8 mindset | What he believes | The Gates Operating System
[8.0 p] Strip away the money and you find a short list of beliefs he has acted on for 50 years. Some made him rich. One nearly got his company split in two. Two of them he had to unlearn in middle age. Here are eight, each with the moment that shows it. [187,192,189]
[8.1 h] 1. Stick to what you actually know 
[8.2 p] In Boston in 1974, Paul Allen kept bringing him big ideas, many about hardware. Gates kept popping them. Those would take too many people and too much money, he said. They were not hardware experts. What they knew was software. Allen admits his ideas were often ahead of their time or beyond their reach, and that those reality checks stopped them wasting years. So when the Altair invitation came that December, they were ready for the one thing they could really do. [187]
[8.3 note] Saying no to good ideas is a skill. He had it at 18. 
[8.4 h] 2. Assume someone is about to kill you 
[8.5 p] In May 1995, Microsoft was huge and Windows 95 had not even shipped. Gates still sent his executives a memo warning about a young company called Netscape. He called it a new competitor born on the internet, and he saw that its browser could make the operating system underneath matter much less. Then he turned the whole company around. From 1995 onward, Microsoft spent more than ==$100 million a year== developing Internet Explorer. [192,191]
[8.6 quote] "First we need to offer a decent client" -- Bill Gates, “The Internet Tidal Wave” memo, May 1995, quoted in the US court’s Findings of Fact [192]
[8.7 p] Look at how plain that is. The world's most powerful software boss wrote that his product first had to become merely decent. __Fear made him honest about how far behind he was.__ That same fear also pushed Microsoft into the conduct that a federal judge later condemned. The What Went Wrong chapter tells that half of the story. [192]
[8.8 h] 3. Truth beats rank 
[8.9 p] Allen's stories and Williams's stories, from more than a decade apart, match. The fastest way to anger Gates was to bluff. The fastest way to win his respect was to know more than he did and say so loudly. Williams says senior people would call him out when they disagreed, and that he was almost always right anyway. Spolsky earned a word of praise by knowing about two strange months in 1900, and that was the end of the meeting. [187,185,184]
[8.10 example] Try this in your own team. When someone asks a question you cannot answer, say “I don't know, I'll find out by tonight”. In Gates's meetings, those were the people who survived. The ones who made something up did not. [185]
[8.11 h] 4. Every life has the same value 
[8.12 p] This is the belief that changed his second life. At Harvard in 2007 he described reading, with Melinda, an article about millions of children in poor countries dying from diseases rich countries had long ago made harmless. One he had never heard of, rotavirus, was killing half a million children a year. They had assumed the world would fix anything that could be fixed so cheaply. It had not. The market did not reward saving these children, he said, and governments did not pay for it. [5]
[8.13 quote] "reducing inequity is the highest human achievement" -- Bill Gates, Harvard commencement address, 7 June 2007 [5]
[8.14 p] He made the same point with theatre at a TED talk in 2009. He opened a jar of mosquitoes on stage, said they were not infectious, and noted that the world had put more money into drugs for baldness than into malaria research. Rich men go bald. Poor children get malaria. __Markets follow money, not need, and he decided to fund the gap.__ [201]
[8.15 note] He was 51 when he told Harvard this was his one regret. 
[8.16 h] 5. Measure it, and publish your failures 
[8.17 p] In the same speech he said the hard part of doing good is complexity. His method has four stages: set a clear goal, find the approach with the most leverage, find the best technology for it, and use the tools you already have in the meantime. Then measure the results and share both the wins and the losses so others can learn. [5]
[8.18 p] He put that into practice in his first annual letter, in January 2009. The foundation had spent ==about $2 billion over nine years== trying to build better American high schools, much of it on smaller ones. He wrote plainly that many of the small schools did not improve student achievement in any significant way. Most billionaires never write that sentence about their own money. It is more useful than any success story. [202,203]
[8.19 h] 6. Optimism is a calculation 
[8.20 p] The foundation's most prized number is the drop in child deaths from preventable causes, which UN figures show fell by almost half between 2000 and 2020. That is his case for hope. He also reports the bad numbers. In a blog post around the turn of 2026 he noted that child deaths rose from 4.6 million to 4.8 million that year, the first rise this century, and warned of five difficult years. He still said he was hopeful about the long term. [204,205]
[8.21 p] The same habit explains his October 2025 climate memo, which angered some climate scientists. He wrote that climate change is serious but will not end civilisation, that temperature is not the best measure of progress, and that health and prosperity are the strongest defences for the poor. You may agree or not. __He judges a cause by the suffering it can reduce, and he will say so even when his own side objects.__ [206]
[8.22 note] Hope with numbers attached is harder to argue with. 
[8.23 h] 7. You are allowed to change your mind 
[8.24 p] He believed in packed calendars for decades, until Buffett's blank pages showed him otherwise. He told graduates in 2023 not to wait as long as he did to take a break. In 2023 he wrote that the risks of AI were real but manageable. By August 2026 he was warning that the coming transition would be one of the most turbulent periods in history. His 2009 letter shows the foundation changing course on schools in the same way. [189,207,208,202]
[8.25 h] 8. Money is for giving away 
[8.26 p] His mother, Mary, was seriously ill with cancer in the run-up to his wedding. At a bridal event she read aloud a letter to Melinda about marriage, and it closed with the old line that from those to whom much is given, much is expected. Gates repeated that line to Harvard's graduates. He told the BBC in 2016 that you are not going to spend it on yourself, and that only a small part should go to his children so they could make their own way. [5,188]
[8.27 p] In May 2025 he took it to the end. He said he would give away ==99%== of his remaining fortune, about $107 billion at the time, and that the foundation would close in 2045 rather than two decades after his death. He told the Associated Press that 20 years struck the right balance. Critics ask why one rich family should have so much say over global health. His answer is that any private citizen can choose where their money goes. [204]
[8.28 note] Once the world’s richest. Now planning to end up not rich. 

=== CH9 personal-life | Off the clock | The Private Bill
[9.0 h] Parents who pushed 
[9.1 p] Gates has always credited his parents, in both directions. His mother pushed him to develop social skills and to talk with adults, which he says he did not enjoy. His parents, he told the BBC, were involved in Planned Parenthood and taught him about giving back. Mary Gates died in 1994, the year he married. His father, Bill Gates Sr., lived to 94, helped steer the early foundation, and died in September 2020. [209,188,210,211,5]
[9.2 p] His father's last years turned into a cause. In November 2017 Gates put ==$50 million of his own money== into the Dementia Discovery Fund, separate from the foundation, saying Alzheimer's had struck his own family. He described how awful it is to watch someone you love lose their mental capacity. In early 2018 he revealed that his father had the disease. [212,213]
[9.3 h] Different, and finally saying so 
[9.4 p] In his 2025 memoir, Source Code, Gates wrote that if he were growing up today he would probably be diagnosed on the autism spectrum. He has no formal diagnosis. He told Axios he always knew he was different, in his energy and intensity and in going off to study things, and that as a child he kept misreading social signals. The first time someone asked him about it, about 25 years earlier, he reacted angrily that he ran a company. Then he thought it was probably true. [209,214]
[9.5 p] He says he shared this for children like him. They should treat it as a strength, he says, and be proud of what they are good at. That is his own telling, and we leave it there. __He does not explain his harshness at Microsoft with it, and neither should we.__ [209]
[9.6 note] If you feel different, read that interview. He felt it too. 
[9.7 h] A marriage, and its end 
[9.8 p] Melinda French studied computer science and business and built a career at Microsoft. She and Gates married, had three children, and built the foundation together. On ==3 May 2021== they announced their divorce after 27 years. It became final that August. They first agreed to a two-year test of running the foundation together. She left in May 2024 with $12.5 billion for her own philanthropy, a move he said disappointed him. [202,215,200,216]
[9.9 p] In her first interview on it, in 2022, she said there was no single moment. She named an affair he had in 2000 with a Microsoft employee, and his relationship with Jeffrey Epstein, whom she had told him not to deal with. She said she met Epstein once and had nightmares afterwards. Asked about other affairs and about his ties to Epstein, she said those were questions for him to answer. He said at the time that meeting Epstein was a mistake he deeply regretted. [215]
[9.10 p] He told The Times of London in January 2025 that the divorce was the mistake he most regrets, and that it was miserable for both of them. He had earlier said on NBC that he was responsible for causing his family a lot of pain. Knowing it would not last, he said, he would still do it again for the three children and the work. In June 2026 he told Congress of three extramarital affairs and said they had been painful for his family. [216,217,218,219]
[9.11 p] He now describes Paula Hurd, a philanthropist and the widow of Oracle's Mark Hurd, as his serious girlfriend. In early 2025 he said he had two grandchildren and still saw Melinda at family events. The family has kept the rest private, and so will we. [217,216]
[9.12 note] He told The Times his other regrets do not matter. This one does. 
[9.13 h] How he spends it 
[9.14 p] Most of his money is going one way: Forbes counts more than ==$63.9 billion== donated to the foundation. What is left is run by a professional team at his investment firm, Cascade. Its oddest holding is dirt. The Land Report ranked him the largest private owner of farmland in America, with about 248,000 acres of farmland in 2022. He told Reddit in 2023 it was under 1/4,000 of US farmland, an investment to make farms more productive, and that there was no grand scheme. [1,220]
[9.15 p] His most famous purchase came in 1994, when he paid ==$30.8 million== for the Codex Leicester, a notebook in which Leonardo da Vinci worked through hydraulics and the moon. An American oil executive had bought it in 1980 for about $5.1 million. Gates has lent it to museums for public display. It is a very Gates trophy: a dead genius thinking on paper about how things work. [221,222]
[9.16 p] He admits to an obvious contradiction. In his 2025 climate memo he said some people would call him a hypocrite because of his carbon footprint, which he says he fully offsets with legitimate credits. His pleasures are less grand. He plays bridge, including that five-minute daily puzzle on Think Week. His Desert Island list included Under Pressure, Blue Skies, Are You Experienced and a song from Hamilton, and he asked for DVDs of the world's great lectures as his luxury. [206,191,188]
[9.17 p] He dedicated the Beatles' Two of Us to Steve Jobs on that show, the rival with whom he had competed and cooperated for decades. He said only the two of them understood how intense it had been. When Jobs was ill, their talks turned mellow, and were about their lives and their children. __Even his great rivalries ended up as long conversations.__ [188]
[9.18 note] Decades of competing, and the song he picks is Two of Us. 
[9.19 h] Old friends 
[9.20 p] His deepest friendship is with Warren Buffett, 25 years his senior. Gates sat on the Berkshire board for 16 years, and Buffett gave the foundation more than $47 billion over two decades. In July 2026 Buffett stopped those gifts, called Gates's Epstein ties distasteful, and said in the same breath that he too had misjudged people. He called their friendship wonderful. Gates had visited him in Omaha a few weeks earlier, and said his gratitude was beyond measure. [223]

=== CH10 the-brand | The public Bill | The Many Faces of Bill
[10.0 p] Few people have had as many public faces in one life. In the 1990s he was the nerd who won. By 1998 he was the bully in a courtroom. In the 2010s he was the kindly man who wanted to save children and talked about toilets. By 2026 he was explaining himself to Congress. He did not plan most of these turns. But he did work hard on the middle one. [224,225,226,227]
[10.1 h] The nerd on late-night television 
[10.2 p] In November 1995 Gates, 39 and already the world's richest person at $12.9 billion, went on David Letterman's show to promote his book The Road Ahead and Internet Explorer. Only about 14% of Americans were using the web. Letterman asked what the internet actually was. When the talk turned to following a baseball game online, Letterman asked if radio rang a bell. He ended by joking that there was no money in it. [224]
[10.3 note] He could not convince the host. He was also completely right. 
[10.4 p] He did not win the room, and that became the image: not cool, just correct. In that same interview he called a thinking computer a scary thought, and said almost no progress had been made on it. Thirty-one years later, that scary thought has become the main subject of his public life. [224,208]
[10.5 h] The worst year of his public life 
[10.6.0 timeline] Early 1998: In Brussels, activists protesting his wealth and corporate power throw cream tarts at him. Four hit him in the face. [228]
[10.6.1 timeline] Apr 1998: At a trade show, Windows 98 crashes on stage in front of him when an employee plugs in a scanner. He jokes that this must be why it is not shipping yet. [229]
[10.6.2 timeline] May 1998: The Justice Department and states sue Microsoft for monopoly abuse. [230,193]
[10.6.3 timeline] Aug to Sep 1998: He gives 20 hours of videotaped testimony over three days at Microsoft’s campus. [193]
[10.6.4 timeline] Nov 1998: Fortune reports that he flopped on video after the government plays clips in court. [225]
[10.7 p] The video did what no rival could. Gates argued about the meanings of ordinary words. At one point he said it depended on what the lawyer meant by the word compete. The judge openly laughed at the tape. The world saw the smartest man in software looking evasive. __A reputation that took 20 years to build was damaged by a few hours of video.__ One review of the leading book on the trial called it as much a public-relations disaster as a legal one. [225,193,231,232]
[10.8 h] Toilets, mosquitoes and the second act 
[10.9 p] Then came the rebuild, and it was clever because it was not about him. In 2009 he began publishing a yearly letter about what the foundation had learned, including what had failed. That year he opened the mosquito jar at TED. A year later he came back to TED with fireflies. CNBC later noted he had won a reputation as a great presenter, which nobody said in 1998. [202,201]
[10.10 example] In January 2015 he went on The Tonight Show with two glasses of water marked 1 and 2, and had Jimmy Fallon choose one. They both drank. Then Gates revealed that both glasses held what had once been sewage, cleaned by a machine called the Omniprocessor. Fallon spat some out, then admitted it tasted really good. The stunt carried a hard number: about 1.8 billion people drink water contaminated with faeces. [226]
[10.11 p] He also made himself reachable. By January 2023 he had done 11 “Ask Me Anything” sessions on Reddit, taking questions on everything from being a grandfather to what phone he uses. He writes about what he is learning on his own site, Gates Notes. __He stopped defending himself and started explaining the world, and people warmed to it.__ [220,226]
[10.12 p] His style is that of a teacher who cannot help himself. He leads with a number, then a surprise, then a joke. In his August 2026 AI essay he even warned readers of his own bias, saying he still has financial ties to the tech industry, and that readers must decide whether that clouds his view. Few billionaires put that in writing. [201,208]
[10.13 h] The microchip monster 
[10.14 p] Fame has a price. In May 2020 a Yahoo News/YouGov poll found ==28% of Americans== believed Gates wanted to use a Covid vaccine to implant tracking microchips, including 44% of Republicans. There is no truth in it. The Associated Press has described the foundation as the target of numerous unfounded conspiracy theories. Gates said he had never been involved in anything like a microchip, and called the poll result a bit concerning. [233,234,204]
[10.15 p] Such theories have also reached the courts. In October 2024 a Dutch court in Leeuwarden ruled it had jurisdiction over him in a civil claim brought by people alleging vaccine injury. Fact-checkers stressed this was a procedural ruling in a civil case, not a criminal charge or a finding against him. The plaintiffs' claims remain unproven, and we found no reliable report of any verdict. [235,236]
[10.16 note] Fame means strangers will invent a villain. Prepare for it. 
[10.17 h] How India sees him 
[10.18 p] India has seen every version of him. In December 2001 he made a 24-hour trip, marking ten years of Microsoft in India and signing an alliance with Infosys. That morning he sat on an old wooden bench in a Delhi polio clinic and squeezed vaccine drops into the mouths of 30 squirming children. Most of the mothers, AP reported, had never heard of him. He said what mattered most was people having healthy children. [237]
[10.19 p] India then became the proof for his whole idea. By 2009 the foundation had committed nearly $1 billion there, and India's president gave him the Indira Gandhi Prize. He praised how the country sent over 2 million health workers to more than 200 million homes, and even to train stations and ferry terminals. India had the world's highest number of polio cases in 2009. By January 2012 it had gone a full year without a single reported case. [238,239]
[10.20.0 facts] 30: children he gave polio drops to at a Delhi clinic in 2001 [237]
[10.20.1 facts] ~$1bn: foundation commitments in India by July 2009 [238]
[10.20.2 facts] 200m+: homes visited on an Indian immunisation day [238]
[10.21 p] The mood can flip fast. In late 2024 a clip went viral from a podcast with Reid Hoffman. Gates called India a kind of laboratory for trying things that, once proven, could be taken elsewhere. He was making a compliment, in his way: he said India was stable, improving, and home to the foundation's biggest non-US office and most of its pilot projects. But some Indians online heard guinea pigs. Others defended him and said the outrage was unfair. [240]
[10.22 note] One careless word can sour twenty years of goodwill. Choose carefully. 
[10.23 p] Then came February 2026. Gates was to give a keynote at India's AI Impact Summit in New Delhi. His foundation had dismissed rumours that he would not show. Hours before the speech, he pulled out. The foundation said it was to keep the focus on the summit's priorities, and sent Ankur Vora instead. It came as scrutiny of his Epstein links intensified, though the foundation did not answer Reuters' question on any link. He has also travelled there for talks, including in Andhra Pradesh on health and governance. [241,242]
[10.24 h] Elsewhere 
[10.25 p] Europe shows how far the wheel can turn. In 1998 Brussels threw cream tarts. On 21 October 2026 he was back there, meeting European Commission President Ursula von der Leyen and spending 25 minutes at the Atomium talking about nuclear energy. Three years earlier, Belgian media note, he had toured the city's Sewer Museum. In the United States, meanwhile, his name is now tied to a congressional inquiry. No one has one picture of him any more. [228,227]

=== CH11 what-went-wrong | The costly mistakes | What Went Wrong
[11.0 p] This is the chapter to read twice. Gates made some of the most expensive strategic errors in business, broke a partner's trust, saw his company ruled to have broken the law, and at 55 sat down with a convicted sex offender in the hope of raising money. Some of it he has admitted. Some of it he disputes. Here is what happened and how each one ended. [187,192,243,218]
[11.1 h] 1. The monopoly case 
[11.2 p] Fear of Netscape produced the most consequential behaviour of his career. A federal trial ran from October 1998 to June 1999, and in late 1999 Judge Thomas Penfield Jackson published his findings of fact. They are public, and they are remarkable to read. They describe Microsoft using control of Windows as a lever against almost everyone who worried it. [192]
[11.3 example] Three scenes from the court record. In 1995 Gates told Intel's CEO, Andy Grove, that Intel could not count on Microsoft to support its next chips as long as it built competing platform software. In 1997, after Apple made Netscape its standard browser install, Gates called Apple's CEO to ask how they should announce the cancellation of Mac Office. And while IBM kept promoting its own rival software, Microsoft tied IBM's Windows 95 licence to an audit dispute. IBM missed the back-to-school season and lost substantial revenue. [192]
[11.4 quote] "Through its conduct toward Netscape, IBM, Compaq, Intel, and others, Microsoft has demonstrated that it will use its prodigious market power and immense profits to harm any firm that insists on pursuing initiatives that could intensify competition against one of Microsoft's core products." -- Judge Thomas Penfield Jackson, Findings of Fact, paragraph 412 [192]
[11.5 p] Gates saw it differently and never really budged. In his deposition he stuck to business explanations for everything the government called illegal, even when shown contradicting documents. The Seattle Times, after reading all 684 pages, said he quibbled and dodged, and that the government's excerpts had been fair. Microsoft complained they were out of context. [193]
[11.6.0 timeline] 18 May 1998: The case is filed. [230]
[11.6.1 timeline] June 2000: Judge Jackson orders Microsoft split into an operating-systems company and an applications company. The order is put on hold pending appeal. [243]
[11.6.2 timeline] 28 June 2001: The appeals court throws out the break-up and removes Jackson, but upholds the finding that Microsoft broke antitrust law. [243,244]
[11.6.3 timeline] 12 Nov 2002: The case settles with a final judgment. There is no break-up. [230]
[11.6.4 timeline] 12 May 2011: The last of the court’s oversight expires. [230]
[11.7 p] So who won? Both. Microsoft stayed in one piece, and the judge hurt his own case by giving secret interviews to reporters, including a half-joke that Gates should read a biography of Napoleon to learn about his own arrogance. But the ruling that Microsoft had illegally protected a monopoly stood. Oversight lasted until 2011. __He kept the company and lost years of attention and much of his good name.__ [243,244,232,230]
[11.8 p] Europe added a bill. The European Commission fined Microsoft ==€497 million== in 2004 for tying Windows Media Player to Windows, then €280 million more, then €899 million for not complying, later cut to €860 million. In 2013 came €561 million, after 15.3 million European users were not shown a promised browser choice screen. The total came to ==about €2.2 billion==. Gates was no longer CEO by then, but the playbook began on his watch. [245]
[11.9 note] Being big changes the rules. What was hustle at 25 is illegal at 40. 
[11.10 h] 2. Losing the phone 
[11.11 p] Then the fear failed, just when he needed it. In 2019 Gates told the venture firm Village Global that his greatest mistake ever was whatever mismanagement of his caused Microsoft not to become what Android became. Software platforms, he explained, are winner-take-all markets. Microsoft was early with Windows Mobile and still lost the place beside Apple to Google. He has said the antitrust fight distracted the company at the time. [246,247]
[11.12 p] You could not ask for a better warning. The man who spotted the internet threat in a Think Week missed the next one while his company was busy in court. His successor later spent $7.2 billion on Nokia's phones to catch up and wrote down $8.4 billion. __Winning one platform war does not make you ready for the next.__ [246,199,191]
[11.13 note] He spotted one wave and missed the next. Nobody is immune. 
[11.14 h] 3. How he treated Paul Allen 
[11.15 p] One more cost was personal, and we have it from one side: Allen's 2011 memoir. Allen says Gates pushed the partnership from 50-50 to 60-40 and then to 64-36. In 1980, Allen says, they agreed to offer Ballmer up to 5% of the company, and Gates, while Allen was away, offered ==8.75%==. Allen called it a major breach of faith. Gates made up the extra points from his own share. [187]
[11.16 p] In September 1982 Allen was diagnosed with Hodgkin's lymphoma and began radiation. One evening that December he says he overheard Gates and Ballmer complaining about his recent output and discussing how they might dilute his stake by issuing options to themselves and others. He burst in and shouted at them. Ballmer came to his home to apologise and said the plan was unfair. Gates sent a six-page handwritten letter of apology. [187]
[11.17 p] Allen says that as he was leaving in early 1983, Gates tried to buy his stock for $5 a share. Allen would not discuss less than $10. Gates said no. That refusal, Allen writes, worked in his favour, because he would otherwise have sold far too soon. __Trying to squeeze his partner's shares out cheaply only left Allen holding them.__ [187]
[11.18 note] Your co-founder is sick. Your first thought should not be his equity. 
[11.19 h] 4. The schools that did not work 
[11.20 p] His philanthropy has had its own big misses. As the mindset chapter showed, about $2 billion over nine years for American high schools fell short of its goal, and he said so. The critics ask a harder question that applies to all his giving: should one rich family set the agenda for schools or global health at all? AP noted researchers have asked this for years. He answers that people can criticise it, and that he will keep going. [202,203,204]
[11.21 h] 5. Oxford and the vaccine rights 
[11.22 p] In April 2020 Oxford University promised to give its Covid vaccine rights to any drugmaker. A few weeks later, Kaiser Health News reported, Oxford, urged on by the Gates Foundation, reversed course and signed an exclusive deal with AstraZeneca. Critics said it was a rare chance to change how vaccines are priced, and that it was missed. Others still regarded the foundation as a positive force in the pandemic, as the Seattle Times reported. It remains argued over. [248,249]
[11.23 h] 6. Workplace conduct 
[11.24 p] In May 2021 Microsoft confirmed it had hired an outside law firm in late 2019 after an employee raised concerns about an intimate relationship with Gates some 20 years earlier. His spokesperson said there was an affair almost 20 years ago that ended amicably, and that leaving the board in March 2020 had nothing to do with it. The Wall Street Journal, citing anonymous sources, reported that board members had decided he should not stay. The New York Times, also citing anonymous sources, reported overtures to women who worked for him. [250]
[11.25 note] Power at work is never just personal. Boards and staff notice. 
[11.26 h] 7. Jeffrey Epstein 
[11.27 p] This is the mistake that has cost him the most in recent years. Be precise about it. Gates has not been accused of any crime, and GeekWire reports that no Epstein victim has accused him of wrongdoing. He denies knowing about Epstein's ongoing abuse. What he admits is bad enough. He spent time with a man he knew had a sex-related conviction, and did not look into it. [223,251,219]
[11.28 p] He told Congress the story went like this. His science adviser, Boris Nikolic, arranged a dinner in New York in January 2011. Epstein claimed he could raise billions for global health from wealthy clients. The money never came. Gates listed about 15 contacts through 2014, some of them Skype calls, including visits to homes in New York and Paris and one flight to Palm Beach. He said he only grasped the scale of the crimes in 2018, through the Miami Herald. [219,218]
[11.29 quote] "I should never have met with Epstein in the first place" -- Bill Gates, transcribed interview with the US House Oversight Committee, 10 June 2026 [252,227]
[11.30 p] Melinda warned him. GeekWire, citing the Wall Street Journal, reports that he kept meeting Epstein after she raised concerns in 2013. Foundation staff raised concerns too. The law firm WilmerHale, reviewing the foundation in 2026, found ==about 30 meetings== between Epstein and 10 foundation leaders and staff from 2011 to 2014. It found no evidence the foundation paid Epstein, and none of knowledge of or participation in his crimes. It did not look at Gates's personal dealings or communications with him. [251,253,254]
[11.31 p] The Justice Department's release of files in early 2026 brought all of it back. On 24 February he apologised to foundation staff in a town hall, saying it was a huge mistake that hurt others, and he told them he did nothing illicit and saw nothing illicit. In June he testified voluntarily for nearly six hours and said he was not blackmailed, though Epstein's notes to himself looked as if they were heading that way. He said he never interacted with victims but may have been in their presence. [255,219,256,257]
[11.32.0 facts] ~30: meetings between Epstein and foundation people, 2011–14, per WilmerHale [253]
[11.32.1 facts] No evidence: that the foundation paid Epstein, per the same review [254]
[11.32.2 facts] ~6 hours: his voluntary interview with Congress, June 2026 [219]
[11.32.3 facts] $47bn+: given by Buffett since 2006, before he stopped in July 2026 [223]
[11.33 p] The costs are now visible. He gave up his India keynote. Warren Buffett left the foundation out of his yearly gift, though Buffett says the main reason is his children's readiness. People who have spoken with him told Axios his new AI campaign is partly an effort to rebuild his reputation. By his own telling, all of it began with a simple trade: he thought a bad man could bring in good money for a good cause. __No cause is good enough to skip checking who you are dealing with.__ [241,223,258,207]
[11.34 h] What not to copy 
[11.35 p] Do not humiliate people to test them; you can demand facts without the abuse, and Allen thought it was never needed. Do not treat a sick partner as a problem on a spreadsheet. Do not assume that winning the market means the rules no longer apply to you. Do not argue about the meaning of words when a camera is on you. And do not let a good purpose become your excuse for ignoring the warnings of your spouse and your staff. [187,192,193,253]

=== CH12 today | As of November 2026 | Where He Stands Now
[12.0 p] Bill Gates turned 71 on 28 October 2026. He is the chairman of the Gates Foundation and of the nuclear company TerraPower, the founder of Breakthrough Energy, and he still advises Microsoft. Forbes put his fortune at ==$120.4 billion== on 1 November 2026, which it ranked 16th in the world. His Microsoft stake is now under 1%, Forbes estimates. [1,251,259,208]
[12.1.0 facts] $120.4bn: Forbes real-time net worth, 1 Nov 2026 (#16 in the world) [1]
[12.1.1 facts] $63.9bn+: donated by him to the foundation so far, per Forbes [1]
[12.1.2 facts] $9bn: Gates Foundation budget for 2026, its largest ever [205]
[12.1.3 facts] 31 Dec 2045: the day the foundation closes for good [258]
[12.2 p] That number will swing with markets, and the plan is for it to shrink to almost nothing. Forbes has estimated he would have about ==$356 billion== more if he had kept what he gave away. His stated aim is that the foundation will spend about $200 billion in 20 years, at around $9 billion a year, and then disappear. __Fortune lists usually reward what people hold on to. He is trying to fall off them on purpose.__ [1,204,260]
[12.3 h] A shrinking machine with a deadline 
[12.4 p] To run a charity that ends, you plan like a business winding down. In January 2026 the board approved a record $9 billion budget and capped operating costs at $1.25 billion, about 14% of the total. To get there the foundation will cut up to 500 of its 2,375 jobs by 2030. It also created a new division for its Africa and India offices. Its priorities are maternal and child health, infectious disease and poverty. [205]
[12.5 p] The money picture has changed too. About 41% of what the foundation has received so far came from Buffett, who has now stopped giving. Buffett's stated reason is that his children are ready to distribute his fortune. The foundation's 2025 endowment was $77 billion, and it expects Gates's own remaining wealth to fund the rest. Governance has also tightened: after the WilmerHale review, the board approved central vetting for brokers and donors, and heightened review of meetings held at private homes. [204,223,254]
[12.6 note] A deadline focuses a charity the way cash running out focuses a startup. 
[12.7 h] The nuclear bet 
[12.8 p] His biggest personal technology bet is being built in Wyoming, near a retiring coal plant. TerraPower won a federal permit on ==4 March 2026== for its Natrium reactor at Kemmerer, the first commercial reactor approved for construction in nearly a decade and the first of its non-water-cooled kind in more than 40 years. Official construction began on 23 April. It is a 345-megawatt sodium-cooled plant with molten-salt storage that can push output up to 500 megawatts when needed. [259,261]
[12.9 p] It is not finished, and it is not guaranteed. TerraPower expects completion around 2030, and it still needs a separate operating licence before it can run. On climate more broadly, Gates's October 2025 memo said the world should cut the cost gap between clean and dirty ways of making things like cement, steel and jet fuel down to zero. He calls that a key climate strategy. [261,206]
[12.10 h] The AI alarm 
[12.11 p] His new mission is the one he once called a scary thought. On 26 August 2026 he published a 6,000-word essay, his first long piece on AI in three years. It says the shift will be one of the most turbulent times in human history, and that the world is not preparing. He said many jobs will disappear for good, with entry and mid-level roles most at risk, within about a decade rather than over generations. [262,263,208]
[12.12 p] He also made concrete proposals, which is rare for him on AI. Tax AI tokens and robots. Set aside some kinds of work for people only, a zone he calls human reserved, like a nature reserve. Build new national bodies and an international one for AI, which would need US and China to cooperate. He told The Verge's Decoder podcast in October that his big concern is a 20-year transition with winners, losers and a lot of disruption. [262,263,264]
[12.13 example] Think about what this means if you are 18. Gates's essay names the accounting worker replaced by a bot, and the $20-an-hour worker who loses out to a $10-an-hour robot. He says the jobs at most risk are entry-level and mid-level, and that the new ones will mostly need skills that take years to learn. Those are the first jobs of your career. Plan for that now. [208]
[12.14 p] In October 2026 Axios reported he was preparing a bigger campaign, talking with AI executives including Anthropic's Dario Amodei and telling one person he could reach CEOs better than they could. Axios also reported that people who have spoken with him see it partly as an effort to rebuild his reputation after Epstein, and he has said he knows some will dismiss his warnings because of it. Later that month he warned of more AI-powered cyberattacks within a year, while saying AI getting out of control is still years away. [207,265]
[12.15 h] What he says he wants next 
[12.16 p] He has been plain about it. He will write about AI regularly, raise it with lawmakers every time he visits Washington and with leaders abroad, and push for a national and international framework. He wants polio gone, malaria and other killers controlled, and malnutrition reduced by 2045. He told the AP that the foundation's work has had far more impact than he expected, and called it his second and final career. [208,204]
[12.17 p] He also knows the clock is real. At 69 he acknowledged he may not have a say in how it all ends. So the question that hangs over the last pages of this book is simple. LESSONS
0 Read the whole thing.: In 1992 Gates read a 500-page plan overnight and filled every margin with notes before one meeting. Nobody could bluff him after that. Before your next big meeting, do the reading nobody else will do.
1 Say "I don't know".: At Microsoft, Chris Williams saw that people who admitted they did not know survived Gates's questions, and people who guessed got destroyed. Guessing costs more than ignorance. Say it, then go and find out by tonight.
2 Know the details of your own business.: Gates questioned an engineer down to single bits after buying Fox Software in 1992. You cannot lead what you do not understand. Learn enough of the real work that nobody can fool you.
3 Take a think week.: Twice a year he went alone to a cottage with a stack of papers, and a week in 1995 led to "The Internet Tidal Wave". You do not need a cottage. Block out real time to read and think about where your market is going.
4 Let anyone bring you an idea.: Think Week took papers from every level of Microsoft, and a researcher's worm paper reached the top in a night. The best ideas rarely start with the boss. Build one simple route from the newest person to you.
5 Write the bad news down.: In his 1995 memo Gates told his own executives that Microsoft first needed to offer a decent browser. In 2009 he wrote that many of the schools he funded did not work. Plain words about your own failure are how a team turns around.
6 Watch your payroll.: When Ballmer wanted to add 50 or 60 people around 1980, Gates said he would bankrupt the company, and he had been tracking salaries and incoming cash by hand. Know your cash number by heart. Grow when you can afford it, then push hard.
7 Stop filling every minute.: He packed his days until Warren Buffett showed him a date book with blank pages. A full calendar proves nothing. Keep empty space, because that is where your thinking happens.
8 Do not rule people through fear.: He memorised licence plates and swore at staff, and Paul Allen never believed it helped. Gates himself later told graduates not to repeat it. Demand great work with your standards, not your temper.
9 Treat your co-founder fairly.: Allen says Gates pushed the split to 64-36, offered Ballmer more equity behind his back, and discussed diluting him during his cancer treatment. Allen wrote it all down in a memoir decades later. Agree on equity early, put it in writing, and never touch it when someone is down.
10 Learn the rules before you get big.: Tactics that looked like hustle at Microsoft became evidence in a federal court, where a judge ruled the company had illegally protected a monopoly. Once you are the giant, the law watches you differently. Get good advice before you squeeze a partner.
11 Fight the next war, not the last.: He spotted the internet in 1995 and then lost phones to Android, which he calls his greatest mistake. Winning makes you slow. Ask every year what could make your best product matter less.
12 Check who you are dealing with.: He met Epstein in 2011 hoping for billions for global health, knowing of a conviction and not looking into it. The money never came, and the cost has been enormous. A good cause is never a reason to skip the background check, or to ignore your spouse's warning.
13 Fund what the market ignores.: At Harvard he said the market did not reward saving poor children, so they kept dying of things that cost under a dollar to prevent. Your biggest opportunities can sit where nobody is looking. Ask who is being ignored, and why.
14 Decide what your money is for.: In 2025 he promised to give away 99% of his fortune and close his foundation in 2045, following his mother's line that much is expected from those given much. Wealth with no purpose only piles up. Decide early what you are working for.
