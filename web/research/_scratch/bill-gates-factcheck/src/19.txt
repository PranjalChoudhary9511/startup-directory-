What does a doctor say to a philanthropist who’s already given over $100bn to improve global health and reduce poverty, and yet is still willing to learn how to do more, and do it more effectively?
In a roundtable with Bill Gates on global health, I shared two challenges I believe we can solve together:
1. How we break down language barriers so women can easily and safely access the care and information they need, and the role AI and open-source datasets can play in reducing these barriers.
2. How diaspora networks can better collaborate to share effective approaches to women’s health across communities and borders, drawing on work I’ve previously done to adapt a programme from rural Tanzania for implementation in the NHS.
As clinicians, we are experts at treating conditions, but as advocates we must be better at supporting initiatives that also address underlying root causes. Our professional and cultural experiences can help shape both well-meaning philanthropic programmes and the decisions being made by policymakers.
Today, with governments unfortunately retreating from ODA, philanthropy can form stronger alliances with professional and community changemakers to ensure that global health solutions are driven by the people closest to the challenges and solutions on the ground.
Thank you Gates Foundation and Global Health Partnerships (formerly THET) team for the invite, and I look forward to continue to work alongside you to improve healthcare for women everywhere.

Had quite an exciting Friday...
...met Bill Gates 
I joined a number of health care professionals from the UK to advocate for greater support for global health in the UK. Bill clearly knows his health care policy and is quite rightly worried that changing world tensions will continue to reduce countries spending on international development. 
We discussed the importance of clinical communities of practice and networks, and I was able mention my experience with the Global Pediatric Readiness Network (GPRN) and the Pediatric Emergency Research Network (PERN). 
A common theme threading through all the discussions was how learning on implementation between the UK and other counties is bi-directional, collaborative networks enable education (either through success or failure) in one community or country to be applied in another. 
Thanks to colleagues from Healthy World Secure Britain and the Global Health Partnerships initiative for enabling this to happen.

 In today’s complex business environment, the Chief Marketing Officer is no longer just a brand custodian but a strategic force shaping trust, influence, and long-term value. In this in-depth conversation, Adefisayo Akinsanya MBA, ARPA, AMNIPR, ANIMN, CDMP offers a clear window into how modern CMOs must think and lead to drive real business outcomes.
At the heart of her philosophy is the belief that communication is not a support function but a growth lever. She positions influence—not visibility—as the true source of CMO power, arguing that while data guides decisions and brand expresses intent, influence is what ultimately moves organisations forward. Throughout the interview, she consistently emphasises disciplined judgment: knowing when to speak, when to stay silent, and how to align messaging with purpose rather than noise.
Akinsanya highlights a recurring tension in the industry—between being seen and being strategic. Her approach prioritises relevance, credibility, and long-term trust over short-term attention. She also challenges widely accepted practices, warning against confusing reach with trust, over-polished storytelling detached from reality, and blind reliance on automation without human insight.
Leadership, in her view, is situational. Collaboration matters, but decisiveness becomes critical during crises. Above all, alignment stands out as her non-negotiable principle: what organisations say, do, and are willing to defend must consistently reinforce one another. When this alignment breaks, trust erodes and trust, she reminds us, is the most valuable and fragile corporate asset.
This conversation offers rare clarity on how senior communication leaders navigate influence, data, ethics, and executive decision-making in high-stakes environments.

 WeForest
 • 2K followers
 𝗣𝘂𝘁𝘁𝗶𝗻𝗴 𝗛𝗲𝗮𝗹𝘁𝗵 𝗦𝘆𝘀𝘁𝗲𝗺𝘀 𝗮𝘁 𝘁𝗵𝗲 𝗛𝗲𝗮𝗿𝘁 𝗼𝗳 𝗚𝗖8
As Malawi moves forward with Global Fund Grant Cycle 8 (GC8) processes, we have an opportunity to think differently about Resilient and Sustainable Systems for Health (RSSH).
The question is not simply “What should we fund?” but:
“What should we invest in today to build a health system that delivers better outcomes tomorrow?”
For me, the RSSH conversation in GC8 should focus on a few essentials:
(a) A stronger health workforce — the right skills, numbers and distribution, with greater focus on retention and continuous development.
(b) Better data and digital health systems — integrated, timely and reliable information that is actually used for planning and decision-making.
(c) Stronger laboratories and diagnostics — integrated laboratory networks, quality assurance and diagnostic capacity across all levels of care.
(d) Resilient supply chains — stronger forecasting, procurement and distribution to ensure medicines, diagnostics and commodities are available when needed.
(e) Stronger primary health care — investing in the facilities, referral systems, infrastructure and management capacity that form the backbone of service delivery.
(f) Stronger community systems — recognising communities and civil society as partners in access, accountability, demand creation and service delivery.
(g) Sustainable health financing — using Global Fund resources strategically while strengthening domestic financing and long-term sustainability.
(h) Better governance and value for money — clear ownership, strong accountability, measurable results and efficient use of every resource.
These priorities are interconnected. Health workers need medicines. Medicines need resilient supply chains. Services need reliable data. Communities need accessible primary health care. And all of these require strong governance and sustainable financing.
This is why RSSH should be more than a collection of activities competing for funding.
It should be about building capabilities.
Capabilities that remain when a grant ends.
Capabilities that strengthen HIV, TB and malaria responses.
Capabilities that improve the wider health system.
Capabilities that strengthen national ownership and sustainability.
For GC8, perhaps the ambition should be simple:
Fund less in isolation. Build more in systems.
Focus less on activities. Focus more on capabilities.
Move from short-term outputs to sustainable results.
Think beyond the grant. Build for the future.
The opportunity before Malawi is to ensure that GC8 does more than finance the next grant period,it helps leave behind a stronger, more resilient and more sustainable health system for the next generation.

 The Draper Richards Kaplan Foundation (DRK Foundation) funds early-stage social enterprises worldwide — including those in Africa — offering up to US $300,000 in grant or investment capital, combined with capacity-building, mentoring, and community support. DRK’s goal is to help high-impact ventures scale sustainably and transform lives in underserved communities. 
⏳ Urgency / Timeline
Applications accepted on a rolling, year-round basis — apply anytime. 
💡 Why It Matters
Many impactful social ventures struggle to scale after pilot success because they lack long-term funding, mentorship, or business support. DRK closes that gap — offering not just money, but also hands-on support, governance, financial strategy, and network access so these ventures can grow, sustain, and maximize social impact in vulnerable communities. 
🎯 Grant Snapshot
Funding Amount: Up to US $300,000 over up to 3 years 
Focus: Social enterprises tackling urgent social or environmental challenges using scalable, impact-first approaches 
Geography: Africa, Europe, India, USA (with some flexibility for select other regions) 
Stage: Post-pilot, early stage (pre-scale or seed to early growth) 
✅ Who Should Apply
Nonprofits, mission-driven for-profit ventures, or fiscally-sponsored social enterprises
Organizations with a viable product/service and early evidence of impact
Founders or leadership teams committed full-time, with clear vision for scale
Ventures with a plan for financial sustainability (earned income or other models) 
🧰 What Awardees Will Receive
Up to US $300,000 in flexible capital (grant or investment) 
Hands-on operational support: board-level mentoring, organizational development, fundraising strategy, leadership & governance support 
Access to a global network of social impact funders, peer entrepreneurs, and alumni community 
Strategic support to scale and sustain impact over 3-year partnership period 
📝 How to Apply
Visit the DRK Foundation portal and submit an online application (executive summary or pitch deck/business plan + basic organizational info). Applications can be saved and resumed later. 
✅ For Support…
Comment “SUPPORT” if you’d like help preparing your application.
