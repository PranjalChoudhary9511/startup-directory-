=== 9 personal-life | The private man | A Soft Voice, a Long Shadow
0 [p] Larry Page is one of the most private people in this series. He almost never talks about his family, so this chapter is short on purpose. We report only what is on the public record and what shaped his work, and we leave his children out of it.  [203,180]
1 [h] Lucy  
2 [p] Page married Lucinda "Lucy" Southworth in December 2007. Reuters described her as a doctoral student in biomedical informatics at Stanford, with a degree from the University of Pennsylvania and a master's from Oxford. They wed on Necker Island, Richard Branson's Caribbean retreat, and reports named Branson as best man. Up to 600 guests were expected, and Google declined to confirm any of it.  [204,205,206]
3 [p] She is a serious figure in her own right. TechCrunch reported in 2022 that Southworth, a research geneticist, founded and directs Oceankind, which had by then spent more than ==$121 million== on marine science, ocean life and climate work. That made it one of the biggest non-government funders of ocean science in the world. Even there, the money is hard to trace, because Oceankind is a company and not a public charity.  [207]
4 [note] She runs her own mission, at serious scale.  
5 [h] The trip to India  
6 [p] The most moving thing he has said in public came in that 2009 Michigan speech, with an India connection most Indian readers have never heard. He and Lucy were walking through narrow streets in a very hot, very poor village in rural India. Waste ran through an open gutter into the river. They met a boy whose leg was paralysed by polio.  [4]
7 [p] His mind went to his father. Carl Page caught polio as a first-grader, and it left him with lifelong breathing problems. In late March 1996, soon after Larry moved to Stanford, his father had trouble breathing and drove himself to hospital.  [4]
8 [p] He died two months later. Larry was 23, and he told the students he was completely devastated. That day in Michigan he said the world was close to ending polio, with 328 cases so far that year, and asked the graduates to finish the job.  [4]
9 [note] Google's founder, in an Indian village, thinking about his dad.  
10 [h] The voice  
11 [p] Listen to any recent clip of Page and you will notice the voice: thin, raspy, sometimes almost gone. He explained why in a Google+ post in May 2013, at age 40. About 14 years earlier, a bad cold had left him hoarse, and his voice never fully came back.  [208]
12 [p] Doctors found paralysis in his left vocal cord. In 2012 another cold affected the right one too. Doctors called the combination extremely rare. They could not find the cause.  [208]

14 [p] Look at the choice he made. Investors had just lived through Apple's secrecy about Steve Jobs's health, and an analyst said shareholders deserved to know. Page told them, and then he turned a private problem into a research project. He asked other patients with similar symptoms for data and paid for science. He also made a joke, saying his friends still found he had “way more stamina than them when we go kitesurfing!”  [208,209]
15 [quote] Sergey says I'm probably a better CEO because I choose my words more carefully — Larry Page, Google+ post, 2013, quoted by Bloomberg Businessweek [180]
16 [p] The cost was real, though. Two former Google vice presidents told Bloomberg Businessweek that the pace of the CEO job weighed on his health. The Verge's history of his career marks this as the point when he began to cut back drastically on speaking in public. Whatever else we say about his silence in later chapters, remember this: talking is physically harder for him than it is for you.  [180,210]
17 [note] Before you judge a quiet founder, remember what talking costs him.  
18 [h] His giving: billions in, little visible out  
19 [p] Page set up the Carl Victor Page Memorial Foundation, named after his father, in 2006. It is huge. By a 2023 count it held ==$6.7 billion== in assets. Yet it has no staff, and in 2023 Inside Philanthropy found that 97.8% of the more than $600 million it granted between 2017 and 2021 went into donor-advised funds. These are accounts at charity sponsors, where money can sit and where grants do not have to be publicly disclosed.  [211,212]
20 [p] Critics are blunt. Nonprofit Quarterly called it a violation of the spirit of the rule that foundations must spend 5% of their assets a year. It also pointed to the foundation's tax filing, which showed Page spending an average of 15 minutes a week on foundation work. Forbes gives him a philanthropy score of 1, its lowest, meaning less than 1% of wealth given away, based on about $7 million in grants it could trace.  [199,213,3]
21 [p] To be fair, Forbes itself warned that the score could be too low: if those funds are making grants, his real giving could be far higher, and nobody outside can tell. The Chronicle of Philanthropy notes that foundations have legitimate reasons for using such funds, including donor privacy. Page did not answer Forbes's request for comment. __So we can't say he gives little. We can only say he does not show it.__  [213,214]
22 [example] For example: the visible gifts are mostly about germs. He pledged $15 million to fight Ebola in 2014. After seeing data from Google's flu tracker, he paid for free flu shots for children across the Bay Area. A program called Shoo the Flu has offered free shots to schoolchildren around Oakland, and a company called Flu Lab funds work on a universal flu vaccine.  [211,19,215]
23 [note] Polio, flu, Ebola. Notice the pattern. His dad caught a virus.  
24 [h] How he spends and what he plays at  
25 [p] His best-known hobby is kitesurfing, often with Branson. His spending runs to the very large but quiet. In 2011 The Independent described his yacht, Senses, at 193 feet, with an interior by Philippe Starck, and noted that he bought it second-hand. Bloomberg Businessweek reported he has a private Caribbean island. Around the turn of 2026 he paid about ==$173.4 million== for two waterfront homes in Miami.  [208,203,180,10]
26 [p] Mostly he seems to spend on the future he wants. He has personally funded companies working on flying vehicles, a dream that goes back to his student plan for a personal transit system at Michigan. In that 2009 speech he said a dream like that never really goes away. It just incubates as a hobby.  [180,4]
=== 10 the-brand | His public image | Eight Hours a Year
0 [p] Most founders build a personal brand. Page built the opposite. In 2008, according to the author Ken Auletta, he told Google's PR team they could have ==eight hours== of his time for the whole year, for press conferences, speeches and interviews combined. Auletta described him as openly contemptuous of the whole trade.  [203]
1 [quote] aggressively disdainful of public relations and marketing — Ken Auletta on Larry Page, quoted in The Independent [203]
2 [p] There may have been a strategy in it too. One Page loyalist told Bloomberg Businessweek that keeping him out of view was partly deliberate. Google had watched Bill Gates become a cartoon villain during Microsoft's antitrust fight, and did not want Page to become the same kind of mascot and punching bag. __The product became the brand. The man stayed out of the picture.__  [180]
3 [h] The Playboy problem  
4 [p] His biggest brush with old-style publicity was a near disaster. In April 2004, just before Google filed for its stock market debut, he and Brin gave an interview to Playboy magazine. Companies going public are meant to stay quiet, so that nobody can be accused of hyping the shares. The interview appeared in the September issue, just as the share auction opened.  [39,131]
5 [p] Regulators could have delayed the whole deal. Instead, the Securities and Exchange Commission required disclosures, and Google printed the entire Playboy article inside its official prospectus, as Appendix B. A securities lawyer called this unprecedented. Google also warned investors that it might have to buy back shares if a court found a violation, and it corrected facts in the piece, such as its staff numbers. The IPO still went ahead at $85 a share.  [39,216,131]
6 [note] Google's IPO document contains a Playboy interview. Look it up. It's real.  
7 [h] Written, not spoken  
8 [p] His best communication has always been on paper. The 2004 founders' letter in the prospectus told Wall Street to its face that Google would not behave like other companies. That line became a kind of flag. Eleven years later he repeated it word for word when announcing Alphabet, in a blog post that even joked about the new name.  [18,11]
9 [quote] Google is not a conventional company. We do not intend to become one. — Larry Page, 2004 founders' letter, repeated in 2015 [11]
10 [p] His exit letter with Brin in 2019 used the same warm, slightly goofy voice. It compared Google to a 21-year-old leaving home, and said the founders would become proud parents.  [12]
11 [quote] offering advice and love, but not daily nagging! — Larry Page and Sergey Brin, December 2019 [12]
12 [p] Out loud it is a different story. Fortune described rambling sentences in a raspy monotone, and counted nearly two dozen uses of the word "excited" or similar in one hour with him. Before his 2014 TED talk, Business Insider reported, he and two senior staff spent a day in a hotel room preparing.  [181,14,217]
13 [p] On stage he showed an AI teaching itself a video game, and the audience had little idea what he was getting at. Nicholas Carlson's verdict was blunt: the ideas are good, but they get lost in sentences he doesn't finish. Fortune's reporter saw it differently, and came away with a picture of a confident, in-control chief.  [181,14,217]
14 [note] He couldn't pitch like Steve Jobs. He still built Google.

18 [p] He also complained about press stories setting Google against rivals. The Verge joked that he wanted a beta-test country. Slate mocked the whole thing. It was the honest Page, unfiltered, and few CEOs would have dared.  [219,218]

20 [h] Then he went silent  
21 [p] In October 2013 he told investors he would stop joining every earnings call, because they needed him to “ruthlessly prioritize my time”. He did not come back. By 2018 he had not presented at a product launch or on an earnings call since 2013, or done press since 2015. In his place, Google's public faces were Schmidt and then Pichai.  [220,180]
22 [h] How the world sees him  
23 [p] In America the image shifted over time, from cheerful futurist to absent landlord. After the 2018 hearing, pundits called him unpatriotic, and a former colleague told Bloomberg there was no strong central voice on society's problems. Yet to engineers he remains a hero. Ben Horowitz compared the breadth of his ambition to Thomas Edison's. In Europe, Fortune noted in 2014, the criticism of Google's power was especially sharp, and European regulators have since issued some of its biggest fines.  [180,181,221]
24 [p] In India most people meet Larry Page through someone else. He is the man who named Chennai-born Sundar Pichai as Google's CEO in 2015. The Hindu ran his whole announcement. In it, he praised Pichai warmly.  [222,11,183,223]
25 [p] When Pichai went back to IIT Kharagpur in 2017, more than 3,500 people waited for him, and most hands went up when the students were asked who wanted to work at Google. This next line is our reading, not a survey: __in India, Page's reputation is built on who he promoted.__  [222,11,183,223]
26 [quote] Sundar has been saying the things I would have said (and sometimes better!) — Larry Page, announcing Pichai as Google CEO, August 2015 [222]
27 [p] There are also harder views of Google in India. By late 2015 India was sending Google its second-highest search traffic after the United States, and Pichai was calling it a big bet. In 2022 the Competition Commission of India fined Google ==₹1,337.76 crore== over its Android rules, which we cover in the next chapter.  [184,224,225]
28 [note] He promoted an IIT boy to run Google. Remember whom you promote.  
=== 11 what-went-wrong | The bad calls | What Not to Copy
0 [p] Larry Page was right about the big things so often that it is easy to skip his mistakes. Don't. Some were honest bets that failed, some cost other people dearly, and a few ended up in front of judges. We have tried to say which is which. Remember too that the rulings below are against Google, not against him personally, and that Google has often been run by other people.  [221,0]
1 [h] 1. Betting everyone's bonus on Google+  
2 [p] As he took over as CEO in April 2011, Page sent a companywide memo tying staff bonuses to Google's social strategy. A leaked internal FAQ described a multiplier from 0.75 to 1.25, so bonuses could be 25% higher or 25% lower depending on how the whole effort went. People who had nothing to do with social products were told to test them and push them on family and friends.  [69,70]
3 [p] Google+ launched that June. Under Vic Gundotra, it shipped features at record speed and Google proudly reported over 40 million users. But nobody really stayed. In 2018 Google itself admitted that ==90%== of Google+ sessions lasted less than five seconds.  [186,197,226]
4 [p] Bugs had also exposed the private data of up to 52.5 million users to app developers, though Google said it saw no evidence of misuse. It shut the consumer service in April 2019.  [186,197,226]
5 [note] You can't pay people to love a product. Read this twice.  
6 [h] 2. The $12.5 billion phone company  
7 [p] Levy called the Motorola purchase the boldest move of his tenure. Google paid ==$12.5 billion== in 2012, mostly to get patents that would protect Android from lawsuits. Less than two years later, Page sent a memo announcing the sale of the phone business to Lenovo for ==$2.91 billion==. His reason was that the smartphone market was super competitive and you had to be all in to thrive.  [19,14,227,228]
8 [p] Do not read that as a simple $9.6 billion loss, because Google kept most of the patents, which was the main point of the deal. Still, Page's own memo argued that Motorola would do better under a company fully committed to making phones. The lesson is about clarity: know exactly which piece of a deal you actually want.  [227,228]
9 [note] Buying patents and running a phone factory are different jobs.  
10 [h] 3. The moon shots that crashed  
11 [p] Failure was part of his method, and these were honest bets. Some were still expensive. Google Glass went on sale at $1,500 and got banned from some bars and restaurants; sales stopped in January 2015. Loon closed in 2021.  [229,29,230,180]
12 [p] Google Fiber was reported to be costing at least $1 billion per market, and in 2016 Page reportedly ordered its staff cut in half. Bloomberg found a project called Heliox, a giant plastic tube meant to blow cyclists through the air on a mix of oxygen and helium. It fizzled.  [229,29,230,180]
13 [note] An honest failed bet is not the same as a scandal.  
14 [p] Investors eventually pushed back, and Bloomberg reported that almost all of Alphabet's spending was going to Google by 2018. __Google could afford all this because search paid for it. You cannot.__ If you have no search business behind you, make the same kind of bet at a smaller scale, with a cheap test first, the way he did with the metronome.  [180]
15 [h] 4. The pharmacy ads: $500 million  
16 [p] Here is a case that many young founders have never heard of. In August 2011 Google agreed to forfeit ==$500 million==, one of the largest forfeitures in US history, over ads for Canadian online pharmacies aimed at American customers. The Justice Department said Google knew from about 2003 that shipping these drugs into the US was usually illegal, and did not change course until it learned of the investigation in 2009. Google admitted improperly helping those advertisers.  [231,232]
17 [quote] Larry Page knew what was going on — Peter Neronha, US Attorney for Rhode Island, to The Wall Street Journal, 2011 [232]
18 [p] Be careful with that quote. It is a prosecutor's claim. The documents behind it were never made public because Google signed a non-prosecution agreement, so the claim was never tested in court.  [232,233,234,235]
19 [p] Neronha said he had no plans to charge Page or other executives, though he said they were not off limits. Wired reported that Google denied the allegation, according to press accounts. Shareholders also sued over the money; we could not confirm how those suits ended, so we leave that open.  [232,233,234,235]
20 [quote] it's obvious with hindsight that we shouldn't have allowed these ads — Google statement, August 2011 [231]
21 [h] 5. The Andy Rubin payout  
22 [p] This is the one that hurt his own people most. In October 2018 The New York Times reported that Google had investigated a claim of sexual misconduct against Android's creator, Andy Rubin, in 2014 and found it credible. According to executives who spoke to the paper, Page asked for his resignation. Rubin denied the accusation.  [147]
23 [p] What came next caused the fury. Rubin reportedly left with ==$90 million== paid in instalments, a loan deal and a warm public goodbye. Weeks before, the board had approved a $150 million stock grant for him. The Times said Page usually recommended senior pay, and that it was unclear whether he or the board knew about the inquiry when that grant was approved. Page did not respond to the paper.  [147]
24 [quote] With Android he created something truly remarkable — Larry Page's public farewell to Andy Rubin, 2014 [147]
25 [p] At the staff meeting that week, the top-voted question to Page and Pichai asked what real action would follow, and said the company seemed to value powerful abusers over their victims. Pichai told staff that 48 people had been fired for sexual harassment in two years with no exit package. Then more than ==20,000== employees walked out. Shareholders sued, alleging that the board and Page knew of the claims when they approved his pay. In 2020 Alphabet settled by pledging $310 million over ten years to diversity and anti-harassment work, ending forced arbitration in such cases and limiting non-disclosure agreements.  [236,147,237,238,239]
26 [note] Rule: how you treat a star when it's hard is your real culture.  
27 [h] 6. The empty chair  
28 [p] On 5 September 2018 the Senate Intelligence Committee met about foreign manipulation of social platforms. Jack Dorsey of Twitter and Sheryl Sandberg of Facebook sat at the table. Next to them was a black leather chair with a place card marked "Google" and a blank notepad. Page had been called and did not come, and neither did Pichai. Alphabet said it had offered its head of global affairs, and that freeing Larry to focus on long-term technical problems was what Alphabet was set up for.  [180,240]
29 [p] Senators called it arrogant and an outrage. Mark Warner asked what the company had to hide. An early Google investor, Roger McNamee, called it a basic failure of corporate governance.  [180]
30 [p] Bloomberg's report also noted that his health might have played a part in his retreat, without saying it was the reason for that day. Either way, __an empty chair becomes the only story when you are the one missing.__ Your critics will happily speak for you.  [180]
31 [h] 7. The monopoly rulings  
32 [p] By far the largest bill came from Google's dominance itself. Page co-founded a company so strong that courts and regulators on three continents found it broke competition law. Page once told Levy he could not think of a company that had failed because of litigation, only because of bad choices or lack of ambition. He may yet be right. But look at the list.  [19]

35 [p] In fairness, his habit of charging ahead sometimes won in court as well. Authors sued over scanning books, and in 2015 an appeals court found Google's project was fair use; the Supreme Court declined to hear the case in 2016.  [246,247,47]
36 [p] Oracle sued over Android's use of Java code, and in 2021 the Supreme Court ruled 6–2 for Google. So do not take the easy lesson that he was simply reckless. __Sometimes the rule-breaker gets vindicated. You will not know which you are until the bills arrive.__  [246,247,47]
37 [h] What a young founder should not copy  
38 [example] For example, do not copy these: (1) Firing people in public with no warning. (2) Paying your team to pretend a product is working. (3) Going quiet just when your company most needs a voice. (4) Treating every critic as wrong. Page told Levy much of the anger over his book project was phony, and Levy wrote that this dismissive streak hurt Google's reputation. (5) Letting a star's value decide how you handle misconduct.  [14,69,180,18,147]
=== 12 today | As of November 2026 | The Quietest Giant
0 [p] As of 2 November 2026, Larry Page is 53. He has not run anything at Google for almost seven years. And he still holds more of its votes than anyone else. He is a director of Alphabet and chairs the board's Executive Committee, though that committee held no meetings in 2025.  [0,12]
1 [p] Sundar Pichai is the CEO. Page stepped back with Brin in December 2019, promising to stay on as directors and shareholders and to keep talking with Pichai.  [0,12]

3 [p] Those votes matter. In 2026 a shareholder proposal asked for equal voting rights, and the board recommended against it. All ten directors, Page included, were re-elected. Each of his shares carries ten votes, against one for an ordinary Class A share. __That is how a man who barely appears in public can still help steer a company worth trillions.__  [0,248,176]
4 [h] A $68 billion wobble  
5 [p] Here is how strange it is to be this rich. At the end of April 2026, on strong AI and cloud results, Forbes put Page above ==$300 billion== for the first time, at No. 2 in the world. Alphabet was worth about $4.5 trillion.  [176,249,250]
6 [p] By mid-October Forbes had him around $288 billion. Then on 29 October, Alphabet's shares fell more than 12% after third-quarter revenue missed expectations and it spent $51.4 billion in a single quarter, mostly on AI. Page lost about ==$31 billion== in a morning.  [176,249,250]
7 [note] One share price moves his whole fortune.  
8 [p] Forbes called it Alphabet's worst dollar-loss day and the second-worst one-day wipeout in market history. And yet he climbed to No. 3 the next day, because Dell's shares fell harder. Elon Musk, his old friend, led the list at about $869 billion on 1 November. These numbers swing with one share price, so treat them as a snapshot.  [171,251]
9 [note] $31 billion gone in one morning. He was still No. 3.  
10 [h] His AI moment, from the sidelines  
11 [p] He saw this coming long before most. Levy described him in 2011 as wanting Google to be a learning machine fed by massive data. Bloomberg credited his tenure with prescient investments in AI. In 2014 he stood on the TED stage showing an AI teach itself a video game, and the crowd did not quite get it. Google bought DeepMind that same year.  [18,180,14,201]
12 [p] Then came ChatGPT. In December 2022, at Pichai's invitation, The New York Times reported, Page and Brin came back for several meetings about Google's AI plans. They reviewed strategy, approved plans and pitched ideas for putting chatbot features into search. They had not spent much time at Google since 2019. Beyond that, we have not found any reliable public account of how much Page personally does at Google today.  [152]
13 [h] Current bets: making physical things  
14 [p] His own money has gone where his head has always been: transport and building things. He backed the flying-car company Kittyhawk, started as Zee.Aero in 2010. It showed off a one-person Flyer in 2018, and by 2020 it was dropping that programme. It wound down in September 2022, though its partnership with Boeing, Wisk, carried on.  [252]
15 [p] As Kittyhawk was closing, its chief technology officer, Chris Anderson, gathered a few staff to try something new. That became Dynatomics, a startup Business Insider says is exploring AI and 3D printing to build aircraft, drawing on filings. Page had already been pushing Kittyhawk's engineers to try 3D printing to cut costs. The Information reported, as carried by The Indian Express, that it aims to use AI for highly optimised designs that factories can make. He has not spoken about any of it in public.  [253,254]
16 [example] For example: think how small this looks. A man worth more than $200 billion, backing a small team that is working out how to design and print parts. It is the same habit you saw with the metronome. Find the cheap way to test a big idea.  [253,254]
17 [h] Goodbye, California  
18 [p] The biggest thing he has done lately was about tax. California's Proposition 40 would charge billionaires a one-time 5% tax if they lived in the state on 1 January 2026. In late December 2025, his family office, Koop, and other linked entities, including Flu Lab and Oceankind, were moved out of California and incorporated in Delaware. He also bought those Miami homes. Fortune's estimate, at his October net worth, is that he would have owed nearly ==$15 billion== if he had stayed.  [255,10]
19 [p] His representatives told Forbes he is now a Florida resident, and Forbes's profile lists Miami. But Forbes also could not find updated voter registration or a declaration of domicile, and his spokespeople declined to provide proof. As of mid-October he was still not registered to vote in Florida. Courts may yet test the residency date. California voters decide on 3 November 2026, the day after this chapter was checked, so look up the result.  [249,256,3,255]
20 [note] Fair or not, he moved before the deadline. Decide what you think.  
21 [p] Silicon Valley split over it. Congressman Ro Khanna responded sarcastically that he would miss them. Palmer Luckey warned that founders would have to sell parts of their companies to pay.  [10,255,256]
22 [p] Jensen Huang said he was perfectly fine with the tax and would stay. Page has not made his case in public. Sergey Brin put more than $100 million into opposing the measure; we could not confirm any spending by Page himself.  [10,255,256]
23 [note] Smart people disagree here. Find the strongest version of each side.  
24 [h] What he says he wants next  
25 [p] Honest answer: he has not told us. There is no recent interview. So the best guide is the last thing he said on the record about what drives him. In 2014 he told Fortune he wanted to build a company that could stay on top for generations, and said Google's mission had become too narrow.  [181,192,12]
26 [p] He said then that the world was spending too little on important problems like self-driving cars. When he left in 2019, the first bet his letter pointed to was Waymo.  [181,192,12]
27 [p] As a young man he was struck by how Nikola Tesla ended up short of resources. He got everything Tesla lacked: money, control, time. He now runs almost nothing, yet holds more votes than anyone at one of the world's largest companies. __Whether that silence is wisdom or absence is the last open question about Larry Page__, and so far he has left it unanswered.  [18,0,176]
28 [note] Write your own answer in this margin. What would you do with his freedom?
