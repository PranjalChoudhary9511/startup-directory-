=== 0 origins | Ages 0–12 | The Professor's Messy House
0 [h] A hammer in the family  
1 [p] The Larry Page story does not begin in Silicon Valley. It begins in Flint, Michigan, a car-factory town. His father's father worked on the assembly line at the Chevy plant there. He was not an engineer or a professor. He built cars with his hands.  [4]
2 [p] Grandpa Page owned a strange tool. Larry called it an “Alley Oop” hammer: a heavy iron pipe with a lump of lead melted onto one end. Workers made these during the sit-down strikes, to protect themselves. Years later, when Larry was a boy, the family used the same hammer to bang stakes into the ground in the yard.  [4]
3 [p] In 2009 Larry gave the graduation speech at the University of Michigan, and he brought the hammer to the stadium. He joked that most people no longer need a blunt weapon for safety, “but just in case, I have it here.” The co-founder of Google wanted a stadium full of graduates to see exactly where his family came from.  [4]
4 [photo] https://commons.wikimedia.org/wiki/File:Flint_Sit-Down_Strike_window.jpg | Strikers at the Fisher Body Plant No. 3 during the 1937 Flint sit-down strike. Larry Page says autoworkers made homemade “Alley Oop” hammers during the sit-down strikes. His grandfather kept one. | Sheldon Dick | Public domain
5 [p] That grandfather had one big rule. He drove his two children to Ann Arbor, home of the University of Michigan, and told them where their future was. Both of them did graduate from Michigan. Larry called it “the American dream.”  [4]
6 [quote] That is where you're going to go to college. — Larry Page's grandfather, an autoworker, to his two children (as told by Larry Page, 2009) [4]
7 [note] Factory floor, then professor, then founder. Three generations, one family.  
8 [h] The boy who caught polio  
9 [p] One of those two children was Carl Victor Page, Larry's father. In first grade Carl went on a trip to Tennessee and caught polio. He spent two months in hospital and was flown home on a military DC-3 plane. It was his first flight.  [4]
10 [quote] Then, I had to stay in bed for over a year, before I started back to school — Carl Victor Page, in his fifth-grade autobiography, quoted by his son Larry [4]
11 [p] Polio left Carl with breathing problems for the rest of his life. It did not slow his mind. In ==1956== he was valedictorian, the top student, of Flint Mandeville High School, in a class of about 90 kids. In his graduation speech he said the world was entering an age of automation, where education would be an economic necessity.  [4]
12 [p] Decades later Larry found that old speech and said it blew him away. A teenager in a car town, in 1956, was already talking about machines changing work. __Larry grew up in a house where the future was normal dinner-table talk.__  [4]
13 [note] A 1956 school speech predicted automation. His son built Google.  
14 [h] Kitchen chemistry  
15 [p] In the cold September of 1962, at a student co-op house at the University of Michigan, a student named Gloria climbed a ladder to scrub a filthy kitchen ceiling. A young boarder named Carl stood below, admiring the view. That is how Larry's parents met. He later joked that he was a direct result of that “kitchen chemistry experiment.”  [4]
16 [p] The early years were not rich. While Carl studied, the couple argued at times over pennies, and they were raising a newborn son, Larry's older brother, Carl Jr. Gloria typed Carl's whole dissertation by hand. His PhD, earned about ==44 years before 2009==, was officially in “Communication Science.” Larry says that was because the university thought computers were just a passing fad.  [4]

18 [p] Carl became one of the early computer scientists in America. Moment magazine reported that he received one of the first PhDs awarded in the field. He became a professor of computer science and artificial intelligence at Michigan State University. Gloria earned a master's degree in computer science and taught college programming classes.  [20,5]
19 [p] Gloria was raised Jewish, and her father later moved to Israel, where he worked as a tool and die maker in the desert town of Arad, near the Dead Sea. But Larry was brought up more in his father's mould. Moment magazine described Carl's religion as technology.  [20]
20 [h] A university brat  
21 [p] Lawrence Edward Page was born on ==26 March 1973== in Lansing, Michigan. He grew up in nearby East Lansing, where his father taught at Michigan State. A professor's timetable is flexible, and Larry says his dad spent “oodles of time” raising him.  [5,18,4]
22 [quote] Could there be a better upbringing than university brat? — Larry Page, University of Michigan commencement, 2009 [4]
23 [p] The Page house was messy. Computers and magazines were lying everywhere, and Larry read whatever was around, including Popular Science. In a 2013 letter to Google shareholders he remembered spending a huge amount of time poring over books and magazines as a kid.  [5,22]
24 [note] What lies around your room is teaching you. Choose it carefully.  
25 [h] Six years old, with a computer  
26 [p] Larry first met computers at about age six. This was the late 1970s, when almost no family owned one. In a 2000 interview he said his father was willing to spend all his available income on buying a computer. He said very few people his age had that experience.  [5]
27 [example] For example: Larry believes he was the first kid in his elementary school to hand in a word-processed assignment. Picture a classroom of handwritten homework, and one small boy turning in a printout.  [5]
28 [p] In the early grades he went to a Montessori school. In Montessori classrooms, children choose their own activities instead of waiting for the teacher's orders. Years later, Google executive Marissa Mayer said that this kind of schooling explained a lot about how Larry and his co-founder Sergey Brin questioned everything.  [5,18]
29 [quote] You can't understand Google unless you know that both Larry and Sergey were Montessori kids. — Marissa Mayer, Google vice president, to Wired (2011) [18]
30 [p] His older brother was his first engineering teacher. Carl Jr. showed him how to take things apart, and Larry took apart everything in the house. He also had lots of ideas about what could be built and how. Before long he had built an electric go-kart.  [5]
31 [quote] My brother taught me how to take things apart, and I took apart everything in the house. — Larry Page, Academy of Achievement interview, 2000 [5]
32 [note] First he broke things open. Then he built things. Same curiosity.  
33 [h] Twelve, and crying over Tesla  
34 [p] In ==1985==, when Larry was 12, he finished reading a biography of Nikola Tesla and cried. Tesla was a genius who helped invent the way most of the world's electricity is made. Yet he died alone and in debt in a New York hotel room in 1943, unable to find investors for his ideas.  [14]
35 [p] Larry took a hard lesson from that sad ending. Inventing alone was not enough. In his words, you really had to get inventions out into the world and have people use them, or they had no effect. So the boy decided he would need business skills as well as engineering skills.  [5,14]
36 [quote] So probably from when I was 12, I knew I was going to start a company eventually. — Larry Page, Academy of Achievement interview, 2000 [5]
37 [p] Think about how early that is. Most 12-year-olds want to be inventors, astronauts or cricket stars. Larry wanted to be an inventor and also the person who sells the invention. __An idea that never reaches people is just a sad story, however brilliant it is.__  [5,14]
38 [note] Read this twice. Most smart people never learn this lesson.

=== 1 growing-up | Ages 13–18 | The Quiet Kid Who Kept Time
0 [h] The years he rarely talks about  
1 [p] Here is an honest warning. Larry Page has said much less in public about his teenage years than about Google. What exists on record is a small set of details from his own interviews and from careful reporters. We could not find any reliable record of a teenage business, a first deal, or a bullying story, and we will not invent one.  [5,18,14]
2 [note] No made-up hustle stories here. Only what's actually on record.  
3 [p] What we do know: he went to East Lansing High School, in the college town where his father taught. He graduated from there before leaving for the University of Michigan in Ann Arbor, the school his grandfather had chosen for the family years earlier.  [5,4]
4 [photo] https://commons.wikimedia.org/wiki/File:East_Lansing_High_School.jpg | East Lansing High School in Michigan, where Larry Page went to school (photographed in 2015). | kennethaw88 | CC BY 4.0
5 [h] Saxophone and the clock in his head  
6 [p] Here is a detail most people never hear. Growing up in Michigan, Larry played the saxophone and studied music composition. That sounds like a hobby with nothing to do with search engines. Larry disagrees.  [15]
7 [quote] In music you're very cognizant of time. Time is like the primary thing. — Larry Page, to Fortune (2014) [15]
8 [p] In a 2014 Fortune interview he explained it with drums. If you are a percussionist and you hit something, the sound has to come in milliseconds, a fraction of a second. He said that in some sense, music training led to “the high-speed legacy of Google” for him.  [15]
9 [p] He also complained that software developers get lazy and accept apps that take a while to respond. “But it's really not okay,” he said. People process information very quickly, so a slow phone is a huge problem. __The teenager who counted beats grew into a CEO who counted milliseconds.__  [15]
10 [example] For example: at early Google, Business Insider reports, if an app loaded slowly during a product demo, Larry would start counting out loud. “One one-thousand.” “Two one-thousand.” The sense of timing from music class had become a management habit.  [14]
11 [note] Speed is a feature. He learned that from a saxophone.

13 [h] Not a social animal  
14 [p] Larry was not the loud kid in class. Steven Levy, who spent years reporting on Google, wrote that Page was not a social animal and could unnerve people just by not talking. Business Insider's Nicholas Carlson put it more simply: he had not been a social child.  [18,14]
15 [p] But when he did speak, Levy wrote, he often came out with ideas that bordered on the fantastic. Later, in college and graduate school, Larry found his way to connect with people. He bonded with people over big ideas: visions of the future and cool technology.  [18,14]
16 [note] Shy is fine. Find the people who share your obsession.  
17 [h] A head full of ideas  
18 [p] His teenage mind was fed by that messy house. “I just sort of kept having ideas,” he said in 2000. He read Popular Science and wondered how devices worked. He kept thinking about what things could be built and how to build them.  [5]
19 [p] His goal was clear. He told Wired he wanted to be an inventor, not just for the gadgets, but because he really wanted to change the world. Inventing was the dream. Business was the vehicle that would carry the dream to people.  [18,19]
20 [quote] When I was growing up, I wanted to be an inventor. — Larry Page, to Wired (2013) [19]
21 [p] The Tesla story he had read at 12 stayed with him through these years. He later told Wired that Tesla could have achieved much more with more resources, and had trouble commercialising his work. He said it was a good lesson: he did not want to just invent things, he also wanted to make the world better.  [18]
22 [p] He said it even more bluntly to Fortune in 2008. Tesla was one of the greatest inventors, he said, but he could not commercialise anything and could barely fund his own research. Larry said you would want to be more like Thomas Edison, who got inventions into the world and earned money to pay for the next project.  [16]
23 [quote] You don't want to be Tesla. He was one of the greatest inventors, but it's a sad, sad story. — Larry Page, to Fortune (2008) [16]
24 [note] Inventor plus business brain. Most people pick one. He wanted both.  
25 [h] The older-brother effect  
26 [p] Larry was not the only tech-obsessed child in the house. His brother, Carl Jr., was years older and was the one who taught him to take things apart. Larry said his older brother was interested in computers as well, which made their home unusual for that time.  [5,4]
27 [p] Carl Jr. later became a successful internet entrepreneur himself. So by the time Larry was a young man, he had a living example under his own roof. A kid from that messy East Lansing house could build a real technology company.  [5]
28 [example] For example: think about who your “older brother” is. It could be a cousin, a senior at school, or a founder you email. Seeing someone close to you do it makes the impossible feel ordinary.  [5]
29 [h] The family rule: Michigan  
30 [p] When it was time for university, there was not much debate. Everyone in the family went to Michigan: Larry, his brother, his mother and his father. His father alone collected three and a half degrees there.  [4]
31 [p] So the teenager who left for Ann Arbor carried a strange kit of tools. He had a musician's feel for time, and a habit of taking machines apart. Above all, he had a lesson from a dead inventor: __build it, then get it to people, or it does not count__.  [4,5,15]
32 [lesson] Larry's saxophone, his magazines and his broken gadgets looked like random hobbies. Years later they became Google's obsession with speed, its love of engineering and his drive to commercialise. Follow what you are curious about now, and pay attention to what it teaches you.  
=== 2 first-steps | Ages 18–25 | Lego, a Dream and a Garage
0 [h] Ann Arbor: the engineer who wanted a monorail  
1 [p] At the University of Michigan, Larry studied engineering with a focus on computer engineering and graduated with honours. Google's own profile of him calls it following in his father's footsteps in academics. He finished in ==1995==.  [5,21,19]
2 [p] He did not just attend lectures. He built things. One idea was that inkjet cartridges could print giant posters cheaply. To test it, he built a working inkjet printer out of Lego bricks.  [5,21]
3 [quote] in college I built an inkjet printer out of Legos. — Larry Page, Academy of Achievement interview, 2000 [5]
4 [example] For example: a student with no budget for big machines used a children's toy to test an idea that printer companies spent millions on. You do not need a lab to start. You need a cheap way to try.  [5,21]
5 [p] He also joined Michigan's solar car team. The team designed a solar-powered car that raced in the ==1993 World Solar Challenge==. Wired later linked it to his long-running love of electric cars.  [19,5]
6 [photo] https://commons.wikimedia.org/wiki/File:Maize_and_Blue_solar_car,_University_of_Michigan,_1993_-_Museum_of_Science_and_Industry_(Chicago)_-_DSC06862.JPG | The University of Michigan's 1993 “Maize & Blue” solar car, on show at the Museum of Science and Industry in Chicago. Larry Page was on Michigan's solar car team. | Daderot | CC0
7 [p] The most important thing he picked up at Michigan came from a summer camp turned training programme called LeaderShape. Its slogan stuck with him for life. It pushed him to chase a crazy idea: a personal rapid transit system to replace the campus buses. Levy describes it as an elaborate monorail giving students a “futuristic” ride between dorms and classrooms.  [4,18]
8 [quote] have a "healthy disregard for the impossible" — LeaderShape's slogan, as quoted by Larry Page (2009) [4]
9 [p] There were business ideas too. At Michigan he wrote a business plan for a company that would use software to build a music synthesiser, mixing his engineering with his music. He also took a class on usability. Students had to estimate how long experts would take to do tasks in a program. That taught him that building good, efficient interfaces is hard, and more like engineering than you might think.  [15,22]
10 [p] He was not always easy to work with. A college friend told Steven Levy that even then, Page was “controlling and paranoid” because “he wanted to make sure everything was done well and right.” That habit would later make Google great, and also cause real fights inside it.  [14]
11 [note] High standards can look like control. Learn the difference early.  
12 [h] A tour of San Francisco, and an argument  
13 [p] In 1995 Stanford University admitted Larry to its computer science PhD programme. During a visit for admitted students, a second-year student named Sergey Brin volunteered as a guide. He showed newcomers the campus and led a tour of San Francisco. Larry ended up in his group.  [17,6,20]
14 [p] It was not friendship at first sight. Walking up and down the city's hills, the two argued nonstop, including about urban planning. Brin had his own version: “We both found each other obnoxious.” But they kept talking, and a partnership slowly formed.  [17,6]
15 [quote] I thought he was pretty obnoxious. He had really strong opinions about things, and I guess I did, too. — Larry Page on first meeting Sergey Brin, to Wired (2005) [17]
16 [note] His future co-founder argued with him on day one. Good sign.  
17 [h] Picking a problem  
18 [p] At Stanford, Larry chose Terry Winograd, a pioneer of human-computer interaction, as his adviser. From his father he knew a dissertation can shape a whole academic career, so he took the choice seriously. He played with about 10 ideas. In 2013 he said he wanted to work on self-driving cars even then.  [17,19]
19 [p] In his 2009 speech he remembered having three projects in mind. His adviser told him, “why don't you work on the web for a while.” Larry called it seriously good advice, because the web was already growing fast in 1995.  [4]
20 [p] Larry liked the web for its maths. Every page was a dot, and every link a line between dots. “Computer scientists love graphs,” he told Wired. He noticed something odd: you could easily follow a link forward, but you could not see which pages linked back to you.  [17]
21 [p] He thought of academic papers. In science, a paper is judged partly by how many other papers cite it. __A link, Larry realised, is really a citation.__ Count the links pointing to a page, and you learn how much the web respects it.  [17]
22 [h] The worst spring  
23 [p] Then came the hardest moment of his young life. In late March 1996, soon after he moved to Stanford, his father had trouble breathing and drove himself to the hospital. Two months later, Carl Page died. Larry said he was completely devastated. The complications of polio had taken his father too soon.  [4]
24 [p] Friends told Wired that Larry saw finishing his PhD as a kind of tribute to his father. That matters for what comes next. When the moment came to leave Stanford and start a company, it was not just a business decision for him. It was personal.  [17]
25 [note] His father, the computer pioneer, never saw Google.  
26 [h] The dream at 23  
27 [p] At 23, Larry woke in the middle of the night from a vivid dream. His first thought: what if we could download the whole web, and just keep the links? He grabbed a pen and spent the rest of the night scribbling details and convincing himself it would work.  [4]
28 [p] Soon after, he told Winograd that downloading the web would take a couple of weeks. Winograd nodded, knowing it would take much longer, but wise enough not to say so. Larry later laughed about his own confidence.  [4]
29 [quote] The optimism of youth is often underrated! — Larry Page, University of Michigan commencement, 2009 [4]
30 [p] In ==March 1996== he pointed his program, a “crawler”, at one page, his own Stanford homepage, and let it loose. It worked outward from there, link by link. The web then held an estimated 10 million documents. He called the project BackRub, after the backlinks it collected.  [17]
31 [p] Sergey Brin, who had been hopping between projects, was hooked. He said it was the most exciting project he had found, “both because it tackled the Web, which represents human knowledge, and because I liked Larry.” Brin's strength in maths helped solve the hard part.  [17]
32 [p] The hard part was this. Not every link is equal. A link from an important page should count more than a link from an unknown one, and the importance of that page depends on its own links. Together they built a ranking system to handle this loop, called PageRank, after Page.  [17]
33 [example] For example: about 10,000 pages linked to Stanford's homepage. Which 10 do you show first? When Larry ranked links by links, Stanford came out on top. When he ranked universities, they came out in the order you would expect. “This thing really works,” they thought.  [5]
34 [p] Here is the surprise. “Amazingly, I had no thought of building a search engine,” Larry said in 2009. But BackRub already behaved like one, and its results beat AltaVista and Excite, which, in his words, “were looking only at text.”  [4,17,5]
35 [note] He wasn't trying to build Google. He was chasing a curiosity.  
36 [h] The mentor from IIT Kanpur  
37 [p] One important adult in this story was Rajeev Motwani, a Stanford professor. He was part of the very first group of undergraduate computer science students at IIT Kanpur, then did his PhD at Berkeley. With other professors and Brin, he formed a Stanford web data-mining group called MIDAS.  [24]
38 [p] Motwani became “the perfect sounding board for Sergey and Larry,” his colleague Prabhakar Raghavan wrote. He co-wrote an influential 1998 PageRank paper with Brin, Page and Winograd. Google's research blog says its founders were advised by him or took his courses. Brin said in a 2009 BBC radio interview that Google might not have been possible without him.  [24,23]
39 [note] Indian students, notice: a Kanpur graduate helped shape PageRank.  
40 [h] Eating Stanford's internet  
41 [p] Crawling the web needed many computers, and they had no money. So they begged and borrowed. They took a hard drive from the network lab and an idle processor from the computer science loading dock. Larry's dorm room became a machine lab, and Sergey's became the office.  [17]

43 [p] At one point BackRub's crawler used nearly half of Stanford's entire network bandwidth. In the autumn of 1996 it regularly crashed the university's internet connection. Stanford mostly let them carry on.  [17]
44 [quote] We're lucky there were a lot of forward-looking people at Stanford. They didn't hassle us too much about the resources we were using. — Larry Page, to Wired (2005) [17]
45 [p] When they needed storage, they maxed out three credit cards buying hard disks off the back of a truck. Larry later said that felt like being a sidewalk worm in a rainstorm, and that this feeling means you are probably on the right track.  [4]
46 [photo] https://commons.wikimedia.org/wiki/File:Gates_Computer_Science_Building_-_panoramio.jpg | The Gates Computer Science Building at Stanford University, home of the department where Page and Brin did their graduate work. | Bohao Zhao | CC BY 3.0
47 [p] In the summer of 1997, the researcher Jon Kleinberg visited Larry to compare notes and urged him to publish a paper on PageRank. Larry hesitated. He felt he had a secret formula and worried someone might steal it. In the end, he and Brin did publish.  [17]
48 [quote] He was concerned that someone might steal his ideas, and with PageRank, Page felt like he had the secret formula. — Jon Kleinberg, to Wired (2005) [17]
49 [p] On ==15 September 1997== they registered the domain google.com. The name came from “googol”, the number written as a 1 followed by 100 zeros. It was a hint at how much information they planned to organise.  [5,6]
50 [note] He almost hid PageRank. Publishing it helped make his name.  
51 [h] Nobody wanted to buy it  
52 [p] Here is the twist most people miss. Larry and Sergey did not rush to start a company. Both were reluctant to leave the PhD programme. First they tried to license their technology to existing web companies, and offered it for about $1 million. No one was interested.  [18,20]
53 [p] Why? In the late 1990s the hot idea was the “portal”, like Yahoo or AOL, offering email, news and weather in one place. Moment magazine put it bluntly: no one cared about search. Larry later admitted how close they came to quitting before they began.  [20,4]
54 [quote] But we almost didn't start Google because my co-founder Sergey and I were too worried about dropping out of our Ph.D. program. — Larry Page, University of Michigan commencement, 2009 [4]
55 [p] In the end they chose to build it themselves. They had completed master's degrees and took a leave of absence from the PhD programme. Larry's attitude, as he told Wired, was simple. __When nobody will buy your idea, maybe you are the one who is meant to build it.__  [5,25,18]
56 [quote] If the company failed, too bad. We were really going to be able to do something that mattered. — Larry Page, to Wired (2011) [18]
57 [h] A cheque made out to nobody  
58 [p] They collected money from faculty members, family and friends, enough to buy servers. Business Insider puts that first friends-and-family round at about $1 million. Then, after a quick demo, Sun Microsystems co-founder Andy Bechtolsheim wrote a cheque for ==$100,000== to “Google, Inc.”, in August 1998.  [20,14,6]
59 [p] There was one problem. Google, Inc. did not exist yet. The company had not been incorporated. For two weeks, while they did the paperwork, the two students had a $100,000 cheque and nowhere to deposit it.  [20]
60 [example] For example: imagine holding a $100,000 cheque made out to a company that is not real yet. That was Larry Page, aged 25, in the summer of 1998.  [20,5]
61 [p] Google was incorporated on ==4 September 1998==. Larry became chief executive and Sergey became president. They moved the servers out of the dorms and into a garage in Menlo Park, California, rented from Susan Wojcicki, who later became a Google employee and then CEO of YouTube. The rent was reportedly $1,700 a month.  [14,5,6]
62 [photo] https://commons.wikimedia.org/wiki/File:Google_garage.JPG | The Menlo Park garage where Google set up in 1998, photographed in 2011. | Jordiipa | CC BY-SA 3.0
63 [p] The grandson of an autoworker and the son of a computer pioneer was now running a company. He was 25, and the garage was his first office. What happened next is a different chapter.  [4,5]
