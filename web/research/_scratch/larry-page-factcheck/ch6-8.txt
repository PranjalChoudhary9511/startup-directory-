=== 6 the-fortune | 2019–2026 | $232.7 Billion, on Paper
0 [h] The fire alarm  
1 [p] For three years after 2019, Page stayed away. He and Brin let Pichai run things and focused on projects like flying cars and disaster relief. Then, in November 2022, a small company called OpenAI released ChatGPT, and it looked like the first real threat to Google's $149 billion search business in decades.  [152,153]
2 [p] Pichai declared a "code red". In December he invited the founders back for several meetings. They reviewed the company's AI plans, approved plans and pitched ideas for putting chatbot features into search. Brin went further and filed his first code request in years in January 2023. What Page has done since, day to day, has not been made clear in public.  [152,154]
3 [p] The irony was painful. The Transformer, a key technique behind modern AI chatbots, is listed by Google itself among the achievements of its own research teams. In April 2023 Google joined its two big AI groups into Google DeepMind. __Page's old purchase of a little London lab was now at the centre of Google's defence.__  [97]
4 [p] It worked better than many expected. By October 2026, Alphabet reported that its Gemini app had reached one billion users, and that its search query growth was the highest in five years. Pichai said its AI investments were driving search growth. Analysts were less sure that those searches earn as much money as the old ones.  [40,50]
5 [note] A rival nearly caught them sleeping. Even the best companies can become complacent.  
6 [h] In court, and still standing  
7 [p] Meanwhile the courts were catching up. In August 2024, Judge Amit Mehta ruled that Google had broken US antitrust law, the rules against abusing a dominant position. Google held about 90% of online search. It paid $26.3 billion in 2021 alone to be the default on phones and browsers. The judge noted an estimated $20 billion going to Apple in 2022.  [155,156]
8 [quote] Google is a monopolist, and it has acted as one to maintain its monopoly. — Judge Amit Mehta, ruling of 5 August 2024 [155]
9 [p] But the penalty was much lighter than the government wanted. In September 2025, Mehta refused to force a sale of Chrome, calling that incredibly messy and highly risky. He banned exclusive deals and ordered Google to share some search data with rivals. Google is appealing the ruling, and in January 2026 it asked to pause those orders.  [157,158]
10 [p] A second US judge, Leonie Brinkema, found in April 2025 that Google had illegally monopolised parts of the ad technology market. DoubleClick's old ad server sits in the middle of that case. On 2 September 2026 she too declined to order a break-up, choosing behaviour changes instead. Google can still appeal. The EU also fined Google €2.95 billion in September 2025 over similar practices, and Google has said it will appeal.  [159,160,161]
11 [note] That $3.1 billion deal from 2007 is now inside a monopoly case.  
12 [h] The bets that paid off, sitting in the portfolio  
13 [p] Here is a twist in the fortune story. Some of the best returns in Google's recent history did not come from a Google product. In 2015 Google invested ==$900 million== in Elon Musk's SpaceX. By 30 June 2026, after SpaceX's IPO, that stake was worth about ==$94 billion==. SpaceX shares have since fallen.  [162]
14 [p] In 2023 Google put an initial $300 million into the AI company Anthropic, later growing that to $13.3 billion, with up to $30 billion more committed. Bloomberg valued its stake of about 14% at around $124 billion in mid-2026. These gains helped Alphabet report a record ==$112.1 billion== quarterly profit in the second quarter of 2026.  [163,164]
15 [p] That profit was mostly on paper, not cash from customers. Alphabet booked $98 billion of net gains in that quarter, mainly from unrealised rises in shares it holds. Without such gains its profit growth would have been about 23% instead of nearly 300%. __Learn this difference early: a gain on paper has not yet paid anyone's bills.__  [165,166]
16 [example] For example: Alphabet turned $900 million into about $94 billion in 11 years. That's about 100 times the money. It is more than Google's entire stock market value on the day it went public, which was $27.2 billion.  [162,135]
17 [h] From $26 million to $84.75 billion  
18 [p] Google once raised $26 million and made it last until it was profitable. In June 2026 Alphabet announced an equity raise of ==$84.75 billion==, to pay for AI data centres and chips. It included a $10 billion private sale to Warren Buffett's Berkshire Hathaway. Buffett told CNBC he had made a mistake by not buying earlier.  [38,167,168]
19 [p] That is what AI costs. Alphabet expected to spend $195 billion to $205 billion on capital expenditure in 2026, meaning buildings, servers and chips, and more than $300 billion in 2027. In the third quarter of 2026 it took in $127.6 billion in revenue and still had negative free cash flow of $9.07 billion. Free cash flow means what is left after paying for all that building.  [169,40]
20 [p] Investors noticed. On 29 October 2026, the shares dropped about 12% to 13%, the worst day since Google went public. Close to $500 billion of value disappeared in one trading session. Page personally lost about ==$31 billion== on paper that day, and yet he moved up to third-richest in the world the next day, because Michael Dell's fortune fell faster.  [170,113,171]
21 [note] $31 billion gone in a day, and he went up the rankings. Crazy.

23 [h] Where the money actually is  
24 [p] Page has no big salary to speak of. He and Brin have taken just ==$1 a year== since around the IPO. Nearly all of his wealth is one thing: shares in the company he co-founded. Forbes estimated his net worth at ==$232.7 billion== on 1 November 2026, third in the world.  [172,3]
25 [p] Alphabet's April 2026 proxy statement, a yearly filing that shows who owns what, lists him with 389,051,160 Class B shares. That is 46.5% of all Class B and ==27.4% of all votes==. With Brin, he has 52.7% of the votes. So if the two men vote together, they can outvote every other shareholder combined.  [0,173]
26 [p] The proxy leaves out his non-voting Class C shares. But under the 2014 agreements, he cannot hold more Class B than Class C. So he must own at least another 389 million or so Class C shares. Let's do the maths, using prices around $284 and $281 from 29 October 2026.  [0,143,146,113]
27 [example] For example: 389 million Class B shares at about $284 comes to about $110 billion. Another 389 million Class C shares at about $281 comes to about $109 billion. That is about $220 billion, out of Forbes's $232.7 billion. It is about 6% of Alphabet's roughly 12.3 billion shares (our arithmetic, using share counts from company filings).  [0,113,40,3]
28 [note] About 6% of the shares, 27% of the votes. That gap matters.  
29 [p] Page has spent real money on his bets, but those amounts are tiny next to this stake. We could not find a reliable public value for his private companies. He has a family office, called Koop, which is a private firm that manages a wealthy family's money. His flying-car vehicle is One Aero, and Flu Lab funds research into influenza.  [107,27]

31 [h] Leaving California  
32 [p] Wealth like this also draws political attention. A union-backed ballot measure in California, Proposition 40, proposed a one-time 5% tax on billionaires who lived in the state on 1 January 2026. In December 2025, more than 45 California companies linked to Page filed to go inactive or move out of state, according to state records seen by The New York Times.  [177,178]
33 [p] Business Insider reported that his family office and Flu Lab were converted to Delaware companies. The Wall Street Journal reported that he bought two Miami homes for about $173 million, and Forbes now lists his home as Miami. The vote is on 3 November 2026, the day after this page was checked, and a late-October poll showed the measure trailing. We do not know the result.  [107,10,3,177]
34 [p] The New York Times reported that he had discussed leaving because of the measure, citing people briefed on the talks. Page has not explained it publicly, as far as we could find, so we will not put words in his mouth. What is on record is the filings and the timing.  [179,178,107]
35 [h] What to take from all of this  
36 [p] Add up the whole story and you see something odd. He made nearly all his money from a single company built in his twenties, yet spent much of his later life on bets, many of which failed or are still unproven. __The fortune came from one thing done extremely well, and the failures were what that fortune could afford.__  [0,62,59]
37 [lesson] Nearly every moonshot on these pages lost money, and it hardly touched his wealth. Protect the core business first. Then you can afford to be a dreamer with the profits, not with your rent.  
38 [lesson] He went from $301.7 billion to $232.7 billion in about three and a half months without doing anything. Do not tie your sense of worth to a share price, and do not spend paper gains.  
39 [lesson] He holds about 6% of the shares and 27% of the votes. That let him make long, strange bets, but it also means shareholders can hardly challenge him. Understand both sides before you copy it.  
=== 7 how-they-work | How he runs things | The Boss Who Hated Meetings
0 [p] Start with what we cannot tell you. No reliable source has published a Larry Page daily routine: no 5 a.m. alarm, no morning ritual, no secret breakfast. Anyone selling you one is guessing. What we do have is better, because it comes from people who sat across the table from him.  [180,181]
1 [p] When he took the CEO job back in 2011, Bloomberg Businessweek reported, he treated it like an engineering puzzle. He worked ==80-hour weeks==, read business leadership books by the pile, and studied people he admired, such as the coach Bill Campbell and the investor Warren Buffett. By 2014 Fortune found him going to meetings with only his phone, no laptop. He did this on purpose. He wanted to feel what users felt as the world moved to mobile, and he asked engineers to try a phone-only day each week too.  [180,181]
2 [note] He made himself the test user. A CEO with only a phone.  
3 [h] The day he fired the middle  
4 [p] July 2001. Google had about 400 staff and Page was 28. He had come to hate the layer of managers between him and the engineers. He thought great engineers did not need supervising. He also suspected the managers were steering people away from projects he cared about, like scanning the world's books.  [14]
5 [p] Google's HR head, Stacy Sullivan, told him the plan was crazy. People need somebody to go to with problems, she said. Eric Schmidt agreed with her. So did Page's coach, Bill Campbell, who one evening brought engineer after engineer into Page's office to say they actually wanted a manager, someone to settle arguments and give direction. Page did it anyway.  [14]
6 [p] That afternoon about 130 engineers and a half-dozen project managers stood outside his office among second-hand furniture. The new engineering chief, Wayne Rosing, announced the change. The managers had no warning.  [14]
7 [p] Page explained himself in a flat voice, avoiding eye contact, and added that they had not been doing a very good job. One engineer shouted at him that an all-hands meeting was no place for a performance review. One of the managers later said the moment felt humiliating.  [14]
8 [note] Imagine hearing this in front of the whole company.  
9 [p] It failed. Steven Levy reported that more than 100 people ended up reporting to one overwhelmed head of engineering. Projects went short of resources, work was duplicated, and engineers wondered where their careers were going. The "fired" managers were moved to another team and Google quietly brought managers back.  [18,14,182]
10 [p] __Page was right about one thing and wrong about another: engineers should be led by people who understand engineering, but they still need to be led.__ With Jonathan Rosenberg he later settled on hiring computer scientists as product managers instead of only MBAs.  [18,14,182]
11 [note] Very important for first-time founders! Being half right can still hurt people.  
12 [example] For example: Page eventually wrote down his own management rules. In our words: do not delegate if you can do it faster yourself. If you add nothing, get out of the way and let the workers talk directly. Do not be a bureaucrat. A good idea beats a senior title. And never just say no; if you refuse something, help the person find a better way. Rule one is the one he later reversed.  [14]
13 [h] He read every hire  
14 [p] Page insisted on signing off on every new hire at Google. By 2011 he had checked ==more than 30,000== people. Custom software squeezed each candidate's hiring packet into something he could scan quickly. A batch arrived each week, and he normally sent it back within three or four days, with approvals and some rejections. His reason was simple: it told him what was really going on inside the company.  [18]
15 [p] Indian readers will enjoy the footnote. Sundar Pichai interviewed at Google on 1 April 2004, just after Page had stopped personally interviewing candidates. Years later, as Google's CEO, he told students at IIT Kharagpur a joke he liked to repeat.  [183,184]
16 [quote] I got into Google because Larry didn't interview me. — Sundar Pichai, at IIT Kharagpur, January 2017 [183]
17 [h] War on meetings  
18 [p] In 2007 Page noticed something odd about having an assistant. People were too shy to ask him for a meeting, but they happily asked his assistant. So he and Sergey Brin got rid of their assistants. If you wanted Larry, you had to catch him walking down a corridor. Googlers traded tips on where he might be, and he was very good at flashing a big smile and speeding up as he passed.  [18]
19 [p] As CEO in 2011 he changed the rules for everyone. Business Insider reported that one of his first acts was an email to the whole company on how to run meetings. Kristen Gil, a Google operations VP, later published the basics.  [185,186]
20 [p] Every decision meeting needs one clear decision-maker, or it should not happen. Ten people at most. If you have nothing to add, you should not be there. And a decision must never wait for a meeting: if a meeting really is needed first, hold it right away.  [185,186]
21 [quote] There are no companies that make good slow decisions. — Larry Page, closing speech at Zeitgeist, as quoted by Google's Kristen Gil [186]
22 [note] Copy this list onto your wall. Works for a college club too.  
23 [h] The scrutinizer  
24 [p] The night Google launched its first ads, in 2000, Page stayed late typing search after search. When Amit Singhal, later Google's search chief, arrived the next morning, the corridor walls were covered in printouts.  [181]
25 [p] On each one Page had scribbled a sharp question. Is this ad good for users? Why is that one showing? What went wrong here?  [181]
26 [p] He did something similar one Friday in 2002. He posted printed ad results on the wall of the office kitchen and scrawled across them that these ads sucked. A search for a vintage Kawasaki motorcycle, the H1B, was bringing up ads for lawyers offering help with H-1B work visas.  [187,188]
27 [p] He gave no order. Five engineers who were not even on the ads team spent the weekend on it, and by ==5 a.m. on Monday== they had sent round a prototype. It became the core insight behind AdWords, and the fix proved to be worth billions.  [187,188]
28 [note] He didn't assign it. Smart people saw a problem and took it.  
29 [p] The critics have a point here too. The Financial Times review of the book that tells this story called it management by humiliation. Schmidt and Rosenberg's answer is that Google's culture attracted the kind of people who would respond by fixing it. __On most teams, a note like that just makes people hide.__  [189,187]
30 [p] He also measured everything. Walking down a street in Morocco with the early Googler Megan Smith, he pulled her into an internet cafe and began timing how long pages took to load.  [18]
31 [p] Paul Buchheit, who created Gmail, once showed him an early demo, and Page said it took at least 600 milliseconds to reload. Buchheit doubted anyone could tell. He checked the server logs. Six hundred milliseconds.  [18]
32 [note] He could feel a 0.6-second delay. Speed was an obsession.  
33 [h] How he decides  
34 [p] Page once told a colleague that he solves hard problems by boiling them down to two choices and picking the better one. Whatever got hurt along the way he treated as damage he could live with. That method makes decisions fast. It also explains a lot of the wreckage in the "what went wrong" chapter.  [182]
35 [p] His favourite way to end an argument is to test it himself. In 2002 nobody could say how long it would take to scan all the world's books. So Page and Marissa Mayer set up a scanner in his office, using a metronome to keep the page-turning steady.  [190,18]
36 [p] A 300-page book took ==40 minutes==. He filled spreadsheets with the costs and storage. When people still did not believe the numbers, he went ahead and built it.  [190,18]
37 [example] For example: for company purchases he used what The New York Times called the toothbrush test. Is this something you will use once or twice a day, and does it make your life better? Not earnings, not cash flow. Google bought Nest for $3.2 billion even though its sales were tiny next to Google's profits.  [191]
38 [h] What working for him felt like  
39 [quote] You feel terrified, inspired, and nurtured at the same time. — Andy Conrad, head of Google X life sciences, on pitching ideas to Page [181]
40 [p] Those who worked for him describe the same pattern. Eric Veach, an engineer on the ad system, said Page's ideas were far-fetched and he wanted them immediately. When Veach pointed out that not all countries use credit cards, Page suggested that in Uzbekistan Google could take payment in goats. Veach gently suggested starting with Visa and MasterCard.  [18]
41 [p] But the odd ideas sometimes worked. In 2003 Denise Griffin, who ran customer support, asked for more staff. Page told her the whole idea of answering users one by one made no sense, and that users should answer each other. It felt mad to her.  [18]
42 [p] Google built forums where users did exactly that, and it worked. Staff also learned how to handle him. The product manager Wesley Chan once started a Google Voice review by letting Page and Brin choose their own phone numbers. They spent the hour inventing maths puns, and the product passed.  [18]
43 [note] Your manager's job is partly to be managed. Learn how.  
44 [note] Boss has a weird idea? Test it before you laugh.  
45 [h] The man changes  
46 [p] Here is the most useful part for you. __Page learned.__ Early Google settled fights by loud argument, and he was perhaps the most aggressive of all. Over the years the executives became bitter rivals.  [14]
47 [p] Soon after he became CEO again, at a retreat at the Carneros Inn, he stood up and told his top people that the fighting had to stop. Google's goals were too big now, he said, and those goals needed people working together. From now on there would be ==“zero tolerance for fighting.”==  [14]
48 [note] Rewrite your own rules as you grow. He did.  
49 [p] His "do not delegate" rule also died. The success he was proudest of as president of products was Android, and Carlson argues it came from delegation: he picked Andy Rubin and insisted Rubin be left alone. As CEO he copied that, giving YouTube, Ads and Search their own CEO-like leaders. Then in October 2014 he handed most Google products to Pichai, and he explained why to Fortune.  [182,14,181]
50 [quote] any time I can delegate some things, I should. — Larry Page, Fortune, December 2014 [192]
51 [p] He never fell in love with routine management, though. A former executive told Bloomberg Businessweek that Page's eyes would glaze over when talk moved from technology to ordinary business, and recalled him telling someone that what they did was boring. A former senior director recalled a heated fight among his top team, and Page asking why they could not sort it out themselves. __Know which part of the job you are bad at, then hire for it.__ That is what Alphabet eventually became.  [180]
=== 8 mindset | What he believes | Why Does It Need a Plug?
0 [p] Inside Google X they tell a joke about him. A scientist wheels a working time machine into Page's office. Page looks at the power cord and asks why it needs to be plugged in.  [19,181]
1 [p] Astro Teller, who ran the lab, says this is not ingratitude. It is simply how the man's mind works: there is always a next step to find. Here are the seven beliefs behind the joke.  [19,181]
2 [h] 1. Small improvements are a slow death  
3 [p] Page told Wired that most companies decay slowly because they keep doing roughly what they did before. It feels safe. It is not. In technology, big jumps are coming anyway, so whoever only polishes will be overtaken.  [19]
4 [p] That is why, when Google was just a search company, it launched an email service with ==100 times== the storage anyone else offered. Levy called this the gospel of 10x.  [19]
5 [quote] But incremental improvement is guaranteed to be obsolete over time. — Larry Page, Wired, 2013 [19]
6 [example] For example: the leader of Project Loon, Google's internet balloon project, told Page it might raise the internet's total bandwidth by 5%. Page asked why it could not double or triple it. Teller called this making sure there was a moon shot after the moon shot.  [181]
7 [h] 2. Huge problems are less crowded  
8 [p] This is the most surprising idea in the whole book, so read it slowly. In his 2009 University of Michigan speech, Page argued that mega-ambitious dreams are often easier to make progress on. Hardly anyone else is crazy enough to try, so there is little competition. And the best people want the biggest challenges, so big goals help you hire.  [4]
9 [quote] Always work hard on something uncomfortably exciting! — Larry Page, University of Michigan, 2009 [4]
10 [p] He even has a name for the sweet spot: the "zero million dollar" research problem. He looks for a question that nobody is funding. Balloons for internet access were one. Experts said a balloon could not stay up long enough.  [193,181]
11 [p] So he went searching on his own, and found a picture of a 1960 balloon that had gone around the Earth about five times. His reasoning: if someone did that in 1960, we can do it with better materials. By 2014 Loon balloons were routinely staying up for about 100 days.  [193,181]
12 [quote] And you know that zero million dollars are going into that problem. — Larry Page, Fortune, 2014 [193]
13 [note] Search what nobody is working on. Read this twice.  
14 [h] 3. Aim high and even failure pays  
15 [p] Page does not count missing a bold goal as failing. Not trying is what counts. His argument to Steven Levy was that even a big miss tends to leave something behind. Loon shows both sides.  [18,29,194]
16 [p] Alphabet shut it in 2021 because it could not get costs low enough. But its laser links for beaming data live on in another project, Taara, which is working with partners to bring cheaper internet to parts of Sub-Saharan Africa.  [18,29,194]
17 [quote] Even if you fail at your ambitious thing, it's very hard to fail completely. — Larry Page, Wired, 2011 [18]
18 [h] 4. An invention nobody uses is a sad story  
19 [p] Page admires researchers but does not want to be one of them. When Levy brought up Xerox PARC, the lab that invented many tools of modern computing, Page said its problem was simple. It was not focused on selling anything.  [19]
20 [p] You need both invention and commercialisation. He praised Tesla for spending most of its effort on getting a car out to real people. __A prototype is the start of the work, not the end.__  [19]
21 [h] 5. Technology should buy back human time  
22 [p] At the 2013 Google I/O conference he talked about farming. Long ago, he said, nearly all humans spent all their time hunting or farming. Farming is great if you choose it, he said, and not great if you have no choice. Technology gave people that choice. He sees self-driving cars the same way: you get back the hours you spend driving, and cities get more green space and fewer parking lots.  [195]
23 [p] In Michigan he gave students an unusual tip: be lazy. What he meant was leverage. Three people can write software that millions of people use, but three people cannot answer the phone a million times a day. His advice was to find that kind of leverage. For a student with a laptop in Pune or Patna, that is the most hopeful line in this chapter.  [4]
24 [quote] We should be building great things that don't exist. — Larry Page, Google I/O, 2013 [195]
25 [h] 6. Beating rivals is a boring goal  
26 [p] Page told Wired that he worries something has gone wrong with how companies are run. Press coverage reads like a sports match, he said, and few amazing things happen only because of competition. He finds it hard to get excited about going to work just to crush someone doing roughly the same thing. His own test as Google grew was to keep asking whether he would want to work there. Dying tech companies, he said, stopped being a home for curious people.  [19,192]
27 [p] This is why he argued for years with Steve Jobs, a mentor to him. Jobs told him Google was spread far too thin, and Page later admitted Jobs was right in some sense. But Page wanted more impact, not less.  [196,181,19,176,197]
28 [p] He said Google was touching maybe 0.1% of what technology could do for people. So who was right? Apple stayed focused. Google ended up with a graveyard of dead projects and a few giant wins, and Alphabet still reached about $4.5 trillion in 2026.  [196,181,19,176,197]
29 [quote] You guys are doing too much stuff. — Steve Jobs to Larry Page, as Page retold it in 2014 [196]
30 [h] 7. A good company can be a form of charity  
31 [p] In March 2014 at TED, Charlie Rose asked about his old remark that he might leave his money to Elon Musk rather than a charity. Page did not back away. He said businesses can drive revolutionary change, and praised Musk's dream of Mars as a way to back up humanity. It was a bold thing to say, and critics later used it against him. We return to that argument in the next chapter.  [198,199]
32 [quote] That's a company, and that's philanthropical. — Larry Page on Elon Musk's Mars plan, TED, 2014 [198]
33 [h] Where the belief runs out  
34 [p] Every strong belief has a blind spot. Page and Musk had been friends for over a decade, and Musk used to joke about crashing on Page's sofa. At Musk's 44th birthday party in Napa in July 2015, the two sat by a fire pit and argued about artificial intelligence late into the night.  [200]
35 [p] Page, speaking almost in a whisper, described a future where humans merge with intelligent machines and the best intelligence wins. Musk said the machines would destroy us. Page called him a “specieist”, someone who favours humans over future digital life. Musk later said that was the last straw.  [200]
36 [note] A whispered argument by a fire pit ended a decade-long friendship.  
37 [p] Only a few weeks later, The New York Times reported, Musk had dinner with Sam Altman and several researchers. The dinner led to OpenAI. Walter Isaacson wrote that, before that, Musk had tried to talk Demis Hassabis out of selling DeepMind to Google. __Weeks after a birthday-party argument, one of Google's biggest future rivals was taking shape.__ We only have Musk's side and other people's memories of that night; we found no public account from Page.  [200,201,202]
38 [quote] The future of AI should not be controlled by Larry. — Elon Musk to Demis Hassabis, according to Walter Isaacson [201]
39 [note] Same man, two sides: his optimism built Google and alarmed his friend.  
40 [p] Levy noticed the same blind spot closer to home. Page sees Google's goodness in black and white, he wrote, and that has probably hurt the company's reputation. Yet that same refusal to see grey also gave him the nerve to order the wildest projects. The two come as a package.  [18]
