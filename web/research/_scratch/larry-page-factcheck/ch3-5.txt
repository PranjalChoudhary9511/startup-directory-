=== 3 every-company | Every venture, win or lose | Everything He Ever Started
0 [p] Most people think Larry Page built one company. The real list is much stranger. He wrote a business plan in college, tried to sell his best idea for about ==$1 million==, and then ran the company he could not sell. After that he spent decades backing flying machines, internet balloons, power kites and a lab that studies naked mole rats to understand ageing.  [15,26,27,28,29,30]
1 [p] Some of these he paid for with his own money. Others ran on Google's profits while he was in charge. A few were not his idea at all, but he was the one who said yes. Here is every major one we could verify, in order, including the ones that crashed.  [27,18,11]

=== 4 first-money | 1998–2004 | Betting the Company
0 [h] The $750,000 mistake that never happened  
1 [p] In early 1999, Google was a few months old, and its founders felt it was getting in the way of their studies. Page and Brin tried to sell it to Excite, then the number two search engine behind Yahoo, for $1 million. Excite's investor Vinod Khosla talked them down to ==$750,000==. Excite's CEO, George Bell, still said no, and people argued for years over exactly why.  [35,34,112]
2 [p] Stop and think about that. __Google's founders tried to sell the most valuable idea of their lives for under a million dollars, and they were rejected.__ Excite no longer exists. Google still does.  [35,34]
3 [example] For example: on 29 October 2026, Alphabet lost about $480 billion of stock market value in a single day. That one bad day was worth about 640,000 times the price Excite refused to pay in 1999.  [113,35]
4 [note] Rejection might be the luckiest thing that ever happens to you.  
5 [h] Two rival investors, one rule  
6 [p] A venture capital firm, or VC, is a firm that invests other people's money in young companies in exchange for shares. In June 1999, Google announced ==$25 million== from two of the most famous firms in Silicon Valley. Sequoia Capital and Kleiner Perkins each put in about $12.5 million, and Michael Moritz and John Doerr joined the board.  [33,114,18]
7 [p] The two firms shared the deal equally. By 2004 each firm held 9.4%, while Page held 15.2% and Brin 15.1%. Google's own filing says it sold just $26 million of stock before it became profitable.  [114,38]
8 [p] The money came with one big condition. Page and Brin promised to hire an experienced outsider as CEO, which VCs often demand from young founders. Silicon Valley calls this "adult supervision". Page was 26, and he did not want it.  [18]
9 [quote] They said, 'We've changed our mind. We think we can run the company between the two of us.' — John Doerr, recalling the founders' phone call a few months after the deal (Wired, 2011) [18]
10 [p] Doerr's first instinct was to sell his shares. Instead he made an offer: go and meet the best CEOs in tech, then decide. So Page and Brin toured Steve Jobs, Andy Grove of Intel, Scott Cook of Intuit, and Jeff Bezos. They came back and agreed to hire a CEO, with one condition. It had to be Steve Jobs.  [18]
11 [p] Doerr widened the search. Reports say they rejected a long line of candidates before meeting Eric Schmidt, then CEO of Novell, a real computer scientist who had also been to the Burning Man festival. He became chairman in March 2001 and CEO that summer. As late as 2002 the founders still sounded bitter. Brin told a reporter that investors felt better now they need not worry what "two hooligans are going to do with their millions."  [115,116,14,18]
12 [note] Investors forced this hire. Page later called it brilliant. Both can be true.

14 [h] The day he fired his managers  
15 [p] Page stayed hard to manage. One day in July 2001, aged 28, he decided that all of Google's project managers had to go. He thought a layer of non-engineers was slowing down the brilliant people he had hired. He also suspected they were steering engineers away from projects he cared about, like scanning the world's books.  [14]
16 [p] Business Insider's account says Google's HR head, Stacy Sullivan, told him the plan was nuts. Schmidt and Page's executive coach, Bill Campbell, agreed with her. Page went ahead anyway, in front of about 130 engineers. One engineer shouted that it was totally unprofessional.  [14]
17 [quote] You can't just self-organize! — Stacy Sullivan, Google's HR head, to Larry Page in 2001 (via Douglas Edwards, I'm Feeling Lucky) [14]
18 [p] It did not last. More than 100 people ended up reporting to one overwhelmed head of engineering. Google's managers were moved or rehired, and the founders quietly gave up on the idea. It is a story of a smart person being badly wrong, and __the most useful thing about it is that he reversed it.__  [18,14,117]
19 [note] Very important for first-time founders: being stubborn only works if you can reverse course.  
20 [h] Finding the money machine  
21 [p] Now the awkward question. How does a free search engine make money? At first even Google did not know. The early assumption was that licensing search and selling servers might earn as much as ads. Google's first revenue, in 1999, was just $220,000.  [66,38]
22 [p] One of those licensing deals was with Yahoo, the giant of the web, which began using Google's search in June 2000. Yahoo received a warrant in the deal, a right to buy Google shares later. In 2002, Yahoo paid Google $13.2 million, more than a quarter of Yahoo's profit that year.  [39,118]
23 [example] For example: Yahoo was paying its future rival to run its search box. It later sold Google shares for about $400 million in 2004 and nearly $1 billion in 2005, so those shares brought Yahoo well over $1 billion.  [118,39]
24 [p] Ads came next. In October 2000 Google launched AdWords: any business could buy short text ads tied to a search word, on its own, with a credit card. About 350 advertisers had already signed up during the test. Google said it would not use pop-ups, animations or flashing logos. At that point it handled more than 20 million searches a day.  [119]
25 [p] Page insisted that the system be simple and able to grow without salespeople. When engineer Eric Veach pointed out that not every country used credit cards, Page suggested taking payment in whatever suited the country. In Uzbekistan, he said, Google could take goats.  [18]
26 [note] Goats as payment. He was joking, probably. The self-service idea was not.  
27 [p] Then came the clever part. In 2002 Google moved to paying per click, so advertisers paid only when someone actually clicked. Ads were sold by a nonstop auction, and a good ad that people clicked could beat a bigger bid. A rival, Overture, had pioneered search ads by auction. __Google's twist was to reward the ad that people actually wanted.__  [39,112,120,66]
28 [h] The bet-the-company moment  
29 [p] In spring 2002, the web's biggest prize came up for grabs. America Online, with about 34 million users, was choosing who would run search and search ads for it. Overture had the job. Inktomi supplied the search results. Google had just launched its new ad system, and it wanted to take both.  [121,122,123]
30 [p] Page told his team that he wanted to bid to win. To do that Google offered to hand AOL about 85% of the ad revenue and guarantee a minimum payment, whatever the ads actually earned. A guarantee like this is dangerous: if the ads flop, you still owe the money. Reports put it at around $100 million to $150 million, and Google did not have that kind of cash.  [124,125,121,126]
31 [quote] You're betting the company if you do that. — Omid Kordestani, Google's head of sales, as reported in Ken Auletta's Googled (2009) [124]
32 [quote] we should be able to monetize the pages, if not we deserve to go out of business. — Larry Page's reply, as reported in Ken Auletta's Googled (2009) [124,121]
33 [p] Google won in May 2002. The market's verdict was instant: Overture's shares fell more than a third in a day, and Inktomi's fell about a quarter. The bet paid off. Google's revenue jumped from $86 million in 2001 to $348 million in 2002, then ==$962 million== in 2003.  [123,122,38,125]
34 [p] This was no one-off gamble. Google's IPO filing shows that it kept making such promises to big partners, and that by the end of 2003 it had promised ==$477 million== in outstanding minimum guarantees. It also admits that in 2003 some of those deals lost $22.5 million. The danger was real and it was on paper for all to read.  [39]

36 [h] Yahoo comes shopping  
37 [p] That same summer, Yahoo's CEO Terry Semel tried to buy Google. Wired reported that he offered about $3 billion and was furious when they were not interested. His own team told him Google was probably worth at least $5 billion, but that would have meant spending Yahoo's entire market value. In another account, the founders first asked for $1 billion and raised it to $3 billion once Semel agreed.  [127,112]
38 [p] Semel bought search companies Inktomi and Overture instead. Overture came with a lawsuit: it had sued Google in 2002, saying AdWords copied its patented way of selling ads. Days before the IPO, in August 2004, Google settled by giving Yahoo ==2.7 million shares==, worth $291 million to $365 million at the expected price. That charge meant Google expected to post a loss in its first quarter as a public company.  [127,120,128,39]
39 [note] Google paid its way out of the suit with shares, not cash. Clever.  
40 [h] An IPO that annoyed Wall Street  
41 [p] An IPO, or initial public offering, is when a private company first sells shares to the public on a stock market. Usually big banks set the price and hand shares to favoured clients. Page and Brin wanted an auction instead, open to ordinary people, with a minimum bid of just five shares.  [39,129]
42 [quote] Google is not a conventional company. We do not intend to become one. — Larry Page and Sergey Brin, founders' letter, Google's IPO filing (2004) [38,12]
43 [p] They also gave themselves a shield. Google's shares came in two kinds: Class A for the public, with one vote each, and Class B for insiders, with ten. So they could sell parts of the company without losing control of it. They also said they would not chase smooth quarterly results.  [38,39]
44 [p] Then almost everything went wrong. Page and Brin had given an interview to Playboy, and it ran just as the sale began, when companies are supposed to stay quiet. Google said it believed it had not broken the rules. Regulators did not stop the IPO, but Google had to warn investors it might have, and it printed the whole article in its prospectus. One correction: the article said about 1,000 staff, and Google actually had 2,292.  [130,131,132,39]
45 [p] There was also an inquiry into shares and options it had issued to employees without registering them. It ended up buying back almost 23 million options. Investors grew cautious and the price range was slashed from $108–$135 to $85–$95. The shares on offer fell from 25.7 million to 19.6 million.  [133,129,130]
46 [p] On 19 August 2004, Google went public at ==$85== a share and raised ==$1.67 billion==, the biggest internet IPO so far. The shares ended the first day at $100.34, up 18%, valuing the company at $27.2 billion. Reuters put the founders' stakes at about $4.35 billion. Page was 31.  [134,135,133]
47 [example] For example: Google's IPO fees came to $2.3839 a share, under 3% of the price. By February 2005 the stock had traded as high as $210.86, about two and a half times the IPO price.  [39,129]
48 [h] The maths jokes in the legal papers  
49 [p] Here is our favourite detail in this whole story. Securities filings are the most boring documents on earth. Google's first filing asked to register exactly ==$2,718,281,828== of stock, which is the mathematical constant e with the decimal point moved. Google's own part of the final IPO was 14,142,135 shares, the same digits as the square root of 2.  [38,39]
50 [p] A year later it came back to raise about $4 billion more. The AP spotted that the new number of shares, 14,159,265, matched the digits of pi after the decimal point. Nobody at Google needed to do this. It simply told Wall Street exactly who it was dealing with.  [136,137]
51 [note] Two engineers signed legal papers and hid maths jokes inside. Read this twice.

53 [lesson] Google raised a tiny amount before it was profitable and kept most of the company. When a business grows fast, every share you did not sell early is worth a fortune later. Raise what you truly need.

=== 5 the-big-bets | 2005–2019 | Expensive Dreams
0 [h] Rich, and impatient  
1 [p] After the IPO, Google had money and Page had freedom. In 2003 executives were discussing how fast to grow abroad, and he asked how many engineers Microsoft had. He was told about 25,000. Page said, in all seriousness, that Google should have a million. Schmidt put a hand on his shoulder and brought him back to earth.  [18]
2 [note] Twenty-five thousand? He said a million. And he meant it.  
3 [p] Google then went on a buying run. In 2006 it paid $1.65 billion in stock for YouTube. In April 2007 it won a bidding war with Microsoft and Yahoo for DoubleClick, an early pioneer of online ads, paying ==$3.1 billion== in cash. The FT called DoubleClick a high price, since its revenue was reported at only $300 million to $400 million.  [48,138]
4 [example] For example: in 2008 Google joined a US auction for mobile airwaves, mainly to push the price past a level that would force the winner to open its network to others. It badly wanted to lose. When Verizon topped its bid, executives were relieved. Page, though, urged them to think about bidding again. Google let Verizon's bid stand.  [18]
5 [h] The boss comes back  
6 [p] In April 2011, Page became CEO of Google again, aged 38. Schmidt tweeted that adult supervision was no longer needed. Google had grown to more than 24,000 staff and almost $30 billion in revenue, and Page wanted it to feel like a startup again.  [18,41]
7 [p] He then did something surprising. He called Steve Jobs, who by then had no love for Google. Jobs told his biographer that his first instinct was to refuse rudely. Then he remembered the older men who had helped him when he was young. So he agreed, and Page went to Jobs's home in Palo Alto.  [139]
8 [quote] What are the five products you want to focus on? Get rid of the rest because they're dragging you down. — Steve Jobs to Larry Page in 2011, as told to Walter Isaacson [139]
9 [p] Page listened. He shuffled his management team, shut more than 20 products in six months and wound down Google Labs. He called it putting more wood behind fewer arrows. Not everyone loved it: when Google Reader closed, its users had to go looking for alternatives. __The founder who once wanted to do everything was learning, at 38, how to say no.__  [139,108,71,109]
10 [h] Google+: the bet he got wrong  
11 [p] His biggest early decision went the other way. Page believed Google had to go social to compete, so he sent a memo as he took over. Everyone's 2011 bonus would go up or down by as much as ==25%==, depending on how Google's social plan did. Staff who were not even working on it were told to test the products and to push them on family and friends.  [69,70]
12 [p] Google+ got an exciting start, with 10 million sign-ups in about two weeks. Page was delighted. But sign-ups are not the same as people staying. Seven years later, Google confirmed in public that 90% of visits lasted under five seconds.  [71,72]
13 [note] Sign-ups are vanity. Five-second visits are the truth. Measure what people actually do.  
14 [h] The Motorola maths  
15 [p] Next came a giant cheque. In 2011 Android looked exposed, because Apple and Microsoft had just bought a large pile of Nortel patents. So Google agreed to pay ==$12.5 billion== for Motorola Mobility, mainly for its 17,000 patents. Less than two years later, it sold the phone business to Lenovo for $2.91 billion. MIT Technology Review headlined it "Larry Page Makes Case Against His Own Motorola Purchase".  [74,75,76]
16 [p] So did he lose $9.6 billion? That is too simple. Google also sold the set-top box unit for about $2.35 billion, inherited about $3 billion of cash, and kept most of the patents. Estimates of what it truly cost run from under $1 billion to about $6.6 billion. MIT Technology Review read the sale as Google deciding it had been wrong to start building handsets.  [77,76,78,74]
17 [h] Fights with his friend Elon  
18 [p] Page and Elon Musk had been friends for over a decade. Musk often stayed at Page's home when he was in town. Their friendship is behind two of the strangest episodes in this book. Both accounts come from Musk's biographers, so read them with care.  [95,140]
19 [p] Early in 2013, Tesla was in deep trouble, with reports saying it had just two weeks of cash. Musk went to his friend. According to Ashlee Vance's biography, they talked about Google buying Tesla for about ==$6 billion==, plus $5 billion to expand its factories, with Musk running it for eight years. Page accepted the overall proposal, two people with knowledge of the deal told Vance.  [141,142]
20 [p] Then Model S orders took off, Tesla posted its first profit in May 2013, and Musk no longer needed a saviour. Tesla and Google declined to comment on it. When Vance asked, Page said he would not speculate on rumours, and added that a car company was pretty far from what Google knows.  [140,142]
21 [note] Google nearly bought Tesla for $6 billion. One good quarter ended it.  
22 [p] Their second clash was about AI. In Walter Isaacson's biography, at Musk's birthday party in 2013 the two argued over what happens if machines overtake humans. Page saw it as the next stage of evolution, and called Musk a "specist", someone biased towards his own species. Musk then tried to stop Page from buying DeepMind, and after that began hosting his own dinners on how to counter Google in AI.  [95,93]
23 [quote] Well, yes, I am pro-human. — Elon Musk's reply to Larry Page, as told to Walter Isaacson (2023) [95]
24 [h] How he kept control  
25 [p] Page and Brin had a problem. Selling their voting shares, or letting Google pay for deals with new ones, would shrink their share of the votes. The answer was Class C, a share with no vote at all. In March 2014 each shareholder received one new Class C share for each share they held.  [143,144,0]
26 [p] Shareholders sued in a Delaware court over the plan. The case settled, with a built-in penalty. If the new no-vote shares traded lower than the voting shares after a year, Google would pay holders the difference. They did trade lower, and in 2015 the payout came to ==$522 million==.  [144,145,146]
27 [p] As part of the deal, Page, Brin and Schmidt also signed agreements limiting how they could sell. None of them can sell Class C and be left holding more Class B than Class C. __Keeping control of a giant company can cost half a billion dollars, and Google paid it.__  [143,145,146]
28 [h] Alphabet and the alpha-bet  
29 [p] On 10 August 2015, Page published a blog post that reshaped the company. Google would become part of a new parent company called Alphabet, with Sundar Pichai as CEO of Google and Page as CEO of Alphabet. He explained the name in the way only he would: it was a collection of letters, and also an alpha-bet, alpha being investment return above the benchmark.  [11]
30 [quote] Alphabet is mostly a collection of companies. — Larry Page, "G is for Google" (2015) [11]
31 [p] Wall Street was pleased for a simple reason: for the first time, it could see Google's profits without the costly experiments mixed in. And the numbers were sobering. Alphabet soon scaled back Google Fiber, sold Boston Dynamics and unloaded a satellite business. Page's moonshots now had to answer to a spreadsheet.  [99,11]
32 [h] A dark chapter  
33 [p] Page's tenure also has a darker chapter, and we should state it carefully. In October 2018 The New York Times reported that Andy Rubin, the man behind Android, had left in 2014 with a $90 million exit package after Google found a misconduct claim against him credible. According to the Times, Page was the person who decided Rubin should leave, and publicly thanked him for his work.  [147]
34 [p] Rubin strongly denied the allegation. He called the report inaccurate and said he had left of his own accord. Inside Google the reaction was fury. More than 20,000 employees walked out in November 2018, in cities around the world, and Google ended forced arbitration for such claims within days.  [147,148]
35 [p] Shareholders then sued the board, naming Page and Brin among others. In 2020 Alphabet settled, without a trial, by committing ==$310 million== over up to ten years to workplace programmes, and promising tighter rules on exit payments. These were allegations, and the settlement resolved them without a court ruling on them.  [149,73,150,151]
36 [note] Your biggest mistakes may be about people, not technology. Handle those first.  
37 [h] Handing over the keys  
38 [p] On 3 December 2019, Page and Brin published a short letter. Alphabet and Google no longer needed two CEOs and a president, they wrote. Sundar Pichai would run both. Page was 46. He kept his board seat and, crucially, his votes.  [12,41]
39 [quote] we believe it's time to assume the role of proud parents—offering advice and love, but not daily nagging! — Larry Page and Sergey Brin, letter of 3 December 2019 [12]
