Some information may no longer be current. Comments Share Microsoft CEO Steve Ballmer is the kind of guy who could give the Energizer bunny an inferiority complex. A tall, stocky man with a monk-like fringe of hair and a prominent brow ridge, he gives off enough energy to power a small town, even though it is still only 6 a.m. in Seattle, which is where he was before a morning flight to address this Toronto audience. Like a manic cross between a TV preacher and a football coach, he paces the stage grabbing at the air and slamming his huge, ham-like hands together repeatedly. At one point, he pronounces a word beginning with the letter "p" with such force that you can see a fine mist of saliva floating down over the front row. He points at various members of the audience and targets them with a custom-made sermonette. Later, in a one-on-one meeting, he is so filled with missionary zeal about the world-changing nature of Microsoft Corp.'s products that he almost climbs over the table to grab a listener by the lapel, as though he could convince him of his viewpoint by sheer force of will. Ballmer, who took the reins of the world's largest consumer software company from founder Bill Gates last year, is a salesman's salesman. He has become legendary for his morale-boosting speeches to Microsoft's legions of employees - in fact, he reportedly had to have surgery on his vocal cords after he injured them by shouting. And as chief executive officer of Microsoft, Ballmer is going to need all his skills, because the company is in a battle for its life. To Microsoft watchers, handing Ballmer the reins (Gates became chairman and chief software architect) was like shifting control from one Siamese twin to the other, since the two have been virtually joined at the hip for the past 15 years. Gates was always the brain, the geek with the poor social skills who cared only about the technology; Ballmer was - and is - the can-do guy, the one who rallies the troops. Brad Silverberg, a former top executive at Microsoft, told Forbes magazine: "Bill likes smart people. Steve likes people who get shit done." Ballmer confessed during a recent speech in Toronto that he never really understood DOS - the Disk Operating System - that in 1980 launched tiny Microsoft on the road to where it is today: a company with annual sales of more than $23-billion (U.S.) and 40,000 employees. Before Windows came along, he says, "It was DOS and blah, blah, blah - I worked at Microsoft and never really figured out how to work that thing." For Ballmer, understanding a product is not important. Finding a way to sell it is. But the Microsoft CEO has a lot more on his hands than just selling software: He has to find a way to sell the new Microsoft - a company that still aims to be at the centre of the industry, but wants to be seen as a partner rather than a rapacious competitor. The legendary software maker is facing not just one, but two major threats to its future. One is the continuing antitrust lawsuit that was launched in the fall of 1998 by the U.S. Justice Department: Microsoft is appealing a ruling last summer by Judge Thomas Penfield Jackson, who found the company guilty of anticompetitive activity and ordered that it be split up. The other hurdle is a slowdown in the personal-computer market, which threatens to derail the double-digit growth Microsoft (and the stock market) has become accustomed to. In part, that slowdown - as well as a selloff in tech stocks - is responsible for a 40-per-cent drop in Microsoft's share price, to a recent $70 from a high of $120. Last year, Microsoft was the second-most-valuable company in the world with a market cap of almost $600-billion, just behind General Electric Co. Now it is worth just $375-billion (still more than Nortel Networks Corp., BCE Inc. and Canada's five big banks put together). If sheer determination can put out these twin fires, then Steve Ballmer is the man for the job. Both its supporters and its critics often describe Microsoft as a kind of cult, and if so, then Ballmer is one of its truest believers. As one Business Week writer put it, if Gates is Microsoft's "Big Brain," Ballmer is its "wildly thumping heart." He is certainly not in it for the money: while he may not be in the same league as his boss, who has a net worth of about $84-billion, Ballmer is still a staggeringly wealthy man, with a net worth estimated at $17-billion. Despite this, however, he lives in the Seattle suburb of Bellevue in a relatively modest 4,500-square-foot house, and drives an old Lincoln Continental - perhaps out of loyalty to his father, a Jewish immigrant from Switzerland who became a senior manager at Ford Motor Corp. (Ballmer grew up in the Detroit suburb of Farmington Hills). According to colleagues at Microsoft, even as CEO he still tries to leave every day about 6:30 p.m. to get home for dinner with his wife, Connie, a former Microsoft publicist, and their three young children. After dinner, he will often walk down the street to spend some time with his father, who has lung cancer. He is an assistant coach for a T-ball team one of his sons plays on, and - apart from owning a stake in the Seattle SuperSonics - friends say he does little to flaunt his wealth. Ballmer was not one of the original founders of Microsoft - the software maker was started by Gates and his high-school classmate and programming partner Paul Allen in 1977. Gates and Ballmer met when they both attended Harvard University, where they both got perfect scores on their math SATs and wound up as roommates. Gates later left Harvard to run his fledgling software company, while Ballmer finished his degree and worked briefly at Procter & Gamble Co. before going to Stanford Business School. In 1980, his Harvard pal called and asked him to join the company. At Microsoft, surrounded by the ultimate group of technology geeks, "I was the friend of Bill's who did business-y things," Ballmer recalls. At one point, he says, he worried that he had given up a promising career "just to be the accountant at a 30-person company in Bellevue, Wash." But Gates laid out his grand vision over dinner: a computer on every desk, in every home and in every office. Ballmer says he was hooked. He quit Stanford and became employee No. 30 at Microsoft. Although that vision propelled the company to the forefront of the PC revolution, Ballmer admits that "it became clear a few years ago that the vision was insufficient," because the personal-computer market was hitting critical mass - in other words, almost everyone who wanted one had one. Now, Ballmer is on the trail of a new vision: "empower people anytime, anywhere, on any device" from computers to handheld personal digital assistants, cable set-top boxes and game consoles. This means competing with everyone from Real Networks to AOL Time Warner, from Nintendo to Oracle. That's exactly the way Ballmer seems to like it. Like any good football coach (which he was at Harvard), competition is the name of the game, whether you're the underdog or the 600-pound gorilla. If anyone thought Microsoft would become a kinder, gentler company after Ballmer took over, they can think again. Even as he talks about partnering with more companies, he can't seem to suppress his competitive instincts. When an interviewer brings up Linux - a free software movement that is minuscule next to Microsoft - Ballmer says there will be no partnering. "Linux is another operating system that we compete with and we're going to do our best to compete with it," he says, his voice rising and growing noticeably louder. "It competes with Windows and our job is to BE BETTER!" he says, slamming his hands together with a ferocious smile. "That's capitalism at work!" Critics describe Microsoft's behaviour as anticompetitive and occasionally brutal in a back-alley sense. Ballmer admits Microsoft can be "rambunctious." Some analysts say the company has tried to crush the competition in whatever way possible, and that's why it has been hounded by its critics. Ballmer says the company "became the punching bag for the industry" because it "hadn't stepped forward to articulate a clear enough vision." The one time Ballmer becomes subdued - although still in a forceful way - is when the subject of the antitrust trial comes up. "There were three findings," he says. "One: Microsoft is not allowed to integrate new things into Windows; Two: Microsoft had a bad meeting with Netscape and should be punished for that. We disagree with that on the face of it - the law says if nothing bad happened as a result of the meeting, then it was not bad. And Three: Microsoft did a bunch of things which were perfectly competitive and legal, but taken together - and if it had a monopoly - then they were probably illegal." Ballmer believes that Microsoft should be allowed to add new features to Windows (which some say is an anticompetitive practice known as "tying"), and denies it did anything illegal. "We've behaved as we should - as a company that has to work hard every day to compete for the business of our customers. We're in a competitive industry, we're supposed to compete, that's the essence of capitalism." And yet Ballmer admits that there are things Microsoft could do to perhaps compete a bit less aggressively. For example, he says that when the criticism of Microsoft reached its peak during the trial, the company was surprised that more of its partners - companies that have benefited from their association with an industry giant - didn't come forward with messages of support. "We were surprised not to see more of them come forward and defend us in public," he says, "and that caused us to do some research . . . some soul searching. Why didn't we get more obvious support from the 200,000 small-and medium-sized companies we've worked with?" For Ballmer, the answer again comes down to salesmanship. "We need to create some marketing opportunities for our partners, to spend more time engaged with the venture-capital community and startups. Startups have a natural fear . . . when you start a company you actually don't know, are you a partner to Microsoft or are you a competitor to Microsoft? Some people are a little shy." Like a big St. Bernard puppy (something Ballmer has also been compared to), Microsoft just needs to calm down a little bit and people will stop being scared of it, he suggests. "We need to have an open dialogue . . . we don't have fangs." Whether the U.S. Court of Appeal agrees with this characterization of the company remains to be seen. As for the PC market, is it mature - and are Microsoft's growth rates therefore a thing of the past? That really gets Ballmer cranked. "Is the business more mature? Yes. Mature? No!" By now, he is half-standing, as his handlers try to move him out of the room to the next engagement. "Do we still have a chance to change dramatically the way people work? YES!" He's yelling now. "Have we changed the world as fundamentally as we can? The answer is absolutely not! We have an incredible opportunity to constructively help people change the world. You ask me what about growth rates . . ." He's shouting now, and waving his arms. "I say, 'What about the ways in which we're going to change the world! And if we can't make that leap to growth rates, then the heck with it!' " Then suddenly he is gone - and a rush of air goes with him, as though most of the atmosphere had been sucked out of the room.
