 Nvidia CEO Jensen Huang does Joe Rogan: We listened so you don’t have to
 The billionaire heaped praise on Trump, dismissed AI safety concerns, and talked about his childhood in a wide-ranging conversation on The Joe Rogan Experience.
 Jensen Huang wore his signature leather jacket for the occasion. | Source: Screenshot by the SF Standard 
 By Emily Shugerman Wealth & Power Reporter

 Jensen Huang, CEO of Nvidia and one of the 10 richest men in the world, has finally reached the pinnacle of tech bro success: He shared his life story on Joe Rogan.
 Clocking in at a whopping two and a half hours, Wednesday’s episode of the wildly popular podcast “The Joe Rogan Experience” delves into Huang’s journey to the U.S., his failures and foibles founding Nvidia, and what keeps him motivated after more than 30 years in the tech game. 
 Most of the episode is well-trod ground, but there are moments of genuine insight, including details about his relationship with his new BFF, President Donald Trump (opens in new tab) , whom he’s influencing on AI policy and whom he helped convince not to send the National Guard into San Francisco.
 ADVERTISEMENT
 It is a well-timed interview given Huang’s growing global stature: The day the Rogan interview dropped, he was meeting with Trump and House GOP leadership about a bill that would rein in Nvidia’s ability to sell chips to China.

 Fans of Rogan’s podcast, however, didn’t seem especially impressed by the choice of guests. One of the top comments on Spotify reads, “Oh my god, what timing. Waiting for the AI and crypto bubble to burst as we speak.” Another commenter called the interview “another bow down to the tech oligarchy.”
 Read on for five takeaways from Huang’s appearance, so you can decide for yourself whether Rogan unearthed new revelations, or — as one colorful commenter put it — his show has turned into “the Billionaire Suck Off Podcast.” 
 Huang heaped praise on Trump 
 The guest and host reminisced about a time when Huang was hanging out with Trump, and the president suggested they make a spur-of-the-moment phone call to Rogan, just to say hi. “He’s like a kid,” Rogan said, laughing. “A 79-year-old kid.” 
 Huang, notably, did not agree with this statement but did have laudatory things to say about the president, including that he is “an incredibly good listener.” (“Almost everything I’ve ever said to him, he’s remembered,” he said, implausibly.) 
 “Almost every time he explains something and he says something, he starts with his … love for America,” he said. “And everything that he thinks through is very practical and very common sense.”
 A possible reason for this high praise – besides Huang’s vested interest in selling more AI chips — came to light a moment later, when he told Rogan how he was introduced to Trump by Secretary of Commerce Howard Lutnick.
 “He started our conversation with, ‘Jensen, this is Secretary Lutnick,” Huang recalled. “‘I just want to let you know that you’re a national treasure. Nvidia is a national treasure. And whenever you need access to the president, the administration, you call us.’”
 “And it was completely true,” he added. “Every single time I called, if I needed something, I want to get something off my chest, express some concern, they’re always available.”
 A pretty nice arrangement, if you can get it!

 Shocker! Huang has an optimistic outlook on the future of AI 
 Some CEOs have a mixed perspective — Elon Musk said on Rogan’s podcast that advanced AI poses “a 20% chance of annihilation” — but Huang remains almost unblinkingly positive.
 “There are always a lot of people who are quite concerned,” he said when Rogan pushed him on the possible risks of advanced AI. “And so if history is a guide, it is the case that all of this concern is channeled into making the technology safer.”
 When questioned about artificial general intelligence’s potential to create mass job loss — or, you know, kill us all — Huang brushed off the concerns as “far-fetched.” 
 At one point, he laughed when Rogan described a scenario in which humans could no longer control AI and it attempted to harm them.
 “Is that funny?” Rogan said.
 “No, I just think it’s not going to happen,” Huang replied.
 On job loss, he was slightly more nuanced, saying society will have to become more focused on the purpose of a job, rather than the tasks it entails. “If your job is just to chop vegetables, Cuisinart is going to replace you,” he said. “So people have to find meaning in other things. Your job has to be more than the task.”
 He suggested that AI will create new industries, like manufacturing clothes for robots. Sure.
 But money can’t buy you total peace of mind 
 Huang spent a good portion of the podcast tracking Nvidia’s up-and-down journey over the last 30 years, including several times he was convinced the company was going to run out of money. Even now, he said, he can’t shake the feeling that it is always moments away from failure.
 “The feeling [at the beginning] is no different than the feeling I had this morning when I woke up: that you’re going to be out of business soon,” he said. “The sense of vulnerability, the sense of uncertainty, the sense of insecurity – it doesn’t leave you.”
 He said he works seven days a week and estimated that he reads thousands of emails a day, waking up at 4 a.m. to get it all done. (His children, Madison and Spencer, work at Nvidia and also decline to take days off, he said.)
 This motivation comes not from wanting any kind of great success but from being terrified of falling on his face. Huang said he exists “always in a state of anxiety.”
 “I have a greater drive from not wanting to fail than the drive of wanting to succeed,” he admitted.
 He detailed how he started working with OpenAI 
 Nvidia and OpenAI are hammering out a $ 100 billion partnership (opens in new tab) .  But the relationship wasn’t always so flashy.
 According to Huang, he was introduced to OpenAI by Musk, a cofounder who has since become one of the company’s biggest critics. He and Musk had worked together on the technology for some of the first self-driving cars, and when Huang developed a “supercomputer” in 2016, Musk volunteered to be one of his first customers.
 Huang recalled being thrilled until Musk revealed that the company for which he wanted to buy the technology was a nonprofit. “All the blood drained out of my face,” he said. “I spent just a few billion dollars building this thing that costs $300,000. And the chances of a nonprofit being able to pay for this thing is approximately zero.”
 Of course, the nonprofit turned out to be OpenAI, and Huang wound up doing just fine.
 He gave details about his childhood – including that he tried and quit smoking at age 9 
 Huang’s challenging childhood is part of his mystique, and he often uses it to tout the possibilities of the American dream. He was born in Taiwan, moved to Thailand, and immigrated to the U.S. with his brother at age 9. His parents were two years behind them, so in the meantime, the brothers were sent to a boarding school in Kentucky.
 Huang added humorous color in his conversation with Rogan, saying that one of the first things he noticed about his Southern Baptist boarding school was the number of cigarette butts on the ground. “100% of the kids smoked,” he said.
 His roommate, who was 17, was “the toughest kid in school,” and in order to fit in, the 9-year-old Huang bought a pack of cigarettes and learned to light up. After a few weeks, however, the habit got too expensive for his meager allowance.
 “I had a quarter a month or something like that,” he said. “I would just rather buy popsicles and fudgesicles with it.”
 Since he didn’t have enough money for international phone calls, he and his brother would sit in front of a tape recorder every month and recount their experiences. Then they would mail the tape to their parents in Thailand, who would listen, record their own diary entries, and send it right back.
 “I recorded this [one entry], I said, ‘Mom and Dad, we went to the most amazing restaurant,” he recalled. “This whole place is lit up. It’s like the future. And the food comes in a box. And the food is incredible. The hamburger is incredible.”
 He added wryly: “It was McDonald’s.”
