 Scared of an AI future? This is the man quietly driving it
 Jensen Huang’s brainchild, Nvidia, has had astronomic success making microchips. Stephen Witt’s The Thinking Machine tells the inside story
 4/5

 CEO of Nvidia, Jensen Huang, in 2025
 Credit : AFP
 Tom Knowles
 Published 
 06 April 2025 11:15am BST
 For everyone present, it was an excruciating scene. An employee at Nvidia, the microchip designer, was being screamed at by the company’s chief executive Jensen Huang in front of roughly 150 colleagues. Jensen was, one person present recalled, “just nonstop berating him for a good hour and a half. Honestly, maybe it was two hours.”
 The employee, an “extraordinarily skilled and dedicated chip architect”, had just experienced what Nvidia staff called “the Wrath of Huang”. The mild-mannered looking boss, known in public for his signature style of a leather jacket and square-framed glasses, is prone to explosive outbursts at work. The telling-off in front of others was not accidental either. “He wants everyone to benefit,” as the company’s former chief scientist put it. “He would never just yell at some guy in the hall.”
 Yet, as journalist Stephen Witt shows in The Thinking Machine – his deeply researched, illuminating and often rather funny book on Nvidia (pronounced IN-vidia, not NUH-vidia) – staff love Huang. In fact, they worship him. They see Huang “not just as a leader but as a prophet,” Witt tells us. Since it was founded in 1993, Huang has taken Nvidia from a largely ignored company providing graphic cards for video gamers to a three trillion-dollar firm that provides the chips for almost every AI tool being used today. In the process Huang has become one of the most important players – perhaps the most important – in the booming AI industry.
 Huang, who was born in Taiwan in 1963 but moved to the US at the age of 10, has clear parallels with Elon Musk. Both have a workaholic nature, undoubted engineering skills and expect constant hard work from their staff. Indeed, since 2020, all Nvidia employees have had to email Huang a weekly list of five of the most important things they are working on – an idea you wonder whether Musk adopted for his now infamous federal workers email missive.
 But for such a crucial man and company, Huang and Nvidia are still nowhere near as recognised as Musk and Tesla; nor, say, Gates and Microsoft. That’s partly because Nvidia’s main product is its graphics-processing unit, a circuit board with a powerful microchip at its core, and computer chips are, for the most part, quite boring to read about – a problem which The Thinking Machine sometimes struggles to counteract. While Witt does an excellent job attempting to break down terms such as “parallel computing”, “neural networks” and “GPUs”, this book is unlikely to appeal to readers with only a passing interest in tech.
 Huang and Musk at a technology conference in California
 Credit : Getty 
 Still, for those wanting an engaging and revealing insight into what Nvidia’s journey to becoming one of most vital firms of the modern tech industry has been like, The Thinking Machine is unrivalled. Before AI, Nvidia’s life as a producer of “graphic accelerators” – for gamers who wanted the best playing experience on their PCs – nearly failed at the start, their first two products being a commercial disaster. To avoid going bankrupt, the company had to create an entirely new chip as cheaply and quickly as possible. Ever since, the unofficial corporate mantra at Nvidia has been: “Our company is 30 days from going out of business”.
 That mindset is likely to have come in handy in recent months. Unfortunately for Witt, whose book was finished too early to cover any of this, Nvidia suffered the largest stock market slump in history in January, wiping $589 billion off its market value in one day. The sharp fall was caused by the Chinese startup DeepSeek, who had released an AI model to rival those developed by American tech giants at a much smaller cost and without using Nvidia’s chips.
 But The Thinking Machine still clearly demonstrates why this selloff is likely overblown. Not only does Nvidia still dominate much of the AI chip market – in one incident we see Elon Musk begging for more of its chips at a sushi restaurant in Palo Alto – but Huang has always seemed to have his eye on what’s next.
 The Thinking Machine is published by Bodley Head
 Credit : Getty
 For the company would not be the success it is today if Huang had not made a huge gamble. Instead of simply focusing on its successful core customer base of gamers, Nvidia began investing billions of dollars into CUDA, a supercomputing software package its customers could download from 2006 onwards. For years, no one could find much use for CUDA and its incredible computing power – until 2012, when three AI researchers realised that CUDA was the ideal tool to train their system to recognise images. Huang knew this kind of AI would change the world. It was as if he’d been at the prow of a ship scanning for land and “had found Atlantis”.
 The bet paid off, and Nvidia’s chips have now become an essential part of most of the world’s AI infrastructure. By 2020 the gross profit margins Nvidia made on its AI chips could exceed 90 per cent. “All of this money, all of this talent, all of this innovation… pass through a single corporate siphon… Nvidia,” Witt writes. It sounds like it will be a while before that set-up radically changes.
 The Thinking Machine is published by Bodley Head at £25. To order your copy, call 0330 173 0523 or visit Telegraph Books
