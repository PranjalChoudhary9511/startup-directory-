 Nvidia Cofounder’s Foundation Is Now Bigger than MacArthur
 Michael Kavate | January 27, 2026 
 Jen-Hsun Huang, NVIDIA&#039;s founder, president and CEO. Credit: glen photo/Shutterstock
 You won’t hear its name on NPR. There’s no prestigious fellowship associated with it. It doesn’t even have a website.
 But the foundation of Nvidia cofounder and CEO Jensen Huang and his wife, Lori Huang, is now at least as big as — if not much larger than — one of the most recognizable brands in institutional philanthropy: the MacArthur Foundation .
 The Jen-Hsun & Lori Huang Foundation reported $9.2 billion in assets as of the end of 2024, according to its latest 990. That’s just below the $9.3 billion endowment recorded by the Chicago-based grantmaker whose MacArthur Fellowships, often dubbed its “Genius Grants,” get international press coverage. 
 Yet AI companies’ insatiable demand for computer chips has almost definitely pushed the Huang foundation’s assets well beyond those of its 55-year-old legacy peer over the past year. Its endowment is made up primarily of Nvidia stock , which is up nearly a third since the end of 2024, likely putting its assets close to $12 billion. At that size, it would rival the Gordon and Betty Moore Foundation, and trail not all that far behind the Ford Foundation and its $17 billion endowment — especially when one considers Jensen Huang’s roughly $162 billion net worth.
 Not that there’s any sense of competition, at least in the eyes of the MacArthur Foundation’s leader.
 “I hope that they manage to do EVEN MORE GOOD in the world than the now-smaller MacArthur Foundation!” wrote the latter’s president, John Palfrey, on LinkedIn. “We welcome people passing us — and giving MORE MONEY out to support good works.”
 That had not happened yet as of 2024, when the Huangs were still granting far less ($126 million) than MacArthur ($356 million). Their foundation’s rapidly rising assets likely prompted their grantmaking to follow suit in 2025, but we’ll have to wait until next year’s filings to be sure, barring unexpected new public disclosures from the Huangs.
 What do Jensen and Lori Huang fund?
 There’s actually no telling where most of the Huang’s grant dollars go, or if they reach working charities at all. Two-thirds of their foundation’s $126 million payout in 2024, or $82 million, went to a donor-advised fund, DAFgiving360, formerly known as Schwab Charitable Fund. Due to such accounts’ limited transparency and lack of payout requirements, it’s impossible to say where that money went next, or if it has yet been granted.
 On the plus side, the share of the foundation’s annual payout that the Huangs send to DAFs has fallen, at least from 2022. In that year, I ranked Jensen Huang among the group I dubbed the Black Box Billionaires , a group of ultra-rich donors who send more than 80% of their foundation payout to DAFs. Huang had sent 83%. In 2024, that rate dropped to 65%. 
 Among what is visible, there are a few big winners: the couple’s alma mater and a couple mental health nonprofits. Not counting the DAF transfers, the Huangs made a total of 13 grants to nonprofits in 2024. As in prior years, by far the biggest beneficiary was the institution where the couple met, Oregon State University ($30.5 million, three grants). They have given the college tens of millions annually in recent years.
 Other major awards put the Huangs on the path to becoming mental health megadonors. Big awards went to Crisis Text Line ($10.8 million, two grants) and Mental Health Innovations ($1.7 million). Both had been prior grantees, but the 2024 grants were several orders of magnitude bigger than prior awards.
 Only a few other awards broke six figures, including the food bank and repeat grantee Second Harvest of Silicon Valley ($516,711), as well as Doctors without Borders ($100,000) and College of Adaptive Arts ($100,000).

 What’s next for the Huangs?
 This MacArthur-sized milestone is the latest on a long skyward climb for the Jen-Hsun & Lori Huang Foundation. Thanks to the success of Nvidia over the past decade, an operation with total assets of $150 million in 2016 not only granted nearly that much in 2024 but also now has an endowment about 800 times that size.
 What has not changed much, so far, is the couple’s grantmaking style. Dating back to my colleague Philip Rojc’s 2021 article on the Huangs, they have favored Oregon State, a few other universities (Stanford, Johns Hopkins) and a handful of West Coast nonprofits, with only occasional ventures outside those usual suspects.
 That will almost definitely have to change. Under the 5% payout rule, their foundation will need to get about $600 million out the door each year, putting it in the company of other centibillionaire-funded foundations with grant totals soaring past top legacy funders . Hard to imagine doing that without finding a few new grantees, which suggests several possible paths for the Huangs.
 Perhaps they will turn to intermediaries, whether in climate, health or another field, that allow them to make large awards that can be parceled out by others. Many of their fellow tech megadonors participate in what are basically platforms for collaborative billionaire funding, such as The Audacious Project, Blue Meridian Partners or Lever for Change.
 Maybe they’ll seek to make large endowment awards to universities and other institutions that can hold Nvidia stock and benefit from future gains. Past reporting from Bloomberg News suggests their DAF transfers allowed them to hold onto Nvidia stock while fulfilling their foundation’s payout requirements. They could, instead, let working nonprofits be the beneficiaries.
 Technically, it’s possible that they will decide to staff up and build an operation more akin to that of a Gates or Bloomberg. Yet this seems unlikely. As with most tech donors, theirs has always been an ultra-lean operation. The foundation lacks not only official staff but any substantial accounting or financial management expenses.
 Finally, they could simply ramp up their DAF giving. Moving ever-larger chunks of Nvidia stock from their foundation’s balance sheet to that of their DAF would fulfill the letter of the law, if arguably not its spirit. 
 The choices the Huangs make to meet those monster payout figures will give us another snapshot of what’s ahead for this new tech megaphilanthropy. Yet what we already know underlines the strange realities of this New Gilded Age . A foundation that’s never made more than 15 awards in a year now sits on one of the largest endowments on the planet — and we can’t even say where most of its donations go.
