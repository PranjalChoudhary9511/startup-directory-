0 He earned $18,000 selling newspaper subscriptions in one year. :: Dell said "about $18,000 in one year" in his 2008 interview, and Encyclopedia.com agrees. The Christian Science Monitor (1988) says $18,000 over two years. We use his own account.
1 He sold $80,000 a month by the end of his freshman year. :: The Christian Science Monitor and the Academy of Achievement both say $80,000 a month. Forbes's profile says he grossed $80,000 by the end of freshman year, and another Academy passage says $50,000 to $80,000 in upgrades and components without a time period. Not used on this card; chapter writers should choose the monthly figure and say so.
2 Age when he started the company. :: The foundation's bio says he founded it at 19 in 1984. The Academy dates the registration to January 1984, when he was 18, and the incorporation to May 1984, when he was 19. The glance entry gives both dates.
3 Company name history. :: The Academy says the business was registered as PC's Limited, but also says it was incorporated in May 1984 as Dell Computer Corporation and renamed in 1988. The Franklin Institute also gives 1988. The Goodwill Computer Museum label on Commons says PC's Limited became Dell Computer Corp. in 1987. We do not state a renaming date on this card.
4 Age when he bought and took apart his first Apple computer. :: The Academy of Achievement says 15, while the Christian Science Monitor says 16 and some secondary sources say 14. Not used on this card.
5 Head-office ZIP code. :: Dell's US contact page lists 1 Dell Way, Round Rock, TX 78664. The 10-K and investor relations page list One Dell Way, Round Rock, Texas 78682. We use the SEC filing.
6 Where he first ran the business. :: The Daily Texan (2016) names Dobie 2713, while a book-summary site names a different dormitory. We use the Daily Texan. The Academy adds that he registered the company while operating from a condominium, so the dorm was only the first stage.
7 Age when he bought and took apart his first Apple II. :: The Academy of Achievement, Franklin Institute, Encyclopedia.com and EBSCO say 15. The Christian Science Monitor says 16. The Founders Podcast reading says his 14th birthday, and the host says 13 at one point. We use 15, which fits the 1980 Steve Jobs talk and Encyclopedia.com's dating of his IBM purchase to the following year, 1981.
8 He earned $18,000 from newspaper subscriptions in one year. :: Dell says "about $18,000 in one year" in 2008. The Christian Science Monitor says $18,000 in two years. The Founders reading of his memoir says a little over $18,000 that summer. We use his recorded interview.
9 How he reached the newlyweds and home buyers. :: Dell, the Monitor and Encyclopedia.com describe direct mail or letters. The Chicago Tribune says the names were called instead of cold lists. We describe mail.
10 Age and method of the stamp business. :: Encyclopedia.com, the Monitor and Texas Monthly say 12; the Handbook of Texas says 13. The Monitor says he auctioned stamps off, while Encyclopedia.com and the Tribune say he bypassed auctions by selling to collectors directly, which is what we describe. The Monitor says he made $2,000 in a matter of months and the Tribune says days, so we give no duration.
11 Which teacher was upset by the tax return. :: Dell says his government economics teacher. Dell's Exceptional magazine says economics teacher, and Reference for Business says history teacher. We use his words.
12 How long he stayed away from the business after promising his parents. :: Dell says about ten days (2008) and eight or nine days (2011). Encyclopedia.com says about three weeks and Reference for Business says a few weeks. We use about ten days.
13 Where the $1,000 came from. :: The Chicago Tribune says it was left over from subscription earnings after the BMW and that it bought parts for orders already taken. The Statesman's report of his 2011 talk says he sold more computers to raise it to meet the state's incorporation minimum. Encyclopedia.com and Reference for Business also call it the required minimum. We use the Statesman version and treat the amount itself as well established.
14 What he was like as a young student. :: EBSCO calls him a top student at Herod Elementary. Dell says of his schooling, "I was an okay student." We give both.
15 Dating of the incorporation. :: Encyclopedia.com prints the week before finals in May 1983, which is an obvious slip: Dell, the Statesman and Forbes all place this in May 1984, and we use 1984.
16 His roommates dumping his computer parts. :: Reference for Business places this at his first apartment in Austin, without a date. Other sources say he lived in the Dobie Center dorm first and moved to a condominium or larger apartment later. We report the incident without saying which residence.
17 When he told his parents the truth. :: The Academy profile says he broke the news by spring vacation, when sales were at $80,000 a month. The Monitor says $80,000 was reached by the end of the school year. We use the Monitor for the monthly figure and the Academy for the spring-break timing.
18 Company name history. :: The Statesman says the May 1984 papers were filed as Dell Computer Corp. doing business as PC's Limited, which reconciles the Academy's two statements. The later public rename is dated 1987 by the Handbook of Texas and 1988 by the Academy. That date falls outside this part and is not stated here.
19 The size of the 1988 IPO. :: Dell's own timeline and Reuters say about $30 million at a market value of $85 million. The company-history reference says 3.5 million shares at $8.50, which is $29.75 million. The Harvard case says about 4 million shares, over $34 million, about a quarter of the stock, with net proceeds of about $31 million, and those figures imply a far higher market value than $85 million. We use about $30 million and $85 million. His stake at the IPO is "more than half" in the Harvard case and "around 73%" per the host of the Founders Podcast; we use the former.
20 The $500,000 family loan. :: This rests on one secondary source: the Harvard case, citing Aronoff and Ward, "Michael Dell", p. 121. We found no first-person confirmation and no primary record. It is attributed in the text. A fact-checker should try to confirm it.
21 Dell's private placement was the only financing to survive Black Monday. :: This is Dell's own claim in his memoir, known to us through an auto-generated podcast reading. The Harvard case confirms only that the placement closed after the crash and raised more than $21 million. Presented as his claim.
22 The 87,000 percent stock rise. :: The Harvard case applies it to the decade of the 1990s. Texas Monthly applies the same figure to the period from the 1988 IPO to the March 2000 peak. A secondary finance site gives 64,488 percent from IPO to peak. We use the Harvard wording and call it "one Harvard count".
23 Olympic's end date and whether it reached the market. :: Dell's own Hemispheres article says the project began in 1988, was shown as prototypes in November 1989, and was shut before products saw the light of day. One Encyclopedia.com entry says it was "introduced in 1990". The company history says it was abandoned around 1990. We follow Dell's account.
24 The size of the 1989 chip and Olympic hit to profit. :: The company history says 1990 profits fell 65 percent to $5 million. The Stanford case gives profit of $27.2 million for "1990", which appears to be a later fiscal-year label. We use the company-history figure and describe it without a fiscal-year claim.
25 When Dell left retail. :: The company history says late 1993. The Stanford case says mid-1994. Encyclopedia.com, Reference for Business and strategy+business say 1994. We use 1994.
26 The 1994 fiscal-year loss. :: Most sources say $36 million, the Stanford case $35.8 million, and CFO magazine $39 million. We use $36 million.
27 The 1992 currency-trading questions. :: Kidder Peabody's analyst raised questions about the accounting and said he was not making allegations; Dell's officials denied the implication. The Wall Street Journal abstract says the SEC was examining Dell's books for a planned offering, and that Dell itself approached the SEC about unusual trading. We found no record of any finding against Dell on this, and the text does not suggest one.
28 When Dell.com launched. :: Encyclopedia.com says 1994. Kraemer and Dedrick say the site was created in 1995 after experiments that began in 1994. Dell's online sales in 2000 are given as $50 million a day by Encyclopedia.com and Kraemer, and $35 million a day by Texas Monthly. We use 1994 for the start of the project and $50 million.
29 How much MSD Capital started with. :: The Wall Street Journal's 1998 report says "resources of $1 billion". Its 2019 report and The Real Deal say $400 million. Both are given.
30 DellHost's end in 2001. :: The Dutch trade publication Computable reported a sale to Sprint. Computerworld, on the same day, described an alliance and said no joint venture was involved. We say Dell was "pulling out of hosting through a deal with Sprint" and attribute it to press reports. We found no reliable closing dates for Gigabuys or Dellnet.
31 Battery recall size and rank. :: Most reports say 4.1 million batteries; InfoWorld says 4.2 million. We use about 4.1 million. The statement that the US safety regulator called it the largest electronics recall it had handled comes from search summaries of August 2006 reports; the BetaNews and eWeek pages refused automated reading, so a fact-checker should confirm it on the regulator's site. Sources also differ on when HP first passed Dell: Gartner and IDC data put it in the third quarter of 2006 (IDC called that a statistical tie), while a Reuters timeline says the fourth quarter. We use the third quarter and note the close margin.
32 Faulty capacitor costs. :: Fortune and InfoWorld report a $450 million charge in 2005 covering capacitors, excess stock and layoffs. The New York Times report on the lawsuit says $300 million covered the computers. The text uses "about $450 million, largely over faulty capacitors", following Fortune. The lawsuit's claims were disputed by Dell and the case settled on undisclosed terms, so no court ruled on them.
33 The 2013 buyout's value and his contribution. :: The original deal was $24.4 billion at $13.65, and the final deal $24.9 billion at $13.75 plus $0.13. Fortune says $500 million of new cash from Dell and 70 percent ownership; Forbes says $750 million and 75 percent. The Chancery opinion reconciles these: up to $500 million from him, up to $250 million from an affiliate, and 74.9 percent ownership. We use the court's figures. The total package is given as $13.88 in Dell's press release, excluding the regular dividend, and $13.96 in the court's opinion, including it. His rolled shares are valued at $3.7 billion in one table and "roughly $4 billion" or "more than $3 billion" elsewhere.
34 Whether $13.75 was a fair price. :: The Court of Chancery set fair value at $17.62 in 2016. The Delaware Supreme Court reversed in 2017 and sent the case back, saying the deal price deserved heavy weight. We could not verify the final figure on remand, so the text does not state one. The text says the trial judge found the process would not support a claim that the company's leaders broke their duties, relying on Wilson Sonsini's summary of the 2016 opinion. Writers should treat the fairness of the price as debated, as we do.
35 How the EMC deal's price is stated. :: Dell and most press say about $67 billion at announcement. Institutional Investor says $65.7 billion. Forbes's profile says a "$60 billion merger", which we think refers to the value at closing after the fall in the tracking stock's price. We use about $67 billion.
36 Michael Dell's VMware payout. :: CRN, quoting a VMware SEC filing, says 169,278,015 shares, $12.06 billion cash and 21,329,029 Broadcom shares, totalling $21.65 billion at that date. Other outlets said about $24 billion at announcement. Forbes's profile says Broadcom paid $69 billion, 39 percent of it going to Dell, and the Financial Times reported a higher deal value at closing. We use the filing figures and do not know how many Broadcom shares he still holds.
37 The price of SonicWall and Dell Software. :: Dell never disclosed the SonicWall price. Analysts estimated $1 billion to $1.5 billion, and the $1.2 billion figure comes from The Wall Street Journal as reported by others. The 2016 sale of the software group was reported at about $2 billion or just over $2 billion; terms were not disclosed. The 3PAR bid sequence comes from TAdviser, a less established source, while the $33 final price and $72 million fee are confirmed by other press.
38 Forbes's net worth figure. :: It is an estimate. Forbes shows $228.5 billion on 2 November 2026, and the Dell-stock portion is calculated from public filings, while the remainder cannot be checked because DFO Management is private. A one-day Forbes move of $4.6 billion on a 1.9 percent share rise in September 2026 implies Forbes counts other holdings that move with the market, which we could not break down. His stake is also reported as "about 40 percent" in the press, 40.9 percent of all common stock in the proxy, and 45.7 percent in a Schedule 13G that uses a different denominator.
39 Whether Jobs and Dell "stayed friends". :: This is Dell's own account in 2021 and 2011. Steve Jobs's side is not available, and a widely repeated profane reply by Jobs in 1997 is reported only by Business Insider as "reportedly", so we left it out.
40 Exact wording of the 1997 Apple remark. :: CNN and TechCrunch quote "I'd shut it down and give the money back to the shareholders." The Charter/TIME review of his memoir prints "I'd shut the company down and give the money back to shareholders." We use the version in contemporary press accounts. He has said it was misconstrued (2011) and, per the review, now calls it stupid.
41 Why the 'Dude' campaign ended. :: Dell's marketing chief said in 2003 the character was dropped in fall 2002 because ads were becoming about Steven, months before the actor's 2003 arrest. Other accounts (TechRadar, PCMag, Business Insider) link the end of his contract to the arrest. We use the executive's on-record account and leave the arrest out, as it is not needed for the story.
42 How many laptop batteries were recalled in 2006. :: Associated Press says 4.1 million, Forbes says 4.2 million. We use 4.1 million.
43 Size of the City of New York batch in the capacitor case. :: The New York Times says 20.2% of 5,000 computers; VentureBeat, summarising the Times, says 2,000. Neither figure is printed in the chapter, only the study percentages.
44 Who paid the $1 billion Class V settlement. :: Fortune says Dell Technologies paid. Dell's SEC filings say the company and/or its insurers paid on behalf of the defendants under indemnification obligations, and expected $106 million back from insurers. We use the filings.
45 Buyout price in 2013. :: Often given as $13.65 a share. The Delaware Supreme Court uses $13.75, the final deal price with the added consideration. We use $13.75 with the court.
46 His net worth. :: Forbes showed $228.5 billion and no. 4 on 2 November 2026. Other pieces from the same weeks cite Bloomberg with different figures and ranks (for example fifth, about $241 billion, in October 2026). Dates and methods differ. We use Forbes throughout.
47 How much of Dell Technologies he owns. :: Fortune (2024) said 53% in his or Susan's name; Chosun Biz (2026) says he and DFO hold 40%. The 2026 proxy text we read says only that he holds more than 50% of total voting power in director elections. We state the voting figure only.
48 Total giving. :: Their own April 2026 fact sheet says more than $10 billion in commitments. Other reporting cites over $3 billion given by the foundation. Commitments and money paid out are different things. We state "commitments, by their own count."
49 ZIP code threshold for the $250 gift. :: The White House said in December 2025 that it applied below $150,000 median income. The Dells' final figure in October 2026 is $117,300 median family income, because the money reached 25 million children at that level. We use the final figure.
50 Jeff Clarke's title. :: An Investing.com summary of the September 2026 earnings call calls him "Chief Executive." The Next Platform and Dell Technologies World 2026 materials give chief operating officer and vice chairman, and Forbes and the Goldman Sachs transcript give Michael Dell as CEO. We treat the summary as an error.
51 Dell's phone history. :: McKinsey's note says Venue phones ran 2010 to 2012. Fortune mentions a 'phablet' dropped after one year. These may refer to different products; we use McKinsey's.
52 AI replacing workers. :: His 2024 and 2026 statements point in different directions. We quote both and do not claim he has formally changed his view.
