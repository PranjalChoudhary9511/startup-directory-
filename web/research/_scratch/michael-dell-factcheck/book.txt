
===== [0] origins | Ages 0–12 | The Boy in the Red Bathrobe
0 p: A woman from a testing company turned up at a house in the suburbs of Houston, Texas. She said she was there to see "Mr. Michael Dell." His mother told her he was taking a bath, and that she would let him know he had a visitor. Then Mr. Dell appeared. He was ==eight years old==, still damp, and wearing a red terrycloth bathrobe. {27}
1 p: Here is how that strange meeting came about. Michael had seen an ad in one of his parents' magazines. It promised a high school diploma if you passed "one simple test." Texas Monthly says he clipped the form from the back of the magazine and applied. He was in third grade, and he wanted to cut about nine years off his schooling so he could get on with business. {27,28,0}
2 Q: "When I was in third grade, I sent away for a high school diploma." -- Michael Dell, the opening line of his 1999 book Direct from Dell {29}
3 note: Eight years old. Already trying to skip the middleman.
4 p: The plan failed, of course. He was too young, and he stayed in school like everyone else. But when he told the story decades later, he did not treat it as a cute joke. He treated it as the first sign of how his brain worked: "I've always been fascinated with eliminating unnecessary steps." __The idea that later built his company, cut out every step that does not help, first shows up in a child trying to skip nine years of school.__ {27,33,37}
5 h: A house that talked about money
6 p: Michael Saul Dell was born on ==23 February 1965== in Houston. His father, Alexander, was an orthodontist. His mother, Lorraine, became a stockbroker and financial consultant. He was the middle child, with one older brother and one younger brother. {0,22}
7 p: His parents were not from Texas. In his 2021 memoir, Play Nice But Win, Dell writes that they moved from New York City to Houston in the 1960s because his father had heard the city was rich with opportunity. His father put his office in a building right next to the synagogue. Dell jokes that if you were Jewish and your teeth were crooked, there was a good chance his dad had fixed them. He also says his father became the city's top orthodontist mainly by outworking everyone else. {32}
8 p: So this is not a rags-to-riches story, and you should know that up front. The family lived in an upper-middle-class part of west Houston. Dell himself says he worked as a child for a different reason: "I didn't actually have any need for income as a child, but I felt compelled to earn an income to fund my hobbies." He had a head start. What he did with it is the interesting part. {28,0}
9 note: Not a poor kid. He worked because he wanted to.
10 p: His mother was the key. As Dell tells it, she was brilliant at maths and finance, and she became so good at investing in stocks and real estate that she turned it into a career as a stockbroker. Business and economics came up again and again at the family table. In a 2011 talk at the University of Texas, he described what an orthodontist father and a financial consultant mother meant in practice: a home full of conversation about money and science. {32,22,36}
11 Q: "My parents were always talking about the economy and the stock market, and the oil crisis back in the '70s." -- Michael Dell, Academy of Achievement interview, 2008 {0}
12 example: For example: the magazines lying around the Dell home were Forbes, Fortune and Barron's, along with huge Value Line books full of pages of data on single companies. Dell says he soaked it all in. Texas Monthly adds one more detail about how early this started: at ==nine==, he got his own checkbook. {32,28}
13 p: The family had a saying for when the brothers went out to play street ball with their friends: play nice, but win. Decades later, Dell used it as the title of his memoir. Soon his heroes were business people, above all the founders who challenged the way things were done and built something from nothing. {32,0}
14 p: But all that business talk did not change what the family expected of him. Michael was supposed to become a doctor. He explained it in one flat sentence that many Indian students will know by heart. {0,33}
15 Q: "My father's a doctor, my brother's a doctor, lots of doctors in the family, so I was going to be a doctor." -- Michael Dell, Academy of Achievement interview, 2008 {0}
16 note: Sound familiar? Hold on to this line. It comes back at Thanksgiving.
17 h: The kid who opened things
18 p: At home, he was the child who took things apart. Telephones, radios, televisions, whatever was electronic and within reach. "I liked to kind of see how it worked and sometimes I'd put them back together too," he says. Notice the word sometimes. He cared more about understanding the thing than about returning it in one piece. {0}
19 p: One reference biography says he bought his first calculator at seven, with money from small jobs. He went to Herod Elementary School in Houston. The same biography calls him a top student there, but Dell remembers it differently: "I was an okay student." He says he did better in maths and science, and only when a subject interested him. He read science and science fiction, and he remembers reading Scientific American whenever it came. {34,0}
20 note: An "okay student" by his own account. Keep that in mind.
21 h: Twelve years old, and busy
22 p: At ==12==, he got his first real job, as a dishwasher in a Chinese restaurant. He was promoted to water boy, and then to assistant maître d', the person who greets and seats guests. "So I was moving up pretty quickly in the Chinese restaurant business," he says, enjoying the joke. Then a Mexican restaurant recruited him away. He was 12 or 13, and employers were already competing for him. {0,37}
23 p: That same stretch brought his first look at a computer. In seventh grade he was in an advanced maths class, and his teacher's room got the school's first teletype terminal. This was before personal computers. You typed a program, sent it off to a distant mainframe, and the answer came back. He was hooked, and he stayed after school to program it. {0,34}
24 p: Then there was the ride home. His route on the bike took him past a RadioShack that sat between his junior high school and his house. Inside was a very simple personal computer. He would hang out in the store and wish that one day he could buy one. Every founder story has a moment like this, and it is usually smaller than you would expect. {0}

===== [1] growing-up | Ages 13–18 | Marriage Licences and a BMW
0 p: It began with stamps. Around the age of 12, Michael was collecting them when he noticed that prices were rising. The usual way to sell was through an auction, with an auctioneer in the middle. So he asked a simple question. Why not go straight to the collectors? {22,21}
1 p: So he built a ==12-page catalogue== of his own stamps and his friends' stamps, typing it himself with one finger. He got the names of people who took part in stamp show auctions and mailed the catalogue to them. He also bought an ad in a stamp collectors' magazine, under the name Dell's Stamps. Then the orders came in, and the money with them: ==$2,000==. {27,22,32,21}
2 example: For example: he did not need to own every stamp a customer wanted. When orders came from collectors who wanted to skip the hassle of an auction, he went out and found the stamps afterwards. A Chicago Tribune writer spotted the link in 1999: this was the same order-first, build-later model people later met when they called Dell for a computer. {27}
3 note: Order first, find the stamps after. Remember this when you reach the factory.
4 p: __He was cutting out the middleman before he was old enough to drive.__ This is why the stamp story matters more than the $2,000. A 12-year-old had found out two things. People will pay for skipping the hassle, and a seller who hears from the buyer first does not have to guess. {27,22}
5 h: At 13: real money
6 p: Most 13-year-olds do not read commodity reports. Michael did. Living with a mother immersed in stocks and bonds, he became interested in currencies, interest rates and commodity prices. Then he began investing in stocks and precious metals using what he earned from his jobs. {0}
7 Q: "Kind of an odd thing for a 13-year-old to be doing, but I found it interesting." -- Michael Dell, Academy of Achievement interview, 2008 {0}
8 p: The interviewer asked the question anyone would ask. Was this real money? "Yes," he said. Was his mother fine with that? "She was totally fine with that." Think about what that kind of trust does to a child's confidence. {0}
9 h: At 14: the coin shop
10 p: His next job was at a stamp and coin store, at about ==14==. People walked in wanting to sell their rings or their silver. Michael's job was to assess the metal, work out what it was really worth, and then negotiate with them to buy it at a good price. Picture it for a second. A boy too young to drive is across the counter from an adult, bargaining over their silver. {0}
11 note: Numbers, plus a real human on the other side. Priceless training.
12 p: There was a limit to all this, and it was transport. Until 16, he says, your only choices are a bicycle or asking your parents for a lift, which cut down the jobs he could take. Even so, he had now washed dishes, greeted guests, sold stamps by mail, traded metals and invested in markets. And he was not yet 16. {0}
13 h: At 15: he breaks the Apple II
14 p: By now he had found Byte, a magazine about microcomputers and microprocessors. He subscribed, read each issue from cover to cover, and then read it again. One month it carried a piece by Apple co-founder Steve Wozniak on the Apple II, and Michael decided he had to have one. After a lot of persuading, his parents let him spend his own savings. By his memoir's account, the bill came to almost ==$1,300==. {32,22}
15 p: He collected the computer, brought it home, and took it apart immediately. His parents were furious. They had just watched their son pour his savings into a machine and then dismantle it on arrival. He had a reason, and he gives it in one line. {22,32,0}
16 Q: "I wanted to see how it works, so I took it apart." -- Michael Dell, Academy of Achievement interview, 2008 {0}
17 photo: https://commons.wikimedia.org/wiki/File:Apple_II_motherboard_(53171103722).jpg | The inside of an Apple II: a vintage motherboard, photographed by a later collector. Not Dell's own machine, but the same kind of board. | Steve Jurvetson | CC BY 2.0
18 p: It turned out to be a school in itself. Early personal computers used standard chips, so you could get a book on each chip and read what every pin did. You could design your own circuits and watch the signals move through them. "That was sort of the classroom for me," he says. Then came the better question: how could you make it do more, go faster, and talk to other computers? {0}
19 p: He got it back together, and he used it. The Franklin Institute says he ran a bulletin board system on the machine, an early online message board, and he met other computer fans that way. He also began tutoring neighbourhood kids on how to get the most from their own Apple IIs. That paid well too, so now the hobby was paying for itself. {23,22,32}
20 p: In 1980, he heard that Steve Jobs was coming to Houston to speak to his Apple user group. Jobs was just 25, and Michael had been reading about him in the business magazines with intense admiration. In his memoir, Dell says the room seemed to part when Jobs walked in, and that he spoke with passion about how personal computers would let people do what had seemed impossible. Michael was 15 years old and sitting in that room. {32}
21 note: He studied founders the way other kids study cricketers.
22 h: At 16: the job that became a method
23 p: At ==16== he could finally drive, and he got a job with the Houston Post selling subscriptions over the phone. The job meant cold calls, and cold calls waste a lot of time. Michael did what he had always done, and looked for the pattern. He noticed that speaking the way a customer spoke helped. Then he noticed something better about the people who said yes. {0,22,32}
24 Q: "Either they were moving to a new residence or they were getting married." -- Michael Dell, Academy of Achievement interview, 2008 {0}
25 p: A normal salesman might have shrugged and picked up the phone again. Michael asked where you could find those people in bulk. The answer was the courthouse. In Texas, a marriage licence application was public information, including the address where the licence should be sent. He hired all his friends, sent them across the ==16 counties== surrounding Houston, and had them copy down the addresses. {0,27,32}
26 p: He then used his Apple II to produce the mailing, sending each couple a letter offering a free trial of the paper. He added people applying for mortgages too. In his memoir he says he also went to Houston's new apartment and condo blocks during a building boom, and offered their new residents two free weeks through a simple form. He was named salesman of the month. He sold thousands of subscriptions. {0,21,22,32,35,27}
27 example: For example: a cold call asks a stranger to want a newspaper. A letter to a couple who just moved reaches them at the one moment they are actually building a new routine. Dell imitates that customer's thinking perfectly: "Oh, I got a new house. I'm nesting. Okay. The newspaper, that sounds like a good idea." Same product, same price, very different odds. {0}

He does not think of himself as purely a salesman, either. He says the skill was looking at a problem from a different angle and not being afraid of a completely new thought. __Great selling, in his telling, starts as research.__ "It was data-driven," he says, and he later carried the same habit into Dell. {0}

33 h: At 16 and 17: what was inside the IBM
34 p: In 1981, IBM released its personal computer, and he bought one and did what he always did. He opened it. IBM was then the giant of American business, so what he found surprised him. Almost nothing inside was made by IBM. The parts came from other companies, so a clever kid could buy the same parts too. {22,32,0}
35 Q: "The earliest PCs had IBM's name on the outside but there was no IBM on the inside." -- Michael Dell, Academy of Achievement interview, 2008 {0}
36 p: Next he did the arithmetic. A typical IBM PC sold for about $3,000, and the shop that sold it kept about $1,000 of that as gross profit. The parts inside cost around $700. He could also see the delay. He would read about a new technology in Byte, and then it would take a year or more before anyone could actually buy it. "So I was kind of a frustrated consumer," he says. That is usually how a good idea feels at the start. {22,33,0}

38 p: In ==June 1982==, the National Computer Conference came to Houston. He was 17 and he skipped classes for most of a week to go. In his memoir he compares it to Disneyland, with booth after booth of peripherals, terminals and software. It was not the last time school lost out to computers. By his own account he sat at the back of his classes reading computer magazines. {22,32}
39 p: The first customers came almost by accident. A lawyer, the father of a boy Michael was tutoring, heard about the machine he had souped up and asked for the same work on his own. He paid for the parts plus a fee. He then told other lawyers and doctors. When one doctor asked which PC he should buy for Michael to customise, Michael told him not to bother, and said he would buy it, upgrade it and sell it to him at a reasonable markup. {32,33,22}
40 note: That is a computer business. He was still in high school.
41 p: He went to Memorial High School in Hedwig Village, which drew students from wealthy Houston neighbourhoods and offered competitive classes in computer science and economics. He went to a BMW dealer with his parents, and when it came time to pay, the salesman looked to them. {34,33,32}
42 p: Michael took out a cashier's check for ==$15,000==, then paid the rest with cash. ===== [2] first-steps | Ages 18–19 | Room 2713

1 p: In the fall of 1983, Michael Dell began at the University of Texas at Austin as a pre-med biology student. He really did mean to go. "I was going to school and had every intention of going to school," he says. He called the computer work a fun hobby, and one with a useful side effect: "it provided a little extra income, so I could buy the latest stereo and whatever I wanted to buy." {0,24}
2 p: He lived in the Dobie Center, a private residential complex near the campus, in ==room 2713==. A hobby is a generous word for what happened there. He made and sold upgrade kits, adding hard disk drives and extra memory to the limited PCs of the day. His customers were students and local business people who found him by word of mouth. He bought computers, souped them up, and sold them on. {34,24,36,22,31}
3 Q: "The dorm room sort of looked like a computer lab. There were soldering irons and all this stuff all over the place." -- Michael Dell, speaking at the University of Texas, November 2011 {38,30}

5 p: Here is the part that shows how sharp he was. Some computer shops had IBM PCs they could not sell. Michael bought those unsold machines from them at cost, added memory and disk drives, and sold them for ==10 to 15 percent below retail==. The shop got rid of a problem, and his customer got a better machine for less. Everybody won, except the normal way of doing things. {0}
6 example: For example: he applied for a vendor licence so he could bid on contracts from the State of Texas. A freshman in a dorm was now up against computer dealers, and he won bids. 9 h: "Where are your books?"
10 p: The news reached Houston by the route such news always takes. His parents' friends told them, "Oh, Michael's doing so great. His business is going really well." His parents answered, "What are you talking about? He's supposed to be in college." So in ==November 1983== they made a couple of surprise visits to Austin. {0}
11 p: He remembers the questioning. "Where are your books?" they asked. "Oh, they're at the library," he answered. There's too many computer parts around here." {0}
12 note: Every student who has hidden a side project knows this feeling.
13 p: Then came the talk. "Michael, you've got to get your priorities straight," they told him. His father asked what he wanted to do with his life. He was talking about the most successful company in America, from a dorm room. {0,33,32}
14 h: Ten days
15 p: Around Thanksgiving of 1983, his parents got him to promise. No more computer business, and only his studies from now on. He actually tried. It lasted about ==ten days==, and those days without the work changed everything. He had time to notice how much he missed it. {0,30}
16 Q: "So actually, my parents telling me to stop doing it is probably what caused the company to get created." -- Michael Dell, Academy of Achievement interview, 2008 {0}
17 p: __A hobby you can drop is a hobby. A hobby you cannot drop is telling you something.__ That is what the ten days showed him. "I knew in my heart," he later wrote in his book, "that I was onto a fabulous business opportunity that I could not let pass me by." In 2011 he said it more simply, that the ban "probably threw me in even deeper." {0,33,30}
18 p: What he did next is not something to copy without thinking. He did not tell them. "So like any other 18-year-old who wants to do what their parents don't want them to do, you just don't tell them," he says. By January he was filling about $30,000 of parts orders a month. By the end of the school year it was around $80,000 a month. About half the early business came from around Austin, and half from everywhere else. {0,22,21}

22 h: The truth about the famous $1,000
23 p: You have probably heard that Dell started with $1,000. That is true, but the number has a funny origin. A customer who was a lawyer offered to incorporate the business in exchange for a hard-drive upgrade on his PC. Then Dell found out that Texas law required a company to have $1,000 to incorporate. __The famous figure was simply the legal minimum, and it was not a clever budget.__ {30,22,33}
24 p: He did not even have the cash sitting ready. According to the Austin American-Statesman's report of his 2011 talk, he had to sell a few more computers to raise the cash. The lawyer also hit a naming problem. "PC's Limited" was thought too generic for a corporate name, so he filed the papers as Dell Computer Corp., doing business as PC's Limited. So the Dell name went onto the legal papers because of a naming problem. {30}
25 note: Even the most famous startup number has a boring explanation.
26 p: The timing was absurd. This all happened right before finals week in May 1984, and Dell admits it may not have been wise. "Maybe that wasn't such a good idea," he told CNBC, "but when you're 19 years old, you haven't developed all the skills you need in terms of judgement and rational thinking." That is a rare thing to hear from a founder. He is telling you that part of his courage was simply being young. {31,30,0}
27 h: The deal
28 p: He still had to face his parents. By spring break, he told them the truth. Then he came to them with a proposal, and it was smart. He had checked with the university, and at UT you could take a semester off and come back. He would take off fall 1984 and try the business. If it worked, he would continue, and if not, he would go back to school. {0,30}
29 Q: "It was a $1,000 investment, and if it didn't work, I would go right back to school." -- Michael Dell, speaking at the University of Texas, November 2011 {30,38}
30 p: Look closely at what he did there. He did not ask his parents to believe in him. He asked them to compare a small, known cost with a large, unknown upside. "When I was 19 years old, the risk-reward was overwhelmingly in favor of taking the risk," he says. __He made quitting reversible, and that made it easy to say yes.__ That idea is useful for you too, because leaving a course is much less scary when you know the way back. {30,29}
31 p: They agreed, though not happily. "My parents weren't really supportive," he told Forbes in 2011. In his 2008 interview he added an honest detail: "If they hadn't agreed, I probably would've done it anyway to be honest with you." In ==May 1984== he incorporated the company. He was 19, and the classroom was over for him. {29,0}

   idea: Sell IBM-compatible PCs straight to the people using them, through magazine ads and a phone line, and build each one after the order comes in. Cutting out the dealer removed a markup of 10 to 15 percent. In 1985 his Turbo PC sold for $795, well under IBM's price.
   decision: Dealers would not stock an unknown teenager's machines, so he went around them and made that the whole strategy. The early turning points were bringing in 44-year-old investment banker Lee Walker as president in 1986, and dropping the name PC's Limited for Dell Computer in 1987.
   started: From a dorm room to a condominium to a 1,000-square-foot office. By 1986 the company had 100 workers, and then a factory of more than 80,000 square feet in north Austin was turning out 7,000 machines a month. Early buyers were computer-savvy individuals and nearby businesses, and big companies and government agencies followed.
   funding: $1,000 to incorporate. Then the part the legend skips: a Harvard Business School case says his family lent the firm $500,000, repaid by the end of 1986. After that came bank loans including a $10 million credit line, more than $21 million in a 1987 private placement, and a stock market listing in 1988.
   happened: Sales went from $6 million in 1985 to $159 million in 1988 and $25.3 billion in 2000. It became the world's top computer seller in 2001, lost that spot to HP in 2006, went private in 2013 and came back to the stock market in 2018. Revenue in the year to January 2026 was a record $113.5 billion.
   lesson: If the gatekeeper will not let you in, ask whether you need the gatekeeper at all. His weakness, no dealers, became his edge.
3 note: A $500,000 family loan. The $1,000 legend has a footnote.
4 p: Look again at that funding line, because it changes how the story reads. The famous $1,000 is real, and it was what the company was incorporated with. But the Harvard case, citing a published profile of him, says his family also lent the firm ==$500,000== for its early operations. So this was a brilliant idea and a real risk, and it also had a family safety net. __Most "self-made" stories have a line like this somewhere, so go looking for it before you compare yourself to anyone.__ {39}
5 VENTURE Olympic (1988 to 1990) [failed] {46,47,48,49}
   idea: A single family of machines to cover desktops, workstations and servers, with one or two processors and many operating systems. Dell later called it a "boil the ocean" product, which means trying to do everything at once.
   decision: The company was doing about $250 million a year and wanted a product that would put it on the map. It had just hired computer scientist Glenn Henry from IBM, and he built research and development up from almost nothing to 150 engineers. The safer option was to keep making small improvements to what already sold.
   started: An internal project with a large group of young engineers. It went in front of the public as prototypes at the Comdex trade show in November 1989.
   funding: Company money. Dell's research budget was only about $7 million in 1989, and Olympic took perhaps half of it.
   happened: Customers said no. "Thanks very much, but we don't need that much technology," is how Dell recalls the verdict. He shut it before any product shipped, kept the engineers, and reused the graphics and disk technology in a broad new product line within six to nine months.
   lesson: Show your idea to paying customers before you fall in love with it. And when you kill a project, keep the people.
6 VENTURE Selling through shops: the first retail experiment (1990 to 1994) [shut] {42,40,47,22,50}
   idea: Reach small businesses and home buyers who wanted to see a computer before paying, through CompUSA, Staples, Best Buy, Sam's Club and others.
   decision: Conventional wisdom said direct selling would only ever be a niche, and Dell gave in to it. The alternative, which he later went back to, was to stay direct and keep the customer relationship for himself.
   started: A 1990 deal with Soft Warehouse superstores, later renamed CompUSA, at mail-order prices, followed by Staples in 1991.
   funding: No special funding. The real cost was hidden: when Dell cut prices, it had to pay stores for the fall in value of machines sitting on their shelves.
   happened: It helped push sales from $890 million to over $2 billion. But when Dell finally measured profit by channel, retail was about 10 percent of sales and lost money. "We were losing our shirts," CFO Tom Meredith told Fortune. Dell walked out in 1994.
   lesson: Count profit per channel, not just sales. A channel can grow every month and still be killing you.
7 VENTURE The first notebook line (1991 to 1994) [pivoted] {47,51,52,53,48}
   idea: Get into the fastest-growing part of the PC market: laptops. Dell's first notebook arrived in 1991 and a colour model followed in 1992.
   decision: By 1993 the machines had quality problems. New hire John Medica, who had been chief designer of Apple's PowerBook, persuaded Dell to scrap every existing laptop plan and bet on one premium model. The other choice was to keep patching a bad line.
   started: Medica's redo took an extra six months of customer research before the design was settled.
   funding: Dell's own money, and a $20 million write-off for the cancelled models.
   happened: Dell sat out the market for more than a year. Notebooks fell from 12 percent of its system revenue to 3 percent. Then the Latitude XP launched in August 1994 and sold about 34,000 units in one quarter, as many as all of Dell's 1993 laptops combined.

8 p: Notice the pattern already. Each of these failures happened when Dell stopped doing the thing that made it different, or stopped listening to the buyer. And each failure handed him a rule he used for decades. Olympic gave him "relevant technology". The retail exit gave him total focus on direct sales. The notebook mess gave him a reason to hire people who knew more than he did. {46,42,54}

   decision: After leaving shops in 1994, Dell needed a new way to grow. It tested kiosks and online shopping services first, and the web won.

===== [4] near-death | 1987 to 1995 | Three Times It Nearly Broke
0 p: The third week of October 1987 should have killed the deal. Dell had hired Goldman Sachs and was raising money through a private placement, which means selling shares quietly to a small group of big investors rather than to the public. On ==19 October==, known as Black Monday, the US stock market lost more than a fifth of its value in one day. In his memoir, as read aloud on a podcast, Dell says his president Lee Walker came into his office convinced the deal was finished. He says he was busy taking apart a competitor's computer. {127}
1 p: The deal survived. Dell claims it was the only one of hundreds of financings under way that day to make it through, and we could only check that claim against his own account. What the Harvard case confirms is that the placement closed in the wake of the crash and raised more than ==$21 million==. Investors were looking at a company doubling every year. __Numbers that strong can get a deal through a panic that scares everyone else away.__ {127,39}
2 note: Market crashing? He was opening a rival's PC. Focus is a superpower.
3 p: Next came the IPO. An IPO, or initial public offering, is when a company first sells its shares to the public on a stock exchange. In ==June 1988==, Dell sold shares at $8.50 each, raising about $30 million and valuing the whole company at about $85 million. He was 23, and he still owned more than half of the company. He now had a public share price, public critics and a quarterly report card. {43,41,39}
4 p: Around then he also showed how badly a young founder can handle experienced people. Dell had hired a group of marketing executives from Tandy, a bigger PC maker, and they quickly lifted gross margins from 23 to 31 percent. Then came fights over ad budgets and the size of the sales force. By early 1988 most of the Tandy group had resigned or been forced out. Some people at the time questioned whether he could share control at all. {47,39}
5 h: Break one: the warehouse
6 p: The IPO cash was gone fast. Dell spent it on factory capacity and warehouse space, because it had been struggling to meet demand. Then it overestimated demand in the last quarter of 1988, and suddenly had no cash and warehouses full of unsold computers. For a company built on making machines only after an order came in, this was an embarrassing place to end up. It had also announced a clone of IBM's new PS/2 machine long before it was ready, and then could not deliver it on time. {47}
7 h: Break two: the memory chips
8 p: In 1989 he made the mistake he still talks about. The industry was moving from one type of memory chip to a newer one, and Dell had loaded up on the older kind and designed its products around them. It was left holding chips it could not use or easily sell, and it had to raise prices to cover the loss. Add the Olympic write-off and the result was brutal: profits fell 65 percent to ==$5 million==, even though sales had doubled. {22,47,49}
9 Q: "Failure is always an ingredient of success." -- Michael Dell, The Tim Ferriss Show, 2021 {49}
10 p: This is where the famous Dell supply chain really came from. In his own words, it grew out of "a horrible mistake we made in managing our supply chain", one that nearly took the company out of business. 3 p: His main early rival was Compaq, a fellow Texan that built expensive, high-quality machines. 0 p: The idea did not start with him. In ==June 2012==, Staley Cates of Southeastern Asset Management, Dell's biggest outside shareholder, asked Michael Dell if he would consider a management buyout. That is when a company's leader joins investors to buy all the shares and take it off the stock market. The next month he met Egon Durban of the buyout firm Silver Lake at a Fortune conference in Aspen. In August, the two talked again in Hawaii, where both had vacation homes. {84,56,54}
1 p: He also went to his friend George Roberts of the buyout giant KKR, who said that if the numbers worked, KKR would want in. Then he did the part that protects a founder and everyone else: he called the board's lead independent director, Alex Mandl, and told him. He promised not to hire an adviser without first telling the board. The directors formed a special committee with full power over the process, and he left the room. {84}
2 note: Tell the board first, before the bankers. Process matters when the lawyers come.
3 p: This was to be a leveraged buyout, which means buying a company mostly with borrowed money, and repaying it from the company's own cash. The bids came in on 23 October 2012, when the stock was $9.35. Silver Lake offered $11.22 to $12.16 a share, and KKR offered $12 to $13. Then the buyers started to run. KKR left on 3 December, saying it could not get its arms around the risks of the PC business. TPG looked and said no on 23 December. {84}
4 p: Silver Lake raised its bid to $12.70, and the committee said no. It then held out for $13.75. Durban called Mandl and offered $13.25, and when Mandl refused, Durban threatened to walk away. Mandl told him to go ahead. __A good board works for the shareholders, not the founder, and sometimes its job is to tell him no.__ {84}
5 p: To break the deadlock, Michael Dell agreed to take less for his own shares than everyone else would get. That let Silver Lake reach ==$13.65== a share for the public, and the deal was signed on 5 February 2013. Microsoft came in with a $2 billion loan. It was a premium of 37 percent over the average price of the previous 90 days. Many shareholders still felt it was far too low. {84,41,106}

===== [8] how-they-work | How he runs things | The Bulldozer on His Desk
0 p: In the fall of 2001, Michael Dell received a report card he did not enjoy. Internal interviews showed that people who worked for him found him impersonal and emotionally detached. A survey that summer, taken after the company's first mass layoffs, found that ==half== of all employees would leave if they got the chance. He was about 36, and he had been running the company since he was a teenager. {184}
1 p: Within a week he stood in front of his top 20 managers and criticised himself. He told them he was hugely shy, that it could make him look aloof, and that he would try to build closer bonds with them. Company personality tests had already labelled him an "off-the-charts introvert," so people in that room knew what this was costing him. Days later, a video of the talk was being shown to every manager in the company, several thousand people. {184}
2 Q: "It was powerful stuff. You could tell it wasn't easy for him." -- Brian Wood, then a Dell sales head, to BusinessWeek, 2003 {184}
3 p: Then came the toy. He put a small plastic bulldozer on his desk, as a reminder not to ram ideas through without bringing people along. His partner Kevin Rollins, who had been called autocratic, got a Curious George doll to remind him to listen first. __Think about that for a moment: one of the richest men in America fixed a personal flaw with a desk toy, because willpower alone had not worked.__ {184}

5 p: This chapter is about the system around him, not his genius. Most of it is cheap and most of it can be copied. Some of it was also hard on people, and we will show that too. {184,185}
6 h: His day, as far as it has been reported
7 p: He goes to bed at about 8:30 or 9 p.m. and gets up around 4 or 5 a.m. to exercise. That is his own account, given on a podcast in 2024. "You won't find me at the nightcap," he said. Our reading: the young founder paid a price at the start, and the older CEO learned where extra hours stop paying back. {188,186}
10 example: For example: in 2003, BusinessWeek described him with a BlackBerry strapped to his hip. By 2006, The Independent reported that his first task of any day was checking questions and complaints from customers. 11 p: He protects his time by deciding what he will not do. He told an interviewer in 2025 that he does not join the quarterly earnings calls, though he does meet big investors whose questions he can learn from. 13 p: He dislikes anything that slows information down. "Extra layers, approvals, and meetings just slow things down," he told Harvard Business Review in 2005. 15 p: One meeting says a lot about what he values. 16 example: For example: Dell explained in 2005 how live data changed a normal morning. Salespeople could see their margin on a sale while still on the phone with the buyer. 33 h: What working there was actually like
34 p: Mike Carter joined in 1989 and left in 2020, and he wrote down what he remembered. This is one man's memory, but it is specific. ===== [9] mindset | What he believes | Pleased, But Never Satisfied
0 p: Michael Dell is not a man who gives you big speeches about his philosophy. Fortune says it is hard to get a splashy sound bite out of him. But he has been saying the same few things for 40 years. Here are eight of them, each with the story that shows whether he means it. {193}
1 h: 1. Play nice, but win
2 p: His parents said this to him and his brothers whenever they went out to play ball in the street. He thinks people often remember only half of it. For him it means being fair and ethical and also not forgetting to win. He says he has seen again and again that treating people fairly comes back to you. {49}
3 p: The clearest test came on ==29 May 2013==. Carl Icahn was trying to take his company from him. Dell picked up the phone, called him directly, and was invited to dinner at Icahn's home that same evening. Over Mrs. Icahn's meatloaf, he kept asking what Icahn's plan for the company actually was. He left convinced there was none. Polite dinner guest, cold conclusion: that is the motto in one evening. {200,188,49}
4 note: He went to his enemy's house, ate meatloaf, and found the bluff.
5 p: In fairness, Icahn tells it differently. Dell and his partner Silver Lake did raise their offer, by 10 cents a share plus a special dividend for some holders. "The shareholders got a lot more money because of me," Icahn told Fortune in 2024. And "nice" has limits. In his memoir Dell calls Icahn things like a circus clown, and a review published with TIME called the book a victory lap of sorts. {193,188}
6 h: 2. Pleased, but never satisfied
7 p: Inside Dell they shorten this to PBNS. BusinessWeek reported in 2003 that a success at the company earned five seconds of praise, then five hours of post-mortem on what could have been better. When Dell's first Asian factory opened in Malaysia, the CEO sent the manager one of his old running shoes. The message was that this was only the first step in a marathon. {201,184}
8 Q: "Celebrate for a nanosecond. Then move on." -- Michael Dell, BusinessWeek, 2003 {184}
9 p: He calls it a race with no finish line and links it to the Japanese idea of kaizen, continuous improvement. In 2019, when a reporter asked about his legacy, he said, "Ask me when I'm 90 or something," and claimed to spend 99.99% of his time thinking about the future. His office also keeps mementos of the company's high and low points, because he believes success depends on how you deal with failure. {202,203,201}
10 note: Great for growth. Hard on morale. See the 2001 survey again.
11 h: 3. Success is a horrible teacher
12 p: The year after Dell went public, two things nearly finished it. A hugely ambitious engineering project named after the Olympics failed. And the company got caught holding the wrong kind of memory chip during an industry change. Dell says the fledgling company almost went out of business, and calls the episode embarrassing. {49}
13 p: Then look at what came out of it. The Olympic failure taught the engineers to be more pragmatic, and it left them with the skill to build a broad line of products quickly. The memory mess became, in his words, the root of the just-in-time supply chain that made Dell famous. __The thing the world admired most about Dell was built out of its most embarrassing mistake.__ {49}
14 Q: "Failure is always an ingredient of success." -- Michael Dell, The Tim Ferriss Show, 2021 {49}
15 h: 4. Listen to customers, not rivals
16 p: This one is more radical than it sounds. He says competition can actually limit you, because if your rival is not very good, you are measuring yourself against a low bar. You can learn what not to do from them. But he says you learn far more from customers, and he calls this having "big ears." {49,202}
17 p: This explains something odd about the company. Fortune points out that Dell has never been a "market maker," a firm that creates demand for something nobody knew they wanted. Analyst Patrick Moorhead says what it does well is knowing when to enter a market, because it is so close to its customers. In a 2003 BusinessWeek view, Dell's mission was not to invent the next big thing but to build the current big thing better than anyone else. {193,184}
18 note: You do not need to be Steve Jobs. Dell mostly chose not to be.
19 h: 5. Facts beat ego, even your own
20 p: For more than 20 years, selling direct was the religion at Dell. Then, in April 2007, three months after returning as CEO, he wrote to staff in a memo that leaked. Forbes called it a repudiation of the gospel he had written himself. He was telling everyone that the idea with which he had changed the industry might no longer be enough. {204,205}
21 Q: "The direct model has been a revolution, but is not a religion." -- Michael Dell, memo to employees, 25 April 2007 {204}
22 p: He describes this as plain method. "It's all about the objective function and the objective function was success," he said in 2025. "Not emotional wins." In his list of 21 lessons he says the scientific method works in business, and that you must be humble enough to change your mind if the data says so. __The founder is often the last person in the company allowed to say the founding idea has run out. So he has to say it himself.__ {192,202}
23 h: 6. Being short of money is an advantage
24 p: He loves one comparison. He started with ==$1,000==. Compaq, launched two years earlier in the same state, started with ==$100 million==. Dell had no spare money to pile up unsold machines, so it became a company that holds very little stock. "We didn't become asset-light just because it was a brilliant strategy," he said. "We didn't have any choice." {194}
25 p: He still thinks this way. In 2025 he said he would have been skeptical of early investors even if venture capital had existed in his day, because the company did not need it. He said more companies have too much capital than too little, and "that can be a real curse." {192}
26 note: Read this twice before you spend a month chasing a funding round.
27 h: 7. Most risks are smaller than they look
28 p: Asked about starting up, he says it never seemed like a big risk to him. "It's not life or death. It is just a risk," he said in 2025. His attitude at 19 was that if it worked, great, and if not, he would go back to college. He also says he never cared much what friends thought of the idea, since most of them did not understand it anyway. {192}
29 p: Be fair when you read this. He could say it partly because college was still sitting there, waiting, and many readers of this book do not have that kind of cushion. The useful part is the question behind it: what is the actual worst case, and can you walk back from it? He wrote his memoir hoping people would take more risks, because he sees talent wasted by fear. {192,49}
30 Q: "I have this belief that a lot of human potential is left unrealized because people are afraid to fail." -- Michael Dell, The Tim Ferriss Show, 2021 {49}
31 h: 8. Nobody knows, so build for it anyway
32 p: He is suspicious of predictions, including his own. "History shows that predictions are mostly wrong; nobody really knows," he said in 2025. He says that if you had asked the top 100 AI scientists in 2019 whether language models would change everything within five years, 99 would have said no. When Fortune asked in 2024 how big AI could be, he thought aloud, and ended with, "I don't know for sure. Nobody knows." {192,193}
33 p: So he does not bet on forecasts. He prepares, then moves when conditions are right. He points out that the US ten-year Treasury note dropped below 2% three times in about 12 years. The first time he took Dell private. The second time he bought EMC and VMware. The third was during the coronavirus. {191}
34 Q: "When money goes on sale, stuff happens." -- Michael Dell, McKinsey Quarterly, 2024 {191}
35 example: For example: he says Dell knew data was valuable long before anyone was really using it, and went all in on storing and handling it. It tried servers in 1989, which he says was too early, and got serious in the mid-1990s. Being early without betting the company is how you are still around when the timing finally turns right. {192,193}
36 p: One more line from his list of 21 lessons: "Try never to be the smartest person in the room." Another: "Don't be a victim, ever." Neither one costs anything, and you can start on both today. {202}

In his 2021 memoir, according to one reviewer, he says he called Jobs to smooth things over and now calls the comment stupid. Apple went on to be worth around $400 billion by that 2011 talk, and the line follows him still. {137,188}

12 h: When the internet shouted back
13 p: In June 2005, journalist Jeff Jarvis got fed up with a bad laptop and a worse helpline, and posted on his blog under the headline "Dell sucks." Thousands joined in, and the episode was soon known as "Dell Hell." Dell's policy toward bloggers at the time was "look, don't touch." Jarvis returned the laptop and bought a Mac. {229,230}
14 p: What came next is the useful part. A Dell manager later recalled Michael Dell asking why they were not listening to these voices and connecting them with customer service. In 2006 the company sent technicians to complaining bloggers and started its own blog, Direct2Dell. In February 2007 he launched IdeaStorm, a site where customers could tell the company what to do. Staff told Jarvis it was his own idea. Customers demanded Linux on desktops, and Dell agreed. {231,229,230,232}
15 example: For example: by 2009, Dell's @DellOutlet account on Twitter had passed $3 million in sales from promotions. Commentators at the time noted the money was not the main point. A company once famous for ignoring its critics now had many corporate Twitter accounts, wikis and blogs answering them. Its negative blog mentions had fallen, by Dell's own measure, from 49% to 22%. {233,229}
16 h: When he does speak up
17 p: He picks few public fights, but he has had some memorable ones. At Davos in January 2019, a panel moderator asked him about a proposal for a 70% top tax rate. "No, I'm not supportive of that," he said, as the room laughed. "Name a country where that's worked." From the same panel, MIT economist Erik Brynjolfsson replied: "The United States." He pointed out that the top rate averaged about 70% from the 1930s to the 1960s, and that growth in those years was pretty good. The clip spread across social media. {234,235}
18 p: These days his most direct channel is his own account on X. On 19 June 2024 he wrote, "We're building a Dell AI factory with @nvidia to power @grok for @xai @elonmusk." Dell shares climbed as much as 8.3% the next morning, before giving back most of that. In June 2026 he personally announced on X that shareholders had voted, with 97% approval, to move the company's legal home to Texas. He also writes long posts on LinkedIn, and one of those comes back to bite him in the next chapter. {26,236,237}
19 p: He wrote two books more than 20 years apart, and they show how his public voice changed. He says the first one, from the 1990s, did not disclose much. The second, Play Nice But Win in 2021, tells of near-failures, the arrest and bad decisions. His explanation is age and maturity, and a wish to show people how hard it really was. {49}

In Washington, in October 2026, he received a National Medal of Technology and Innovation from President Trump. 22 p: Abroad, his youth was the first story. When he went to Japan as a 23-year-old CEO, he recalls that it seemed highly unusual to people there. In the US, he says, the reaction was simply: a young computer guy from Texas, okay, we get it. {192}
23 p: India has known him as an employer for a long time, and not always a smooth one. In 2003, after an onslaught of complaints, Dell stopped sending some US corporate support calls to Bangalore. ===== [12] what-went-wrong | The bills he paid | What Went Wrong
0 p: He says it himself, about the turns his company has taken: "we got a lot of them right, but we didn't get all of them right." Here are the ones he got wrong, or that went wrong on his watch. We have been careful here. A settlement is not a conviction, an allegation is not a finding, and we will say which is which each time. {191}
1 facts:
   - $100M + $4M :: SEC penalties in 2010: the company's, and his own. Settled without admitting or denying. {246}
   - $1 billion :: The 2023 Class V settlement, a record in the Delaware Court of Chancery. {247}
   - $300M :: Charge Dell announced in 2005, partly to fix faulty desktop computers. {248}

2 h: He stepped back, and the company slid
3 p: In July 2004 he handed the CEO title to Kevin Rollins and stayed as chairman. By his own account not much really changed, and they kept running it together. Early in 2005 things looked perfect: Fortune named Dell America's most admired company. By late 2005, sales were missing projections. Rivals had copied much of its supply chain, and customers were moving from customisable desktops to notebooks. {200}
4 p: He admits two things in his memoir: "It took us a little bit longer than we would have liked to figure all this out," and that for a few years the company had put profit ahead of growth and share. Back in June 2006, though, his tone was different. Asked about Wall Street's charge of complacency, he said, "Anyone internally would laugh at the complacency charge." Seven months later Rollins was out and Dell was CEO again. Hewlett-Packard had taken the top spot in PCs. {200,190,250,251}
5 p: He had said in 2003 that the company was much stronger with the two of them doing it together. In 2007 the analyst Roger Kay told the Financial Times that Dell probably shared as much responsibility as Rollins for the failings, adding, "The rules of Wall Street demand that there be human sacrifice." Dell himself writes that if there was blame, he shared it. __When you share the wheel with someone, you also share the crash, whatever the title says.__ {189,251,200}
6 note: Laughing at the complacency charge is itself a warning sign.
7 photo: https://commons.wikimedia.org/wiki/File:Dell_headquarters.jpg | Dell headquarters in Round Rock, Texas, in April 2004. He stepped down as CEO that July and returned in January 2007. | Tim Patterson | CC BY 2.0
8 h: The Intel money
9 p: This is the most serious one. On 22 July 2010 the US Securities and Exchange Commission charged Dell Inc. with failing to tell investors about large payments it received from Intel for not using chips from rival AMD. The SEC alleged those payments grew from ==10%== of Dell's operating income in fiscal 2003 to ==38%== in fiscal 2006, and peaked at ==76%== in one quarter of fiscal 2007. It alleged that without them, Dell would have missed Wall Street's earnings estimates in every quarter from fiscal 2002 to 2006. {246,140}
10 p: When Intel cut the payments after Dell announced it would use AMD, profits dropped sharply. The SEC alleged that Dell, Rollins and the CFO, who had been warned this could happen, told investors the drop came from aggressive pricing and component costs. Separately, the SEC charged former finance staff with using "cookie jar" reserves to smooth the numbers. Dell had already restated its books, erasing $92 million of net income. {246,205}
11 p: The outcome: Dell Inc. paid a ==$100 million== penalty. Michael Dell and Rollins paid ==$4 million== each and the former CFO $3 million. All settled without admitting or denying the allegations. In fairness, the company pointed out that Mr. Dell's settlement covered only claims that require negligence, not fraudulent intent, and did not involve the accounting fraud charges. It also did not stop him from remaining CEO and chairman. {246,141,140}
12 example: For example: imagine a shop that says its profit comes from great service, when three-quarters of it in one period is a cheque from a supplier for not stocking a rival's goods. Any investor would want to know. That, stripped down, is what the SEC claimed was missing from Dell's story. {246}
13 note: Very important: know where your profit really comes from, and say so.
14 h: The bad capacitors
15 p: From 2003 to 2005, Dell sold millions of OptiPlex office desktops with faulty capacitors, tiny parts worth a fraction of a penny that leaked and burst. When computers failed at the University of Texas, his own university, Dell reportedly suggested the math department had overworked them with difficult calculations. A 2004 internal study first predicted that 12% of some models would have trouble in three years, then raised that to 45%, and said it could reach 97%. {248,252,253}
16 p: Lawsuit documents unsealed in 2010 showed employees knew the machines were likely to break while playing down the problem to customers, The New York Times reported. Dell did not recall them. It replaced them case by case. The company's answer was that this was an industry-wide problem from a supplier, that it had extended warranties and worked with customers, that there was no safety risk, and that the plaintiff had used desktops as servers. Its lawyers denied the plaintiff's claims, and it settled that suit in September 2010 on undisclosed terms. {248,252,254,255}
17 p: The exploding batteries were a different kind of trouble. In August 2006 Dell recalled ==4.1 million== laptop batteries that could overheat and catch fire. That one was mainly Sony's flaw, the Associated Press and The New York Times both noted, and Apple recalled batteries too. Fair is fair. But it landed in the same bad year, and it hurt. {256,145}
18 h: The $1 billion lawsuit
19 p: To help pay for EMC in 2016, Dell created a special share class, Class V, that was supposed to track VMware's value. It traded at a discount of about 30% from the start. Part of the reason, a Delaware judge wrote, was the company's right to force conversion of those shares. In 2018 Dell bought them out for ==$23.9 billion==. Former holders sued Michael Dell, Silver Lake and other directors, alleging they were pushed into an unfair deal. {257,258,259}
20 p: The case settled on the eve of trial for ==$1 billion== in cash. The judge wrote, "The 'b' is not a typo." It was the largest recovery of its kind in that court, and $266.7 million of it went to the lawyers. The company and its insurers paid, on behalf of the defendants, under indemnity agreements a board committee reviewed. Because it settled, no court ruled on whether the allegations were true. In fairness, the price had already been raised from $109 to $120 a share after big holders pushed back, before the deal closed. {247,260,261,262,167,257}
21 p: There was also a fight over the 2013 buyout itself. In 2016 a Delaware judge decided that the shares bought at $13.75 were worth ==$17.62==. That implied investors had been shortchanged. In December 2017 the Delaware Supreme Court reversed that part of the ruling, saying the judge was wrong to give no weight to the market price, and sent the case back. So on that one, the record leans his way. {106,263,161}
22 note: Complicated share structures make complicated enemies. Keep your cap table simple.
23 h: The promise he reversed
24 p: On 7 September 2022 he wrote on LinkedIn, in his own name, about employees working from home. The words are worth reading carefully, given what came next. He said Dell had found no meaningful differences between remote and office workers on careers, and that the company had committed to letting people choose their work style. {237,264}
25 Q: "If you are counting on forced hours spent in a traditional office to create collaboration and provide a feeling of belonging within your organization, you're doing it wrong." -- Michael Dell, LinkedIn, 7 September 2022 {237}
26 p: In February 2024, Dell told staff that those who stayed fully remote would not be considered for promotion or be able to change roles. Hybrid staff had to be on site 39 days a quarter, and The Register reported that attendance would be graded. Dell's statement said in-person connections were critical to innovation. Business Insider reported that nearly half of workers had picked remote anyway. Then on 31 January 2025 he emailed staff: "We are retiring the hybrid policy." Anyone living near an office would be back five days a week from 3 March. {265,266,267,268}
27 p: He is allowed to change his mind. Belief number 5 in this book says so. But a public promise is different, because staff who chose remote work had relied on it. Over the same stretch, headcount fell from about 133,000 to 120,000, then 108,000, then ==97,000==. As The Register points out, not all of that is layoffs, since people leave on their own and Dell has sold parts of the business. But some of it was. And in 2024 he was still saying AI would augment humans and not replace them. {249,2,269,191}
28 note: Before you publish a principle, ask if it survives a bad year.
29 h: Smaller bills, and the bad bets
30 p: Phones were a turn he saw coming and still missed. He told McKinsey in 2024 that Dell is not an operating system company, and that once Android and iOS won there was no decent return in making Android phones. "So that turned out to be a bad use of capital," he said. Dell's Venue phones came out in 2010 and were dropped in 2012. Back in 2003, HP's then-CEO Carly Fiorina had called Dell "a one-trick pony," and it spent just 1.3% of revenue on research, against nearly 6% at IBM and HP. {191,184}
31 p: Some bills came from the company rather than from him. In 2023 an Australian court ordered Dell Australia to pay A$10 million after it admitted overstating discounts on add-on monitors, sold to more than 5,300 buyers. In 2024 Dell agreed to pay $2.3 million to settle US claims of overcharging the Army through non-competitive bids, with no finding of liability. Also in 2024, someone scraped order data through a partner portal and claimed 49 million records; Dell confirmed names, addresses and order details were exposed, but no financial data. {270,271,272,273}
32 h: What you should not copy
33 p: Do not hide where your profit comes from. Do not blame the customer for a broken product. Do not laugh off a warning sign in an interview. Do not mock a rival who is down, especially on a public stage. Do not design a deal so complicated that your own investors need a court to understand it. And do not make a public promise to your team that you are not ready to keep through a bad year. {246,248,190,136,257,237}
34 p: What he did right, even in these stories, is also worth noting. He came back and took charge in person. He wrote down many of the mistakes in his own book. __Keep that habit from the very start. Writing down your own mistakes is the cheapest business school there is.__ {250,200,49}

===== LESSONS
0 Say your flaw out loud.: In 2001 Michael Dell stood before his top 20 managers and admitted that his shyness made him seem cold, then let the video go to every manager in the company. Half his staff had said they would leave if they could. Your team already knows your flaw, so saying it costs less than you think.
1 Put a reminder on your desk.: After that same talk he kept a plastic bulldozer on his desk to stop himself ramming ideas through. He did not trust willpower alone. Pick one bad habit and make it visible today.
2 Celebrate for a nanosecond.: When Dell's first Asian factory opened in Malaysia, he sent the manager an old running shoe, because the marathon had only just begun. Enjoy the win, then ask what could have been better. Just do not forget to thank people, which is the bit he had to learn later.
3 Make bad news cheap.: At Dell, he said, the worst thing a leader could do was be in denial, and a manager who admitted a problem and asked for help would be fine. Share the real numbers widely so no one can hide. A problem that arrives early is usually a small problem.
4 Listen to customers before rivals.: He has run Friday 7:30 a.m. customer meetings and says you learn far more from customers than from competitors. When bloggers roasted Dell in 2005, ignoring them made it worse, and listening produced IdeaStorm. Your benchmark should be the customer's complaint, not the other company's product.
5 Never marry your method.: Direct selling made him rich, and in 2007 he told his own staff the model "has been a revolution, but is not a religion." Keep the goal and replace the method when the facts change. If you built the idea, you may have to be the one who says it is over.
6 Let shortage teach you.: He started with $1,000 while Compaq began with $100 million, so Dell learned to hold almost no stock. He says that having too much capital can be a curse. Before you raise money, ask what you would build if you could not.
7 Watch your cash when you grow.: Dell jumped from $890 million to $2.1 billion in sales in one year in the early 1990s, and everything began to fall apart. He says a new CFO helped him see he was running out of capital. Fast growth can kill you just as surely as no growth.
8 Pick a partner who can overrule you.: He and Kevin Rollins each reckoned they were right about 80% of the time, and agreed not to make a major move without each other. It worked for years, then ended badly in 2007 when the numbers fell. Choose someone honest enough to stop you, and be clear about who carries the blame.
9 Do not hand over and look away.: He gave up the CEO title in 2004, stayed close, and still watched Dell lose its lead by 2006. In June of that year he laughed off the charge of complacency, and seven months later he was back. A title change does not take away your responsibility for what you can see.
10 Tell investors the real reason.: The SEC alleged in 2010 that Intel's payments reached 76% of Dell's operating income in one quarter, without investors being told. He paid $4 million and settled without admitting or denying it. If one source props up your profit, say so before someone else does.
11 Mean it before you publish it.: In 2022 he wrote that forced office hours were "doing it wrong," and by 2025 he had ordered five days a week. He may have good reasons, but people had planned their lives around his post. Write only the principles you will keep when times get hard.
12 Do not kick a rival who is down.: His 1997 line about shutting Apple down was still being quoted in October 2026, and a review of his memoir says he now calls it stupid. A cheap shot lasts longer than a good quarter, and the rival may come back for you.
13 Take the risk you can walk back from.: At 19 his plan was simple: if the company failed, he would go back to college. He says it never felt like a big risk because of that. Work out your real worst case, and if you can survive it, go.
14 Protect your sleep.: One of the richest men alive is in bed by about 9 p.m. and up by 5 to exercise. He says hours beyond a certain point stop paying back, and he has now lasted more than 40 years at the company. Do not confuse being exhausted with being serious.
