
=== CH0 origins | Ages 0–12 | A Teenage Mom, a Cuban Dad and a Ranch
0.0 P On ==12 January 1964==, a baby boy was born in Albuquerque, New Mexico. His name was not Bezos yet. On his birth certificate he was Jeffrey Preston Jorgensen. [37,10]
0.1 P His mother, Jackie Gise, was ==17== and a junior in high school when he arrived. His father, Ted Jorgensen, was a teenager too, an expert unicycle rider who performed with a local circus. The two had married after Jackie got pregnant. She was 16 on her wedding day. [36,40,43]
0.2 QUOTE "I can assure you that being a pregnant teenager in high school was not cool in Albuquerque, New Mexico, at that time." -- Jeff Bezos, interview with Axel Springer CEO Mathias Döpfner, 2018 [35]
0.3 P At first, the school told Jackie she could not finish. She kept pushing back until it gave in. But it let her return only on strict conditions, and decades later she listed them in a graduation speech. Jeff said his grandfather “went to bat for her” when the school tried to throw her out. [36,35]
0.4 EX For example: Jackie had to arrive and leave within five minutes of the school bells. She was not allowed to talk to other students or eat lunch in the cafeteria. And she was told she could not walk across the stage with her class to collect her diploma. [36]
0.5 NOTE She was 17. Imagine your own junior year, with a baby.
0.6 P The marriage did not last. In Brad Stone's account, Ted drank, stayed out late, dropped out of college and did not look for work. About 17 months after Jeff was born, Jackie filed for divorce. Later Ted said it plainly: “I wasn't a good father or a husband.” [40,36]
0.7 P Jackie found work as a secretary, even though, in her words, she was a terrible typist who could not read her own shorthand. The job paid ==$190 a month==. That paid for a small apartment for her and her son, but not for a telephone. So her father gave her a walkie-talkie and told her to check in every morning at 7 a.m. [36]
0.8 P She also refused to stop studying. Jackie signed up for night school and chose classes by one rule: would the professor let her bring a baby? She arrived with Jeff and two duffel bags, one full of textbooks and the other full of cloth diapers, bottles and toys to keep him quiet for a few minutes. [36]
0.9 FACTS
   - 17 :: Jackie's age when Jeff was born [36]
   - $190 :: her monthly pay as a secretary [36]
   - 7 a.m. :: daily walkie-talkie check-in with her parents (no phone) [36]
   - 40 :: her age when she finally graduated from college [36]
0.10 H The boy from Cuba in a coat made of rags
0.11 P In one of those night classes, Jackie met a young Cuban refugee named Miguel “Mike” Bezos. His story was as tough as hers. In ==1962==, at 16, Mike flew alone from Cuba to Miami. He spoke almost no English. His parents sent him away because, under Fidel Castro, they felt they had to protect him. [36,37]
0.12 QUOTE "My parents were not allowed to go into the airport with me, so they dropped me off. I got on an airplane and landed in Miami 45 minutes later." -- Mike Bezos [37]
0.13 P He was allowed to carry three pairs of pants, three shirts, three pairs of underwear and one pair of shoes. His mother thought America must be freezing, so she and Mike's sister stitched him a heavy coat out of cleaning rags and knitting wool. The Bezos family still has it. Mike was one of about 14,000 young people who fled Cuba to the US in the early 1960s. [37,42]
0.14 P Mike spent three weeks in Camp Matecumbe, a refugee camp in Florida. Then he was sent to high school in Wilmington, Delaware, where he won a scholarship to college in Albuquerque. “I had very little idea at that time where Albuquerque was,” he said later. That is the city where he met Jackie and her little boy. [37]
0.15 NOTE The man who raised him arrived with one pair of shoes.
0.16 P Jackie married Mike in 1968, when Jeff was four. Mike legally adopted him, and Jeffrey Jorgensen became Jeff Bezos. Jackie had asked Ted to stay out of their lives, and he agreed. Over the years he lost track of the family completely. [37,10,42,40]
0.17 QUOTE "The only time I ever think about it, genuinely, is when a doctor asks me to fill out a form." -- Jeff Bezos on his biological father, to Wired in 1999 (as quoted by The Guardian) [42]
0.18 P Ted's side of the story has a strange ending. In late 2012 the journalist Brad Stone, researching his book on Amazon, walked into Ted's bike shop in Glendale, Arizona. Ted had no idea who his son had become. Stone had to show him photos on a phone, and he later wrote that Ted's eyes filled with “sorrow and disbelief.” Ted died in 2015. [40,41,42]
0.19 P Mike finished his degree and became an engineer for Exxon, and the family moved to Houston, Texas. Jeff soon had a younger sister, Christina, and a brother, Mark, five and six years younger than him. Jeff has always called Mike his father. [10,42,36]
0.20 H Summers on Grandpa's ranch
0.21 P Jackie's father, Lawrence Preston Gise, was a serious man with a serious job. He had been a regional director of the US Atomic Energy Commission in Albuquerque, and The Atlantic reports he worked on missile-defence systems for the Pentagon and supervised nuclear labs. He retired early to the family's ==25,000-acre== ranch near Cotulla, a small town in South Texas. [10,43]
0.22 P From the age of four, Jeff spent every summer there, three months a year. At first it was partly to give his young parents a break. His grandfather made the little boy feel useful anyway. [10,35]
0.23 QUOTE "He created the illusion for me when I was 4 years old that I was helping him on the ranch. Which, of course, cannot have been true. But I believed it." -- Jeff Bezos, 2018 [35]
0.24 P Cotulla had about 3,000 people and a tiny Carnegie-style library, smaller than a classroom. By pure luck, one local resident had been a science-fiction fan and donated a whole collection. That is where Jeff fell for Heinlein and Asimov, a love he says never ended. On slow days he also watched soap operas with his grandmother. [10,43]
0.25 PHOTO https://commons.wikimedia.org/wiki/File:Nueces_River_at_Cotulla,_TX_IMG_0452.JPG | The Nueces River running low through Cotulla, Texas, the small ranch town where Jeff spent every summer from age 4 to 16. Photographed in 2009. | Billy Hathorn | CC BY 3.0 | 2009
0.26 H The day he made his grandmother cry
0.27 P Some summers his grandparents hitched up their Airstream trailer and joined a caravan club on long road trips across America. Jeff, about 10, sat in the back seat. His grandmother smoked constantly, and he hated the smell. On the radio, anti-smoking ads were claiming that every puff cost you a few minutes of life. [33,34,35]
0.28 P So the boy who loved numbers did the maths in the back seat. Proud of himself, he announced: “At two minutes per puff, you've taken nine years off your life!” His grandmother burst into tears. [34,33,35]
0.29 P His grandfather pulled the car over and took Jeff outside, away from her. Jeff was scared. His grandfather had never said a cross word to him. But he wasn't angry. He just said one sentence that Jeff never forgot. [35,34]
0.30 QUOTE "You're going to figure out one day that it's harder to be kind than clever." -- Lawrence Preston Gise, Jeff's grandfather, as Jeff retold it in 2018 [35]
0.31 P Thirty-six years later, in 2010, Jeff built his whole speech to Princeton's graduating class around that moment. His line was simple: “Cleverness is a gift, kindness is a choice.” __Being smart came easily to him; the lesson was that being right and being good are not the same thing.__ [38,33]
0.32 NOTE Read this twice. Being right is not the same as being kind.
0.33 H Science fair central
0.34 P Even as a toddler, Jeff took his crib apart with a screwdriver. The Guardian reports he wanted to sleep in a real bed. As he grew, he rigged an electric alarm to keep his younger brother and sister out of his room and turned the family garage into a lab. [10,42]
0.35 P He booby-trapped the house so often that his parents worried, he joked, that one day they would open a door and get ==30 pounds of nails== on their heads. His mother drove him to the RadioShack electronics store again and again for parts. [10]
0.36 QUOTE "Okay. Look. Will you please get your parts list straight before we go? I can't handle more than one trip to Radio Shack per day." -- Jackie Bezos to young Jeff, as he remembered it [10]
0.37 P In fourth grade, around ==1974==, his Houston school got something almost no school had: a teletype machine linked by phone to a mainframe computer. A Houston company had donated spare computer time. None of the teachers knew how to use it, so Jeff and a couple of other kids stayed after class and taught themselves to program from the manuals. [10,35]
0.38 EX For example: after about a week of serious programming, the kids found that someone had already loaded a Star Trek game onto the mainframe. From then on, that is all they did. In playground games of Star Trek, everyone wanted to be Spock or Captain Kirk. Jeff's third choice was the ship's computer. Friends would call “Computer?” and he would answer “Working.” [10]
0.39 PHOTO https://commons.wikimedia.org/wiki/File:ASR-33_at_CHM.agr.jpg | A Teletype ASR-33 at the Computer History Museum. Jeff learned to program on a school teletype like this one, dialling into a mainframe with a phone handset. | ArnoldReinhold | CC BY-SA 3.0 | 2014
0.40 P Jackie fought for his schooling the way she had fought for her own. She drove him 40 miles a day so he could attend a Houston elementary school for high-scoring kids. When a waiting list kept him out of the gifted track in middle school, she kept pressing officials until they made an exception. [43]
0.41 P Here is a real secret from those years. A 1977 book about gifted education in Texas, Turning On Bright Minds, profiled a sixth-grader in a Houston gifted programme called “Tim.” Brad Stone later revealed that “Tim” was Jeff Bezos. The author found him “friendly, but serious,” and his teachers said he was “not particularly gifted in leadership.” [42]
0.42 NOTE Teachers said: not a leader. He became one. Labels at 12 are not destiny.
0.43 P He read constantly. In fourth grade he read about ==30 Newbery Award winners== in one year for a class contest, and still lost to someone who read more. His favourite was A Wrinkle in Time. Being sent to his room was no punishment, because he just read. The only time he got in trouble was losing his library privileges for laughing too loudly. [10]
0.44 P At about six he wanted to be an archaeologist, “before Indiana Jones,” he points out. Then he wanted to be an astronaut. He says he has been thinking about space since he was five. His heroes were Thomas Edison, the inventor, and Walt Disney, who could get huge teams to share one big vision. [10,35]
0.45 TIMELINE
   - 1962 :: Mike Bezos, 16, flies alone from Cuba to Miami. [37]
   - 12 Jan 1964 :: Jeffrey Preston Jorgensen is born in Albuquerque to 17-year-old Jackie. [37,36]
   - 1965 :: Jackie files for divorce from Ted Jorgensen; Jeff is about 17 months old. [40,36]
   - 1968 :: Jackie marries Mike Bezos; Jeff, 4, is adopted and becomes a Bezos. [37,10,42]
   - Age 4 :: First of twelve summers on his grandfather's ranch in Cotulla, Texas. [10,35]
   - c. 1974 :: Fourth grade: teaches himself to program a school teletype. Around age 10, makes his grandmother cry. [10,35]
   - 1977 :: Profiled anonymously as gifted sixth-grader “Tim” in a book on gifted education. [42]
0.46 P Later, Jeff explained what this childhood really gave him. It was not money; the family started with very little. It was people who had his back. __When you know someone loves you no matter what, he argued, you can afford to take big risks.__ [35,36]
0.47 P Jackie never stopped teaching. Years later she and Mike co-founded the Bezos Family Foundation, and she spent her time there on early learning and how young children's brains develop. She died in 2025. Her own line could sum up her life: “I've devoted my life to providing opportunities for others to learn.” [47]
0.48 NOTE Lesson: build your safety net of people before you take big risks.

=== CH1 growing-up | Ages 13–18 | Space Hotels and 300 Eggs
1.0 P When Jeff was a teenager, the family moved again, this time to Miami, Florida. It was the same city where Mike had landed alone as a refugee years before. Jeff enrolled at Miami Palmetto Senior High School. [10,37,11]
1.1 P By his own account, he was not a cool kid. He calls himself “a very nerdy and good student” who did his homework on time and liked school. He also had a famously loud laugh that he says he has had all his life. For a while, his brother and sister refused to go to the movies with him because it was too embarrassing. [10]
1.2 QUOTE "When I was 15, and they were 10 and 9, I had no time for them whatsoever." -- Jeff Bezos on his younger siblings, 2001 [10]
1.3 NOTE Honest: even future billionaires find little siblings annoying.
1.4 H The ranch stops being pretend
1.5 P Every summer until he was 16, Jeff still went back to the ranch in Cotulla. By then the “helping” was real. He and his grandfather did their own veterinary work on the cattle, fixed windmills, laid water pipes, and built fences and barns. The Atlantic adds that he helped castrate bulls. [35,10,43]
1.6 P The big job was the bulldozer, a D-6 Caterpillar. When it broke, they repaired it themselves. The gears were huge, so they built their own cranes just to lift them out. When an animal got sick, his grandfather did not call a vet either. He worked out what to do himself. [10,35]
1.7 QUOTE "I could fix prolapsed cattle; we did all our own veterinary work. Some of the cattle even survived." -- Jeff Bezos, 2018 [35]
1.8 P Asked what the ranch taught him, Jeff gave a short answer: “Being resourceful. If there's a problem, there's a solution.” But he added a twist he learned later in business. __Doing everything yourself works on a ranch; building a company means making a whole team resourceful, not just you.__ [35,38]
1.9 PHOTO https://commons.wikimedia.org/wiki/File:Cotulla,_TX_sign_IMG_3325.JPG | The town sign of Cotulla, Texas, home of his grandfather's ranch. Photographed in 2011. | Billy Hathorn | CC BY-SA 3.0 | 2011
1.10 NOTE Step one: solve it yourself. Step two: teach a team to.
1.11 H The breakfast shift at McDonald's
1.12 P At ==16==, Jeff got his first job: short-order cook on the breakfast shift at a Miami McDonald's, starting before sunrise. He worked strictly in the kitchen and never on the cash registers. The hardest part, he said, was keeping up during the morning rush. [11]
1.13 EX For example: his favourite shift was Saturday morning. “The first thing I would do is get a big bowl and crack 300 eggs into it,” he recalled. One gift the job gave him, he said, was the ability to crack eggs with one hand. [11]
1.14 P His first week did not go smoothly. A five-gallon ketchup dispenser on the wall got stuck open and dumped ketchup into every hard-to-reach corner of the kitchen. As the new kid, Jeff was handed the cleaning solution and told to get started. [11]
1.15 QUOTE "You can learn responsibility in any job, if you take it seriously." -- Jeff Bezos on his McDonald's job [11]
1.16 NOTE Future richest man alive, cleaning up ketchup. No job is beneath you.
1.17 H Computers, teachers and pranks
1.18 P In 11th grade Jeff got his own computer, an ==Apple II Plus==, and kept tinkering. He credits a run of strong teachers: Mr. Bohr in calculus, Ms. Del Champs in chemistry and Ms. Ruehl in physics. Back in fourth or fifth grade, a maths teacher named Mrs. McInerney had already left a mark. [10]
1.19 PHOTO https://commons.wikimedia.org/wiki/File:Apple_II_Plus.jpg | An Apple II Plus, the computer Jeff got in 11th grade. This one was photographed in 2010. | Bilby | CC BY 3.0 | 2010
1.20 P He admits he was “unnaturally on the side of not getting in trouble.” In high school he and his friends played pranks, but, he says, the kind that teachers secretly loved. He liked pleasing teachers, and he says great teachers make learning feel so important that you want more of it. [10]
1.21 P He also had a habit that would later make him rich. He looked at everything around him and saw how it could be better. Even sitting in a restaurant, he would run through ways the place could be improved. [35]
1.22 NOTE Founders notice what's broken. Train this habit everywhere you go.
1.23 H The boy who wanted to empty the Earth
1.24 P His real obsession was space. As a teen he read The High Frontier, a 1976 book by the Princeton physicist Gerard K. O'Neill. It argued that humans could live in giant colonies floating in space. Jeff was hooked by the idea of moving people and heavy industry off the planet. [43]
1.25 P His high-school girlfriend, Ursula Werner, later said he was not optimistic about what humans were doing to Earth, and that was before global warming was a big public issue. He was also, she said, “really intrigued by the idea of rocketing into outer space.” Years later, reporters tracked her down and she put it even more bluntly. [44,43]
1.26 QUOTE "The reason he's earning so much money is to get to outer space." -- Ursula Werner, Jeff's high-school girlfriend [43]
1.27 P In ==1982== Jeff graduated as valedictorian, the top student in his class at Miami Palmetto. He used his graduation speech to describe millions of people moving to colonies in space. The Miami Herald interviewed the 18-year-old about his plans. [43,44,42]
1.28 EX For example: he told the Herald he wanted to “build space hotels, amusement parks and colonies for 2 million or 3 million people who would be in orbit.” His reason: “The whole idea is to preserve the Earth.” The paper summed up his goal as turning Earth into “a huge national park.” [42,43]
1.29 FACTS
   - 1982 :: graduates as valedictorian of Miami Palmetto Senior High [43]
   - 16 :: his age at his first job, McDonald's breakfast cook [11]
   - 300 :: eggs cracked into one bowl on a Saturday shift [11]
   - 2–3 million :: people he imagined living in orbit, at age 18 [42]
1.30 P Most teenage dreams fade. This one did not. In 2000 he founded a private rocket company, Blue Origin, to make space travel cheaper, and it still runs on the same idea he described at 18. __The thing you are obsessed with at 17 can quietly become the work of your life.__ [44,43]
1.31 NOTE Write down your 17-year-old dream. Check it again at 35.
1.32 P There is a fair criticism here too. Jeff was book-smart and knew it. “I have always been academically smart,” he told an audience in Washington in 2018. By his own telling he also had little time for the people around him, like the younger siblings he tried to shut out of his room. The grandfather who taught him kindness had spotted that weak side early. Princeton would test the strong side. [43,10,35]

=== CH2 first-steps | Ages 18–30 | From Physics Dream to the Drive West
2.0 P In 1982, Jeff arrived at Princeton University in New Jersey. He went there mainly because he wanted to study physics, and Princeton was one of the best places in the world to study physics. [10,43]
2.1 P Things went well until quantum mechanics. By then about 30 students were left in the class. Jeff was still getting good grades, but every problem was a slog. He watched three or four classmates absorb ideas easily that took him 12 hours of work. [10]
2.2 QUOTE "I realized I'm never going to be a great physicist." -- Jeff Bezos, 2001 [10]
2.3 P In 2018 he told a funnier version on stage. He had spent three hours stuck on a hard maths problem, a partial differential equation. So he went to the smartest student he knew, Yasantha Rajakarunanayake, a young man from Sri Lanka. Without writing anything down, Yasantha worked out the answer in seconds: cosine. [45,46]
2.4 P Then he wrote out three pages of algebra to prove it. Jeff asked if he had done it in his head. No, Yasantha said. He had solved a similar problem three years earlier and simply mapped one onto the other. Jeff called him “the smartest guy at Princeton.” [45,46]
2.5 QUOTE "That was the very moment when I realized I was never going to be a great theoretical physicist." -- Jeff Bezos at the Economic Club of Washington, 2018 [45]
2.6 NOTE Knowing what you're NOT best at is a superpower. Switch early.
2.7 P He admits giving up physics was hard; it had been his goal for years. But he was already taking computer science classes and found himself pulled toward them. He learned not just to hack code but the maths and algorithms behind it. __He didn't quit learning; he moved to the field where his talent gave him an edge.__ [10]
2.8 P Space stayed with him. Jeff became president of Princeton's chapter of SEDS, Students for the Exploration and Development of Space, and attended seminars by Gerard O'Neill, the author of the space-colony book he had read as a teen. He graduated in ==1986== with a degree in electrical engineering and computer science. [10,43,48]
2.9 PHOTO https://commons.wikimedia.org/wiki/File:Jeffrey_P._Bezos_1986.jpg | Jeffrey P. Bezos in the Princeton University yearbook, 1986, the year he graduated at 22. | Princeton University | Public domain | 1986
2.10 H The startup he didn't start
2.11 P At 22, Jeff already wanted to start a company. He even talked to a couple of friends about it. Then he made a decision that many young founders skip: he decided to wait and learn how business and the world actually worked first. [10]
2.12 QUOTE "One of the things that it's very hard to believe when you're 22 or 23 years old is that you don't already know everything." -- Jeff Bezos, 2001 [10]
2.13 P Instead he joined a young startup in New York called Fitel, which was building a computer network to help brokerage firms clear their trades. He admits it sounds obscure, but it used his technical skills and he loved the people. Then he moved to the bank Bankers Trust and rose to vice president. [10]
2.14 P From then on he worked where computers meet money. That is how he landed at D. E. Shaw, a New York hedge fund that used computers to trade stocks. As Jeff put it, they programmed the computers, and the computers made the trades. [10]
2.15 EX For example: D. E. Shaw hunted talent by mailing unsolicited letters to top students on the dean's lists of elite universities. The letters said, without embarrassment: “We approach our recruiting in unapologetically elitist fashion.” [43]
2.16 P The founder, the computer scientist David E. Shaw, had already played with the early internet in the 1980s and saw that it could change business. He gave Jeff the job of finding investment ideas in this new online world. In those years, The Atlantic reports, the two even kicked around the idea of an “everything store.” Jeff rose fast and became a senior vice president. [43,10]
2.17 NOTE The Amazon idea came from his day job. Look closely at yours.
2.18 P Jeff's personal life had a very Jeff-like chapter. Before he met his future wife, The Guardian reports, he tried to apply analytics to dating. He called it “women flow” and thought ballroom dancing might improve his odds. Then, at D. E. Shaw, he interviewed a fellow Princeton graduate, MacKenzie Tuttle, for a job. [42,10]
2.19 P MacKenzie later said that she was the one who chased him, not the other way round. Within three months they were engaged, and three months after that they were married. The wedding was in 1993. He was 29. [42,10]
2.20 H The number that changed everything
2.21 P In the spring of 1994, Jeff came across a statistic that stopped him cold. Use of the World Wide Web was growing at about ==2,300 percent a year==. “You know, things just don't grow that fast,” he said later. He started asking which business plan would make sense with growth like that. [10]
2.22 P Being a methodical quant, he made a list of the top 20 mail-order businesses and asked which could be run better online. Books won. There were millions of titles, so no paper catalogue could ever list them all, but a website could. He flew to Los Angeles for the American Booksellers' convention and learned that the big book wholesalers already kept electronic lists of their stock. [10,39]
2.23 FACTS
   - 2,300% :: yearly growth in web use, spring 1994 [10]
   - 20 :: mail-order businesses he listed before choosing books [10]
   - 70% :: his own estimate of the chance his parents would lose their money [10]
   - 30 :: his age when he quit Wall Street [10]
2.24 P D. E. Shaw did not want to do it, so Jeff decided to do it himself. He went to his boss and said he was going to leave and start a company selling books online. His boss said, “Let's go on a walk.” They walked through Central Park for two hours. [10]
2.25 QUOTE "You know, this actually sounds like a really good idea to me, but it sounds like it would be a better idea for somebody who didn't already have a good job." -- Jeff's boss at D. E. Shaw, as Jeff recalled it [10]
2.26 P His boss asked him to think about it for 48 hours. Jeff needed a way to make a decision this big. He invented one and gave it a name that, he joked, “only a nerd would call” it: the regret minimisation framework. He imagined himself at 80, looking back on his life, and asked which choice he would regret. [10]
2.27 QUOTE "I knew that if I failed I wouldn't regret that, but I knew the one thing I might regret is not ever having tried." -- Jeff Bezos, 2001 [10]
2.28 NOTE Very important for first-time founders! Most regrets are things you didn't do.
2.29 P Then came the family. In 1994, he says, almost everyone's first question was “What's the internet?” MacKenzie, though, was all in from the start: “Great — let's go!” His mother was more cautious. “Don't quit your job,” she warned, and asked whether he could do it on nights and weekends instead. [35,39]
2.30 P His father's first question was also “What's the Internet?” Jeff told his parents the honest truth: he thought there was a ==70 percent== chance they would lose every dollar. They invested a large part of their life savings anyway. “As corny as it sounds, we were betting on Jeff,” Mike said later. [10,39]
2.31 EX For example: Jeff's 70 percent was not pessimism. He reckoned most startups have only about a 10 percent chance of working, so he was giving himself triple the normal odds. Being realistic, he said, took away the fear of failing, because failure was simply the most likely outcome. [10]
2.32 H The drive west
2.33 P He chose Seattle. It was close to the giant book wholesaler Ingram and full of computer talent. The Guardian notes a sharper reason too: under US law back then, the company had to collect sales tax only on sales within its own state. [10,42]
2.34 P Over the Independence Day weekend of ==July 1994==, Jeff and MacKenzie flew to Texas and picked up a 1988 Chevy Blazer, a gift from Mike. Then they drove across America. MacKenzie drove. Jeff sat beside her and typed the first draft of a 30-page business plan. [10]
2.35 P From the road, he called a friend in Seattle to recommend a lawyer. The friend suggested his own divorce lawyer, and Jeff took him. When the lawyer asked on the phone what to call the company, Jeff said “Cadabra, as in abra cadabra.” The lawyer replied: “Cadaver?” Jeff knew then it was not going to be a good name. A few months later, it was changed. [10]
2.36 P He stopped in San Francisco to interview engineers. There he found Shel Kaphan, the programmer who would build the company's early systems and whom Jeff later called the most important person in Amazon's history. __Business plan, lawyer, first hire: none of it was glamorous, but it was all done before the car reached Seattle.__ [10]

   - 1982–86 :: Realises he won't be a great physicist; switches to computer science; leads the SEDS space club. [10,45]
   - 1986 :: Graduates in electrical engineering and computer science, aged 22. [10,48]
   - After 1986 :: Works at the startup Fitel, then Bankers Trust, where he becomes a vice president. [10]
   - Before 1994 :: Joins hedge fund D. E. Shaw; becomes senior vice president. [10]
   - 1993 :: Marries MacKenzie Tuttle, whom he met at D. E. Shaw. [10,42]
   - Spring 1994 :: Reads that web use is growing 2,300% a year; picks books. [10]

2.38 P Look at the order in which Jeff did things. At 22 he wanted to be a founder, but he spent eight more years learning technology and finance first. He jumped only when the data showed a window opening and he had a clear plan for using it. __He didn't wait for courage; he waited until the odds and the timing made sense, then moved fast.__ [10]
2.39 NOTE He was 30 here. Not 19. Late starts are still starts.
2.40 P In the summer of 1994, a 30-year-old with a good Wall Street job, a new wife and a hand-me-down car rolled into Seattle. His parents were ready to bet their savings on him, and he had a company with a name that sounded like “cadaver.” The real story was about to start, in a garage. [10]

=== CH3 every-company | Every venture, win or lose | Every Company He Ever Started
3.0 P Most biographies give you two names: Amazon and Blue Origin. The real list is longer and much funnier. It starts with a summer camp that signed up six children. It runs through a news service that a bank killed before it began. It includes a phone that sold so badly it was cut to 99 cents, and a health-care company built with Warren Buffett that closed within about three years. [49,50,51,52]
3.1 P Read this chapter as a scorecard. Each entry shows the idea, the choice behind it, where the first money came from, what happened and what to copy. __Count the failures as you go, because the wins only make sense next to them.__ []
3.2 NOTE Twenty-odd ventures. Count how many you had already heard of.
3.3 H Before Amazon
3.4 VENTURE The DREAM Institute (High-school summer (early 1980s)) shut
  idea: A 10-day summer camp for younger children, meant to make them think and read widely. The required reading included Dune, The Lord of the Rings, Gulliver's Travels and Stranger in a Strange Land.
  decision: He had spent a teenage summer working at McDonald's and disliked it. The next summer, instead of finding another job, he and his girlfriend created their own product.
  howStarted: Two teenagers, a reading list and a price. Six children signed up.
  funding: No investors. Parents paid $600 per child. Six children at $600 is $3,600 at most, by our arithmetic.
  what: It was a tiny one-off, and we found no record of it continuing. Then he left for Princeton. Its real value is what it shows: a teenager who would rather charge for an idea than wait for a wage.
  lesson: Charge real money for your first idea, even if only six people pay. A price tells you more than a compliment. [49,53]
3.5 VENTURE Worldwatch, the news service with Halsey Minor (About 1990) failed
  idea: Take several news feeds, sort them by topic and send each banker only the stories that matched their work. Think of a personal news feed, delivered by fax or email, long before most people had a feed at all.
  decision: Bezos was so taken with it that he nearly quit his job at Bankers Trust. Minor had worked at Merrill Lynch, and the pitch to the bank was simple: bankers would value a filter.
  howStarted: Two young men in New York and a partnership, with Merrill Lynch as the planned first customer and backer.
  funding: Merrill was going to put up $750,000, Minor recalled. Forbes reported in 1998 that the bank first approved the project and then pulled the plug. Merrill had lost money in 1990.
  what: They tried to work out how to raise the money elsewhere. On a phone call, they agreed they could not, and went their separate ways. Minor, crushed, later founded CNET. By 1998 his stake in it was valued at $184 million. Bezos moved on, and four years later he was driving to Seattle.
  lesson: Do not build a startup on one backer's promise. And keep your failures in perspective: both men recovered very well from this one. [50,54,49]
3.6 H The engine
3.7 P Everything else in this chapter depends on the next entry. Amazon's shares pay for the rockets, and his Amazon wealth bought the newspaper. Amazon's cash paid for most of the other experiments. So we give it the longest entry, and later chapters tell its story scene by scene. [55,56]
3.8 VENTURE Amazon.com (1994 to today) public
  idea: An online bookstore with millions of titles, something no physical shop could hold. The internet was growing fast, and he reckoned a store with unlimited shelves could only exist there.
  decision: He made the call, he says, with his heart more than his head, because he expected to regret not trying more than he would regret failing. He avoided Silicon Valley partly because of the sales tax he would have had to charge California shoppers. He then chose Kleiner Perkins over General Atlantic for his venture money.
  howStarted: A garage at a rented three-bedroom house in Bellevue, near Seattle, with desks made from Home Depot doors. Shel Kaphan, the first employee, built the site. It opened on ==16 July 1995== and took $12,000 in orders in its first week. By the end of the first month it had sold to all 50 US states and 45 countries.
  funding: $10,000 of his own for 10.2 million shares, plus $84,000 in interest-free loans he made to the company. Then $245,573 from his parents, about $1 million from around 20 angels, $8 million from Kleiner Perkins and $54 million from the 1997 stock market listing.
  what: Sales went from $511,000 in 1995 to $15.7 million in 1996, and to ==$716.9 billion== in 2025, with $80 billion in operating profit. It came close to a cash crisis around 2000 and 2001, as the near-death chapter shows. Bezos stepped down as CEO in July 2021 and is now executive chair. Andy Jassy runs it.
  lesson: Pick a narrow first product inside a market that is growing wildly. Books were the door. The everything store came later. [57,58,59,60,61,62,63,64,13]

3.10 H The first big failures
3.11 VENTURE Amazon Auctions and zShops, later Marketplace (1999 to today) pivoted
  idea: Let other people sell through Amazon. Auctions copied eBay. zShops let small shops sell at fixed prices. Marketplace put outside sellers on the same page as Amazon's own stock.
  decision: eBay was the threat. Fortune noted that Amazon launched auctions two days after eBay announced a share sale. The bigger choice came later. Amazon decided to let rival sellers compete directly beside its own products, even when that took a sale from itself.
  howStarted: Auctions launched in March 1999 and zShops that autumn.
  funding: Amazon's own cash and borrowings. No separate investors.
  what: By Bezos's own telling, almost nobody came to Auctions and zShops did little better. The third attempt worked. In 2017 outside sellers passed half of all items sold. By the third quarter of 2026 they were ==61 percent== of units, and seller services brought in $172.2 billion in 2025.
  lesson: A failed product may be the right idea in the wrong shape. Change the form before you throw out the idea. [65,66,67,68]

3.14 VENTURE The dot-com stakes: Pets.com, Kozmo, drugstore.com, HomeGrocer, living.com and more (1998 to 2001) failed
  idea: Buy big pieces of other online shops. Amazon would get a share of each new category without building it, and those shops would pay Amazon to promote them.
  decision: Amazon believed the internet was a land rush: grab ground now, make it pay later. In 1999 Fortune counted 46 percent of Drugstore.com, 35 percent of HomeGrocer, 54 percent of Pets.com and 49 percent of Gear.com. Amazon announced a major move about every six weeks that year.
  howStarted: Bezos and his team made these investments in rapid succession during the boom. Partners also agreed to pay fees, such as $105 million over three years from drugstore.com and $145 million over five years from living.com.
  funding: Amazon's stock and the billions it had borrowed. About $57.8 million went into Pets.com, $62.5 million into HomeGrocer and $60 million for 32 percent of Kozmo.
  what: The bust wiped most of it out. Pets.com closed in November 2000, sacking about 255 of its 320 staff, with $147 million in cumulative losses. Living.com went bankrupt and Kozmo shut in 2001. Amazon recorded $305 million in investment losses in 2000. It kept selling the Pets.com sock puppet at $19.99 even after the company had folded, 100 times the price of a Pets.com share.
  lesson: Owning part of a business does not fix its economics. Check whether each order makes money before you buy in. [66,69,70,71,72,68,73]
3.15 EX For example: Amazon's most consequential small deal of 1998 had an Indian team behind it. Junglee was started in 1996 by Rakesh Mathur and Stanford PhD students, including Anand Rajaraman, Venky Harinarayan and Ashish Gupta. Four of its five founders were of Indian origin. 3.16 VENTURE Personal investing and Bezos Expeditions (1998 to today) running
  idea: Put his own money into young companies and science projects, separate from Amazon. Bezos Expeditions is the name of the vehicle he uses.
  decision: The first famous cheque went to Google in 1998. Asked why, he told author Ken Auletta that he simply fell in love with the two founders. His official list of holdings now includes many AI and robotics firms.
  howStarted: A meeting in a Menlo Park garage in 1998, arranged by Shriram. Bezos was one of only a few early angels.
  funding: His own money. The Google cheque was reported at $250,000, at four cents a share by Auletta's account.
  what: That cheque became about 3.3 million Google shares at the 2004 IPO. An Amazon spokesman declined to say what he did with them. The official site now lists Airbnb, Uber, Twitter, Workday, Stack Overflow, Remitly, Perplexity, Figure and Physical Intelligence among others. He also backed Cambrian Ventures, the fund of Junglee's Rajaraman and Harinarayan.
  lesson: Your first success opens doors you did not know about. Stay close to the smart people you meet on the way, because the next deal usually comes through them. [79,7,80,76]
3.17 H The rocket
3.18 VENTURE Blue Origin (2000 to today) running
  idea: Make getting to space far cheaper by reusing rockets, so that one day heavy industry can move off Earth. He says the point is to protect the planet, and he has wanted this since high school.
  decision: In 1999 he and novelist Neal Stephenson saw the rocket film October Sky and went for coffee. Bezos said he had long wanted a space company, and Stephenson asked why he did not start one that day. The team first explored alternatives to chemical rockets, then decided that conventional rockets, reused, were the right road.
  howStarted: Blue Operations LLC, in a former envelope factory in south Seattle. Stephenson says he was the only employee for a little while. Early members included inventor Danny Hillis, science historian George Dyson and Amazon's general counsel. Workers would tell neighbours only that they did scientific research.
  funding: Bezos alone for 26 years, with small NASA awards of $3.7 million and $22 million along the way. In 2017 he said he was selling about $1 billion of Amazon stock a year to pay for it. The Wall Street Journal, cited by CNBC, puts the total near ==$30 billion==.
  what: First test hop in November 2006, reaching 285 feet. A vehicle lost in August 2011. First rocket to land after reaching space in November 2015. Bezos flew in July 2021. New Glenn reached orbit in January 2025. In May 2026 a rocket blew up on the pad during a test, and SpaceNews says the rebuilt pad should be ready by the first half of December. That autumn, outside investors came in for the first time at a reported $140 billion.
  lesson: A very long project needs a money machine behind it. He built the machine first, then fed the dream from it. [81,82,83,84,85,55,86,87,88,89,90,91,92,93]
3.19 H The bets made with Amazon's money
3.20 VENTURE Amazon Prime (2005 to today) running
  idea: Pay once a year and get free two-day delivery on everything. All you can eat, for shipping.
  decision: Engineer Charlie Ward first raised the idea of a yearly fee. Bezos added speed to it. The team weighed $49 and $99 before he settled on $79, big enough to matter but small enough to try. Some senior people pushed back hard and told the team it should not let him do this.
  howStarted: A small secret team, code-named Futurama, built it in about six weeks over the 2004 holidays.
  funding: Amazon's own cash flow. Bezos told customers it would be expensive for Amazon in the short term.
  what: Launched on 2 February 2005. Tens of thousands signed up in the first month. The price rose to $99 in 2014. Video streaming was added later as a free extra. Subscription services, which include Prime fees, brought in $49.6 billion in 2025.
  lesson: Sell the habit, not the product. Once someone has paid up front, they stop comparing prices elsewhere. [94,95,96,97,67]
3.21 VENTURE Amazon Web Services (2006 to today) running
  idea: Rent out computing power and storage over the internet, by the hour, so that anyone could run software without buying servers.
  decision: At a 2003 executive offsite at Bezos's home, his team asked what Amazon was truly good at. Running reliable, cheap data centres came out of that exercise. Fortune calls the decision to sell this to outsiders the turning point, one that could easily have gone the other way.
  howStarted: Andy Jassy wrote the plan, which reportedly went through 31 drafts. Chris Pinkham, who wanted to go home to South Africa, set up a team in Cape Town that built the computing service. By late 2004 a small office of about 14 people was working on it.
  funding: Amazon's own cash. There was no outside round and no separate company.
  what: Storage launched in March 2006 and computing that August. Amazon hid the numbers until 2015. In 2025 AWS earned $128.7 billion in sales and $45.6 billion in operating profit. By the third quarter of 2026 it made ==63 percent== of Amazon's operating profit on about 23 percent of its sales.
  lesson: List what you had to get good at to survive. One of those painful skills may be a bigger business than the one you are in. [98,99,100,101,63,102,67]
3.22 VENTURE Kindle and Lab126 (2004 to today) running
  idea: A reading device with a built-in wireless link that could fetch a book in under a minute, so Amazon would own digital books before Apple or Google could.
  decision: In 2004 Bezos took Steve Kessel off the job of running books, which Kessel thought was the best job in the world, and told him to act as if his goal was to put everyone selling physical books out of work. Bezos insisted one person could not run both the old and the new business.
  howStarted: A secret hardware lab called Lab126 in Silicon Valley, led by Palm veteran Gregg Zehr. Amazon had never built hardware. The code name, Fiona, came from a Neal Stephenson novel.
  funding: Amazon's own cash. Kessel first estimated 18 months and a small team. It took three and a half years and far more people.
  what: Launched on 19 November 2007 at $399, with 90,000 books, most new titles and bestsellers at $9.99. The first stock sold out in about five and a half hours, and Amazon ran out again the next Christmas.
  lesson: If your business will be disrupted, do it yourself first. And give the new team a boss who has no old business to protect. [103,104,105,106,107]
3.23 H Abroad: one retreat, one fight
3.24 VENTURE Amazon China (Joyo) (2004 to 2019) shut
  idea: Enter the world's most populous market by buying a local online book and media seller.
  decision: Amazon chose to buy rather than build, paying $75 million for Joyo in 2004. Its largest shareholder at the time was Lei Jun, who later founded Xiaomi.
  howStarted: It kept the Joyo business and later rebranded it under the Amazon name.
  funding: Amazon's own cash.
  what: It held about 15 percent of the market around 2011 and 2012, and under 1 percent by 2019. Alibaba's Tmall and JD.com controlled 82 percent by then. Amazon closed its China marketplace on 18 July 2019, keeping cross-border sales, Kindle and cloud computing.
  lesson: A global brand does not beat local habits. Payment, delivery and marketing have to fit the country, or a local rival will fit it for you. [108,109,110]
3.25 VENTURE Amazon India (2012 to today) running
  idea: After China, do not lose the last big e-commerce market. Enter India as a marketplace, with Amazon handling the warehousing and delivery for local sellers.
  decision: Indian rules barred foreign-owned online retailers from selling their own stock directly, so Amazon had to use the marketplace model. It sent Amit Agarwal, from Bezos's senior team in Seattle, to lead. It revived the old Junglee name first as a price-comparison site in February 2012.
  howStarted: Amazon.in opened in June 2013 with about 100 sellers, selling only books, films and TV shows, from a warehouse near Mumbai. Flipkart, founded by two former Amazon employees, had a six-year head start.
  funding: Amazon's cash. In July 2014, one day after Flipkart announced a $1 billion raise, Bezos announced $2 billion for India. Amazon spent it in under two years and added $3 billion in 2016.
  what: Amazon reached 100 million customers by 2018, with annual losses of up to about $1 billion, but still trailed the Flipkart group on gross sales. Walmart bought Flipkart in 2018. Bernstein estimated Amazon had invested around $6.5 billion, with India still unprofitable as of 2023.
  lesson: Money can buy you speed in a price war, but not automatically first place. Your rival can find a bigger backer too. [111,112,113,114,115,116]

3.27 H Bought, not built
3.28 VENTURE Zappos (Bought 2009) running
  idea: The top online shoe seller, famous for customer service and an unusual company culture.
  decision: Bezos visited in 2005 and was told no at any price, by Tony Hsieh's account. Four years later the recession had tightened credit, and Hsieh writes that his board wanted a sale and that he feared being replaced. Amazon agreed to preserve Zappos's independence and culture, and that made a deal possible.
  howStarted: Amazon came back after waiting four years. Zappos was by then doing over $1 billion in gross sales and had leaned on a $100 million credit line for stock.
  funding: About 10 million Amazon shares, plus $40 million in cash and restricted stock for staff. It was worth about $1.2 billion by closing because Amazon's stock rose.
  what: Zappos still runs as a separate brand. Hsieh's essay remains a warning about how investors can force a founder's hand when credit runs out.
  lesson: Founders: outside money gives outsiders a vote. Buyers: keep what makes the target special, and be ready to wait years. [117,118,119,120]
3.29 VENTURE Quidsi (Diapers.com) (Bought 2010, shut 2017) shut
  idea: Marc Lore and Vinit Bharara started Diapers.com in New Jersey in 2005, an online shop built around what new parents buy again and again.
  decision: Amazon slashed its own diaper prices while Quidsi was growing, which squeezed its margins and made investors shy. Brad Stone's book reports that Bezos threatened to take big losses and drive prices towards zero. Quidsi talked to Walmart, and Amazon won the bidding war.
  howStarted: Quidsi was a going business. Amazon bought it in November 2010.
  funding: $545 million from Amazon. The founders had raised money from venture investors, who grew reluctant during the price war.
  what: Amazon said it could never make the unit profitable, and closed the Quidsi sites in 2017, laying off 263 people. Lore left, built Jet.com and sold it to Walmart for $3.3 billion. The mistakes chapter covers the questions lawmakers later asked about it.
  lesson: If a giant can match your whole product, a price lead will not save you. Build something it cannot copy just by spending. [121,120,122]
3.30 VENTURE The Washington Post (Bought 2013) running
  idea: Buy a famous, struggling newspaper personally, give it time and money, and help it find a national digital audience.
  decision: The Graham family had owned it for 80 years. Revenue had fallen for seven years straight, and publisher Katharine Weymouth warned of a possible $40 million loss in 2013. Bankers at Allen & Co. connected the sides, and Graham and Bezos met at the Sun Valley conference in July and quickly agreed. He told staff he could offer financial runway.
  howStarted: A $250 million cash purchase through a company of his own, not Amazon. He kept the senior editors in place.
  funding: His own money. Reuters noted that the price was about four times what comparable newspaper deals suggested.
  what: The paper had profitable years around the first Trump term. Then it lost $77 million in 2023 and about $100 million in 2024. In February 2026 it cut about a third of its staff. The mistakes chapter tells the wider story.
  lesson: Patient money still needs a working business model. Runway only helps if the plane can eventually fly. [56,123,124,125,126,127,128]
3.31 H The phone and the voice
3.32 VENTURE Fire Phone (2014 to 2015) failed
  idea: An Amazon smartphone with a 3D-like display, face-tracking cameras and a free year of Prime.
  decision: Bezos defended it as a bold bet and said a phone would take many tries. Amazon chose an exclusive deal with one US carrier, AT&T, and launched just before Apple's new iPhones.
  howStarted: An internal devices team that grew to as many as 1,000 people, with Ian Freed a key leader.
  funding: Amazon's own money, reported at over $100 million for the team, plus the charge below.
  what: The $199 price fell to 99 cents with a two-year contract. An estimate put early sales at only 35,000. Amazon took a ==$170 million== charge in October 2014 and stopped selling it by September 2015. That same October, Amazon reported its biggest quarterly loss in 14 years.
  lesson: Do not enter a crowded market with a feature nobody asked for. But do keep the people and the code, because the next bet may need them. [51,129,130,131]
3.33 VENTURE Echo and Alexa (2011 to today) running
  idea: A speaker you talk to, with its brain in Amazon's cloud. Bezos's stated dream was the talking computer from Star Trek.
  decision: He sketched it himself on a whiteboard in early 2011. He wanted it to cost $20. He picked his technical adviser Greg Hart to lead it, even though Hart said he had no hardware experience. Bezos chose the name, after the ancient library of Alexandria.
  howStarted: A team that grew past 200 people with a budget above $50 million, per The New Yorker. Fire Phone people and lessons fed into it.
  funding: Amazon's own cash.
  what: Launched quietly on 6 November 2014 with a press release and a short video, not a big stage show. It created a whole category, and Amazon spoke of hundreds of millions of devices by 2020. It also cost a fortune: The Wall Street Journal reported tens of billions lost across the devices unit.
  lesson: A hit product can still be a poor business. Ask how it will make money before you ship a hundred million. [132,133,131,134]
3.34 H The graveyard
3.35 P Fire Phone gets the headlines, but the small failures teach you more about how the place ran. People could pitch an idea and build it with very little second-guessing. Most of these experiments quietly died. Bloomberg listed 37 projects killed after Jassy took over in 2021, projects it described as dreamed up during the Bezos era. [135]
3.36 VENTURE The small flops: WebPay, Wallet, Local, Local Register, Destinations, Dash, Restaurants, Halo (2007 to 2023) shut
  idea: Quick tests, each aimed at a rival's turf: PayPal and Square in payments, Groupon in local deals, Expedia in travel, the delivery apps in restaurant food. Dash was a physical button that reordered one household item. Halo was a health-tracking band.
  decision: Amazon's rule was to try, measure and move on. Bezos has argued that failure and invention are twins, and that a large company should run proportionately large experiments.
  howStarted: Small internal teams. Destinations launched in April 2015. Wallet lasted about six months. Restaurants began in 2015 and reached 20 US cities plus London.
  funding: Amazon's own budget in every case, with amounts rarely made public.
  what: WebPay closed in 2014 after seven years. Destinations stopped within six months, in October 2015. Local closed that December. Local Register ended in February 2016. Dash buttons stopped in 2019, and Restaurants closed in June 2019. Halo stopped working in August 2023, with Amazon refunding recent buyers.
  lesson: Run cheap experiments, kill them quickly, and make sure the failures are small enough that you can afford many. [136,137,65,138,139,135,140]
3.37 VENTURE Whole Foods Market (Bought 2017) running
  idea: Buy a real-world, upmarket grocery chain with more than 460 stores to push into fresh food.
  decision: Activist investor Jana Partners took a stake of nearly 9 percent in April 2017 and pressed for change. Whole Foods looked for a friendly buyer. Amazon offered $41 a share, Whole Foods asked for $45, and Amazon named $42 as its best and final price.
  howStarted: CEO John Mackey and three executives flew to Seattle for what he called a blind date, arranged through mutual friends. Seven suitors circled in all, and Amazon came close to walking away.
  funding: $13.7 billion, its largest deal ever.
  what: By January 2026 Amazon reported over 40 percent sales growth and more than 550 stores, with 100-plus new stores planned.
  lesson: When you cannot build the skill fast, buy it. Then know your walk-away price, and name it out loud. [141,142,143,144,145,146,147]
3.38 VENTURE Amazon Go and Amazon Fresh stores (2016 to 2026) shut
  idea: Shops with no checkout line. You scanned your phone, cameras and sensors watched what you took, and your account was charged when you left. Fresh stores later brought the idea to full supermarkets.
  decision: Amazon wanted a store format of its own to change physical shopping, not just to own Whole Foods.
  howStarted: A 1,800-square-foot shop in Seattle, open to staff from 5 December 2016 and to the public on 22 January 2018.
  funding: Amazon's own budget. It did not disclose the cost.
  what: In January 2026 Amazon said it had not created a distinctive experience with an economic model that could scale. It closed Go and Fresh stores within days, except in California, where they stayed open longer to meet state rules, and is converting some to Whole Foods. Fresh continues online.
  lesson: Clever technology is not a business model. If the shop does not earn its costs, shoppers' amazement will not save it. [148,145,144,149,147]
3.39 VENTURE Haven (2018 to 2021) shut
  idea: Three giants, Amazon, Berkshire Hathaway and JPMorgan Chase, jointly tackle employee health-care costs.
  decision: The 2018 announcement was so bold that health-care shares fell. Surgeon and writer Atul Gawande was picked to lead it.
  howStarted: A nonprofit based in Boston, which had 57 staff at the end.
  funding: The three founding companies. The amount has not been disclosed.
  what: Gawande stepped back in May 2020. Each parent kept running its own projects with its own staff, which left Haven little to do. It closed at the end of February 2021. Bloomberg reported little evidence of new approaches.
  lesson: A partnership of giants needs one clear owner. If everyone also runs their own version, the joint project will die. [52,150,65]
3.40 VENTURE Amazon Care (2019 to 2022) shut
  idea: App-based medical care with video visits and nurses who came to your home, first for Amazon staff in Seattle, then for other employers.
  decision: Test on your own employees first, then sell the service to other companies.
  howStarted: A pilot for Amazon staff around Seattle in 2019.
  funding: Amazon's own budget.
  what: Few businesses signed up. Amazon said it was not a complete enough offering and ended it on 31 December 2022, cutting 159 jobs, while 236 more went at the contracted clinic. About a month earlier it had agreed to buy One Medical for $3.9 billion.
  lesson: Your own employees are a captive audience. Real customers, who can say no, are the real test. [151,152,135,153]
3.41 H His own money, his own causes
3.42 VENTURE Bezos Day One Fund and Bezos Earth Fund (2018 and 2020 to today) running
  idea: Two giving vehicles. Day One funds groups helping homeless families and runs free preschools. Earth Fund gives grants to fight climate change and protect nature.
  decision: He announced Day One in September 2018, a year after asking for ideas in public, and Earth Fund in February 2020. Each has one defined mission, and Earth Fund has a hard deadline.

3.43 VENTURE The 10,000-Year Clock and the Apollo engines (Clock: ongoing. Engines: 2010 to 2013) running

  decision: Neither was meant to earn money. Hillis was an early colleague at Blue Origin, and Bezos has dreamed of space since he was five.

  funding: His own money: a reported $42 million for the clock. The engine recovery was privately funded through Bezos Expeditions.

3.44 VENTURE Prometheus (2025 to today) running
  idea: AI for the physical economy: tools to speed up how engineers design and make real things. He calls the goal an artificial general engineer.
  decision: He had worked on it since late 2024, and it became the first CEO job he had taken since leaving that role at Amazon in 2021. He says it now takes the bulk of his time, and says it has nothing to do with robotics.
  howStarted: About 150 people by mid-2026 across San Francisco, London and Zurich, hired from places like OpenAI, Google DeepMind and Nvidia. His co-CEO is Vik Bajaj, who co-founded Alphabet's Verily.
  funding: $6.2 billion at launch in November 2025, with Bezos putting in capital. Then a $12 billion second round at a $41 billion valuation in June 2026. Bloomberg assumes he supplied about $5.6 billion of the first round and $2 billion of the second.
  what: Bezos says it is premature to disclose what it has achieved, though he calls the progress remarkable. Bloomberg values his stake at about $12 billion.
  lesson: Past success lets you skip the hard part of fundraising, not the hard part of building. Judge this one only when it ships. [64,160,161]

Zoox, the self-driving startup bought in 2020 for a reported $1 billion or more, now charges for robotaxi rides in Las Vegas and operates in 12 US cities. 4.0 H The money ladder
4.1 P Forget the legend for a minute and read the filing. Amazon's 1997 registration statement, the document a company must file before selling shares to the public, lists every early money move. In July 1994 Bezos bought 10.2 million shares for ==$10,000==. That is about a tenth of a cent per share. [60]
4.2 P He then lent the company $15,000, $29,000 and $40,000, with no interest, and it paid him back. He also personally guaranteed its bank merchant accounts and its company credit cards. So if Amazon failed, the bills would have come to him. He was not just the founder. In those first months he was the bank. [60]
4.3 FACTS
   - $10,000 :: Bezos's own money for 10.2 million founder shares, July 1994 [60]
   - $245,573 :: From his parents in two purchases, Feb and July 1995 [60,163]
   - About $1M :: From around 20 angels, 1995 and early 1996 [164,62]
   - $8M :: From Kleiner Perkins, June 1996 [60,61]
4.4 P The family money did not arrive as one big cheque. The filing shows Miguel Bezos buying 582,528 shares in February 1995. In July, a family trust led by his mother Jacklyn bought 847,716 shares. Both paid $0.1717 a share, and the two purchases add up to $245,573. His brother and sister each bought 30,000 shares in May 1996 at $0.3333. [60,163]
4.5 NOTE Filings beat folklore. Always read the boring document.
4.6 P Then he went looking for strangers, and that was brutal. Bezos told Congress that it took more than 50 meetings to raise $1 million, and that the most common question was what the internet was. Investors put in about $50,000 each for a little under 1 percent. Together they got about 20 percent of the company. [57,164]
4.7 P Tom Alberg, a former McCaw Cellular executive, was one who said yes. He remembered a friend walking away because Bezos priced the company at $6 million before the new money. Bezos would not budge, and the raise dragged on through nearly all of 1995. Brad Stone's account has another backer talking him down to $5 million. The filing shows Alberg paying $0.3333 a share for 150,000 shares, which is $50,000. [61,62,165,60]
4.8 EX For example: in 2016 Bezos worked out what those small early cheques could have become. A $50,000 stake held to that date, if never diluted, would have been worth about $3.5 billion, a return of 70,000 times. In practice, later rounds dilute early investors, meaning each share becomes a smaller slice as new shares are sold. [164]
4.9 H The garage
4.10 P The first home was a rented three-bedroom house in Bellevue, with desks built from Home Depot doors in the garage. The servers needed so much power that he and MacKenzie could not run a hair dryer or a vacuum without blowing a fuse. Early meetings were held at a local Barnes & Noble, which was the company they planned to beat. [58,59,166]
4.11 P The site opened on 16 July 1995. A bell rang for every order, and staff crowded round to check it was not somebody's mother. Within days it was too annoying, so they switched it off. Orders hit $12,000 in the first week and $14,000 in the second, and by the end of the first month they had come from all 50 states and 45 countries. [58,167,59]
4.12 EX For example: one customer in Bulgaria had internet access but no credit card. He mailed cash, hidden inside a floppy disk, and explained that customs inspectors stole money but could not read English. Inside were two crisp $100 bills. Bezos told this story himself in 2010. [167]
4.13 P They had no real warehouse then. Everyone spent the second half of the day packing books on their knees on a cement floor. Bezos suggested kneepads. Someone more experienced suggested packing tables, and output rose by 20 percent. Amazon did not stock most books at first; it ordered them from distributors, and Ingram supplied 59 percent of its purchases in 1996. [167,58,60]
4.14 NOTE He thought kneepads. Someone else said tables. Listen to the floor.
4.15 H Two venture firms and a choice
4.16 P Sales went from $511,000 in 1995 to $15.7 million in 1996. Venture capitalists, the professional investors who fund risky startups in exchange for shares, began to call. General Atlantic proposed a complicated price tied to whether and when Amazon went public. Kleiner Perkins offered a simple $60 million valuation. Bezos took the simple one. [60,61]
4.17 P The filing gives the exact deal: 569,396 preferred shares at $14.05, or ==$8 million==, signed on 21 June 1996. Preferred shares are a special class that turned into six ordinary shares each at the IPO, so Kleiner really paid about $2.34 per ordinary share. That was seven times Alberg's price from six months earlier. John Doerr joined the board. [60,61]
4.18 P Here is the detail almost nobody mentions. As part of that deal, Bezos gave Amazon the right to buy back 612,000 of his own shares at a tenth of a cent each if his job ended in certain circumstances. That right faded over 36 months. __Even the founder was put on a vesting schedule, which means earning your shares by staying.__ The filing ties it directly to the Kleiner round. [60]
4.19 NOTE Founders: expect this clause. Read it, and do not take it personally.
4.20 P The money changed the mood. Staff grew from 11 to 151 during 1996, and a new slogan went on the T-shirts: Get Big Fast. Early engineer Greg Linden remembered designing for four times the current load and hitting it within months. Bezos's logic was that Barnes & Noble would come online eventually, and Amazon had to be big enough by then. [60,168,169]
4.21 P He did not get rich on salary. The filing lists his pay as $64,333, with no bonus and no options. The company's total losses so far were $6 million. The risk section even admits that it depended heavily on Bezos and held no key-person life insurance, a policy that pays out if a vital leader dies. [60]
4.22 NOTE He owned nearly half the company and paid himself $64,333.
4.23 H Going public
4.24 P An IPO, or initial public offering, is when a private company first sells shares to anyone on a stock exchange. Amazon filed in March 1997, assuming a price of $13 a share and 2.5 million shares. By 15 May it sold 3 million shares at ==$18== and raised $54 million, at a market value of about $438 million. [60,62,68]
4.25 P The filing said Bezos would own about 43 percent, and his family another 10 percent, on the first draft of the deal. The bigger final deal brought his share to about 41 percent. His 9.885 million shares were worth roughly $178 million at the offer price. Kleiner's $8 million had turned into about $61.5 million on paper in 11 months. [60,170,68]
4.26 P The Scott Cook detail is worth noticing too. Cook, the co-founder of Intuit, and Patricia Stonesifer, a former senior Microsoft executive, each paid $100,000 for preferred shares in early 1997, joined the board and received stock options. Bezos was collecting people who had already built big things. [60,68]
4.27 P Barnes & Noble, the giant of the trade, answered with lawyers. Days before the IPO it sued over the slogan Earth's Biggest Bookstore. Amazon countersued, and in October the two settled with no damages paid and no liability admitted. A barnesandnoble.com employee later told WIRED that suing Amazon just was not cool. Analysts branded the company Amazon.toast all the same. [171,172,57,68]
4.28 EX For example: one $18 share from the IPO became 240 shares after four splits, in 1998, twice in 1999 and in 2022. A split cuts a share into more pieces without changing the total value. At the 30 October 2026 closing price of $240.35, that single share is worth about ==$57,684==, by our arithmetic. [173,67]

=== CH5 near-death | 1998 to 2002 | One Word: Ouch
5.0 H Borrowing billions
5.1 P Once it was public, Amazon discovered that Wall Street would lend it almost anything. In 1998 CFO Joy Covey raised $326 million in junk bonds, the first such issue by an internet company, with no payments due for five years. Junk bonds are loans to risky companies at high interest. This one yielded 10 percent. [174]
5.2 P Then came January 1999. Amazon asked for $500 million in convertible bonds and got ==$1.25 billion== the same day, because lenders kept offering. A convertible bond is a loan that the lender can swap for shares if the stock rises enough, in this case at about $156. So Amazon was paying 4.75 percent, and it did not even say what the money was for. [175,174]
5.3 P It spent fast. In 1998 and 1999 it put $429 million into warehouses and systems, and by late 1999 it reportedly had 70 to 80 percent more capacity than it needed. Its chief accounting officer warned Bezos that at this rate it would not be profitable for decades. In May 1999, Barron's ran a cover story calling the company Amazon.bomb. [165,57]
5.4 NOTE Easy money is the most dangerous kind. It hides your mistakes.
5.5 P Then came the decision that saved the company, and Bezos did not make it alone. New CFO Warren Jenson, who arrived in September 1999, feared suppliers might demand faster payment if confidence cracked. Advised by Morgan Stanley's Ruth Porat, he went to Europe in February 2000 and sold about $672 million of convertible bonds at nearly 7 percent. It was an expensive deal. [165,68]
5.6 P The Nasdaq peaked on 10 March, about a month later. Brad Stone reported that the deal could not have gone through just three weeks later, and that without that cushion Amazon would almost certainly have faced insolvency, meaning it could not pay its debts. __The best time to raise money is when you do not need it, because the door can shut without warning.__ [165]
5.7 EX For example: here is why cash mattered so much. A customer paid Amazon for a book immediately, but Amazon paid the supplier up to about 90 days later. So growth actually pumped cash into the company. If suppliers got nervous and demanded quicker payment, that pump would run in reverse, and fast. [165]
5.8 H The analyst who said it would die
5.9 P On 22 June 2000 a young Lehman Brothers bond analyst named Ravi Suria published a report that spread everywhere. He called Amazon's credit extremely weak and deteriorating, and said it would run out of cash within four quarters unless it pulled another financing rabbit from its hat. Its 4.75 percent bonds fell from about $75 to $58 in days, and the stock dropped about 20 percent in one day. [176,177,165]
5.10 P Bezos hit back on stage at PC Expo in New York days later. He told a crowd of nearly 1,000 that the analyst had not even called the company, and that Amazon had a billion dollars in cash. Behind the scenes, he, Jenson and treasurer Russ Grandinetti flew around reassuring suppliers. If the suppliers had panicked, the prediction could have come true by itself. [178,165]
5.11 P Suria kept going. In February 2001 he warned of a creditor squeeze in the second half of the year. His numbers were real: Amazon's working capital, its short-term assets minus short-term debts, had fallen to $386 million from over $700 million. Its shareholders' equity had gone to negative $967 million. Amazon's spokesman called it a silly report and said Suria had already missed his first prediction by $1.1 billion. [179,180,177]
5.12 FACTS
   - $116 to $6 :: Amazon's share price, peak to trough, 1999 to 2001, as Bezos told Congress [57]
   - $1.41B :: Net loss in 2000, after $720M in 1999 [179]
   - Nearly $3B :: Total losses from founding to the end of 2001 [57]
   - 1,300 :: Jobs cut in January 2001, 15 percent of staff [181,180]
5.13 P Bezos opened his letter to shareholders for 2000 with one word: Ouch. His own fortune shrank with the stock, from over $9 billion in mid-1999 to about $1.7 billion by 2003. Pundits thought Amazon was going out of business. But he also argued that the company was stronger than ever, and that a falling share price was not a failing business. [57,182,85,68]
5.14 NOTE The stock fell over 90 percent. He kept shipping. Separate price from business.
5.15 H The cuts and the coffee
5.16 P Survival needed real pain. In January 2001 Amazon cut 1,300 jobs, closed a warehouse in McDonough, Georgia, shut a Seattle customer service centre, and took a charge of over $150 million. It also stopped selling items that lost money. Bezos had resisted running to Wall Street's expectations, but the bust forced his hand. [181,180,165]
5.17 P It also rented out what it had built. In August 2000 it signed a 10-year deal to run the online toy store for Toysrus.com, handling the site, fulfilment and customer service. Borders followed in 2001, and Internet News reported $125 million coming in from Toys R Us after one Christmas. That excess warehouse space had started earning. In July 2001 AOL put ==$100 million== into Amazon shares. [183,184,185,165]
5.18 P That same year Bezos had coffee with Jim Sinegal, the founder of Costco, at a Starbucks inside a Barnes & Noble. He had come to talk about buying goods wholesale. Sinegal instead explained how Costco kept prices so low that members saw the value every time they walked in. Days later, Stone reports, Bezos called a meeting on Amazon's incoherent pricing. To be fair, Bezos has never publicly credited that coffee. [186]
5.19 P That summer Amazon cut book, music and video prices by up to 30 percent, while its cost cuts paid for it. Then, on 22 January 2002, came results that almost nobody had predicted. Sales for the last quarter of 2001 were $1.12 billion, its first billion-dollar quarter. Net profit was ==$5 million==, or one cent a share. [186,187,188,189]
5.20 NOTE One cent per share. Seven years in. Celebrate your small wins.
5.21 P In the same announcement he introduced Super Saver Shipping, free on orders over $99, as an everyday offer and not a promotion. He had just scraped into profit, and he gave the money straight back to customers. Bezos said there were two kinds of retailer, those working to raise prices and those working to lower them, and that Amazon had chosen the second. [187]
5.22 P So was Suria wrong? He was wrong about the outcome, and by then he had left for a hedge fund. But Commoncog's case study makes a fair point: he had correctly spotted a real weakness, and the company survived partly because of a bond sale that closed just in time. Stone called it a mix of conviction, improvisation and luck. __Never let a good outcome convince you there was no danger.__ [177,165]

   - May 1999 :: Barron's prints Amazon.bomb. [57]
   - Feb 2000 :: About $672 million raised in Europe, weeks before the crash. [165]

=== CH6 the-big-bets | 2002 to 2021 | Spending Profits Before They Existed
6.0 P Most companies that nearly die become careful. Amazon did the opposite. Within six years of its first profit, it launched a shipping club that would be expensive at first, a cloud business that outsiders called a distraction and a device that could wreck its own book sales. Here is what those decisions looked like from inside the room. [94,57,103]
6.1 H The boathouse
6.2 P It began with an annoyance. Engineer Charlie Ward loved 1-Click ordering, but it did not work with free shipping, and that irritated him more and more. He wondered aloud whether customers might pay a lump sum each year and never see a shipping charge again. His colleagues paused, wondering if he was crazy, so he wrote it up in half a page and used the employee suggestion box. [94,95]
6.3 P Late in 2004 Bezos took it up. One Friday in December, the team cancelled a meeting with him because the site had crashed for hours. He understood, then told them to come over on Saturday morning anyway. They were sent to the boathouse at his home, which one of them remembers as bigger than his own apartment. Bezos wanted a moat around Amazon's best customers. [94,95]
6.4 P He gave them four weeks. They said six was the minimum. So he moved the date of a quarterly earnings call by about two weeks to fit, which is not a thing CEOs usually do for a side project. The team printed T-shirts saying it would ship in six weeks or less, and left one behind whenever they poached an engineer from another team. [94]
6.5 EX For example: two-day delivery cost $9.48 an order back then, so Prime at $79 paid for itself in nine orders. Amazon weighed $49 and $99 before Bezos chose. An insider later said it was never really about the $79. It was about changing how people thought, so that they stopped shopping around. [94,97]
6.6 NOTE An engineer's half-page became a business with 100 million-plus US customers.
6.7 H The side project nobody understood
6.8 P AWS started as a cleanup job. Around 2000, Amazon tried to run online shops for other retailers and found its code was a mess, so it split its systems into clean building blocks. Projects still took too long because every team built its own storage and computing. Then, at a 2003 offsite at Bezos's home, a 30-minute exercise on what the company was good at ran long. [98]
6.9 P The answer surprised them. To survive on thin retail margins, they had become very good at cheap, reliable data centres. Jassy got the job of turning that into a business. By late 2004 a tiny team was in Cape Town, working thousands of miles from headquarters because its leader wanted to go home. It built the computing service almost independently of Seattle. [98,99,101]
6.10 P Observers called it a risky distraction. Bezos remembered them asking what storage had to do with selling books. It launched in 2006, charging by use, so a startup with a credit card could rent what used to require millions of dollars of its own servers. Rivals took years to respond. __Amazon's great advantage was that for years nobody thought a bookshop was serious competition in computing.__ [57,98,100]
6.11 P Then Amazon kept quiet about how well it was going. It stayed silent on the unit's numbers until 2015, when it finally reported them. Even then the quarterly operating profit of over $250 million surprised people, at about 17 percent margins, against 2 to 3 percent in retail. By then its lead was already large. [101]
6.12 FACTS
   - $128.7B :: AWS sales in 2025 [63]
   - $45.6B :: AWS operating profit in 2025, out of $80B company-wide [63]
   - $184B :: AWS annualised revenue run rate, Q3 2026 [102]
   - 39% :: AWS growth in Q3 2026, its fastest in nearly five years [102]
6.13 H Kill your own business
6.14 P In 2004 books were at the heart of Amazon, and Apple was showing with the iPod how fast a media business could move to a device. So Bezos reassigned Steve Kessel, the man running books, and told him his job was to destroy that business. Asked for a deadline, Bezos said he was already late, according to Brad Stone. [103,106]
6.15 P Bezos then did something most leaders would not dare. He priced most e-books at $9.99 at launch, a number one case study traces to Apple's 99-cent songs, not to any margin calculation. The team tested prototypes during a weekend away with him, reading for an hour and then discussing how the device felt in the hand. Kessel told him it would take 18 months. It took three and a half years. [103,107,105]
6.16 NOTE He attacked his own best business before anyone else could.
6.17 H Failing at the right size
6.18 P Fire Phone was the expensive embarrassment. In October 2014 Amazon took its $170 million charge, and hedge fund manager David Einhorn told investors that Amazon's story was breaking: growth was slowing, and losses were growing. It was the biggest quarterly loss in 14 years. The critics seemed to have a point. [130,51]
6.19 P Freed, a key leader on the phone, might have expected to take the blame. According to The New Yorker, Bezos told him he must not lose one minute of sleep over it. Earlier, during a demo of the phone's software, Bezos had asked it for Hotel California. When the song played, he was delighted, and soon asked Freed to build a cloud computer you could talk to. Four months after the phone, Echo arrived. [131,134]
6.20 P Dave Limp, who ran devices, later said that if you had listened to the skeptics you would have cancelled both products. In his 2018 letter Bezos wrote that Amazon's experiments should grow with the company, even to multibillion-dollar failures. He said Fire Phone's lessons and developers sped up Echo and Alexa. __The team that failed was kept, and that is why the next try was possible.__ [134,190]
6.21 NOTE Keep the team after a failure. They just paid for the lesson.
6.22 H The deals
6.23 P Amazon also bought what it could not build. The shoe seller Zappos came in 2009, the warehouse robot maker Kiva in 2012 for $775 million, and the game-streaming site Twitch in 2014 at a reported $970 million. PillPack in 2018 pushed it into pharmacy. MGM, the James Bond studio, was agreed in 2021 for $8.45 billion. By then Bezos had already announced he was stepping aside as CEO. [146,162,156]
6.24 P The biggest was Whole Foods. CEO John Mackey said publicly that it was love at first sight after a blind date in Seattle. The filings tell a tougher story. Amazon offered $41 a share and warned that it would walk away if word leaked or if Whole Foods shopped the deal. Whole Foods asked for $45, and Amazon said $42 was final. Whole Foods took it. [143,141,142,191]
6.25 EX For example: Ring began as Doorbot, a video doorbell pitched on the TV show Shark Tank in 2013. Founder Jamie Siminoff asked for $700,000, was turned down by every Shark but one, refused that offer and ended up in tears, almost out of money. Five years later Amazon bought the company at a reported $1 billion or more. [192,193,194,195]
6.26 NOTE Hundreds of investors rejected Ring. Rejection is data, not a verdict.
6.27 H Who he beat, and who beat him
6.28 P The honest scorecard is mixed. Against book chains he won clearly. He ended up running Borders' website from 2001, and Barnes & Noble's attack in court gave way to Amazon's own patent suit against it over 1-Click in 1999. He also built a cloud business years before rivals responded. [184,172,196,98]
6.29 P But he lost phones to Apple and Samsung, and China to Alibaba and JD.com. India remains unfinished, with Flipkart still ahead on gross sales at last count. In space, SpaceX got its big NASA lander deal first in 2021, Blue Origin winning a $3.4 billion lander contract only in 2023. Walmart bought both Jet.com and Flipkart, and became a stronger rival. [129,108,115,197,121]
6.30 P Amazon crossed a market value of ==$1 trillion== for the first time in September 2018. In February 2021, Bezos told staff he would hand the CEO job to Jassy that summer and focus on Day One, Earth Fund, Blue Origin and The Washington Post. It ended 27 years in the job, and began the next stage of his working life. [198,156,64]

=== CH8 how-they-work | How he works | Ten O'Clock and Six Pages
8.0 H The slow morning
8.1 P Most founders brag about waking at 4 a.m. Bezos told an audience in Washington in September 2018 almost the opposite. He goes to bed early, gets up early, and then potters. He reads the paper, drinks coffee and, in those years, ate breakfast with his children before school. He called this “puttering” and set his first meeting of the day for ==10 a.m.== [207,208,209]
8.2 P The order of the day comes from how he thinks a brain works. Anything mentally hard goes in the 10 o'clock slot, which he nicknamed “high-IQ meetings”. By 5 p.m., he says, he stops trying and pushes the hard problem to the next morning. __He plans his day around when his judgment is best, not around how busy he looks.__ [207,208,209]
8.3 P He also protects eight hours of sleep unless he is crossing time zones. His reason is pure arithmetic. A senior leader is paid to make a small number of good decisions, not thousands of average ones. In his view, if he can make three good decisions in a day, that is enough. [208,209,210]
8.4 EX For example: he once did the sums out loud. Sleep four hours instead of eight and you gain four working hours, so 12 productive hours become 16. That is 33 percent more time, or perhaps 133 decisions instead of 100. Then he asked whether those extra decisions are worth it if a tired, grumpy you makes all of them worse. [209]
8.5 NOTE Not a 4 a.m. hero. He sleeps eight hours. Read this twice.
8.6 H The day he banned slides
8.7 P By 2004 his top team, the S-team, was meeting for about four hours every week. Two or three groups would come in and present. Meetings overran, topics kept coming back, and decisions stayed stuck. Colin Bryar, then his technical adviser, says one painful meeting pushed Bezos to act. [211,212]
8.8 P Bryar and Bezos had read an essay on a business flight by Edward Tufte, a Yale professor who studies how information is displayed. Tufte argued that bullet points damage serious reasoning. On ==9 June 2004== the S-team received an email whose subject line ended PowerPoint at their meetings. From then on, teams had to write their ideas in full sentences. [213,212]
8.9 P It did not work at once. Bryar admits the first memos were not good. They began at four pages, and six turned out to suit a one-hour meeting. His argument for the switch is simple: people read faster than others can talk, and a page holds far more information than a slide. [211]
8.10 P A meeting now opens with about 20 minutes of total silence while everyone reads. Bezos has compared it to study hall. The last 40 minutes are for questions. The memo carries no author's name, because it comes from the whole team, and Bryar and Carr report that Bezos reads each sentence assuming it is wrong until he can prove otherwise. [211,214,215]
8.11 EX For example: Bezos says weak memos usually come from a wrong idea of how long the job takes. People think they can do it in a day or two. His recipe takes a week or more: write it, rewrite it, ask colleagues to improve it, leave it alone for a couple of days, then edit it again with a fresh mind. [214]
8.12 NOTE Can't write it clearly? You haven't finished thinking it through.
8.13 H Last to speak, first to dial
8.14 P In every meeting he attends, Bezos says, he speaks last. He has seen that when the most senior person talks first, even strong-minded people quietly start to doubt their own view. His ideal is the reverse: the most junior person goes first and the order climbs by seniority. __The boss's opinion is expensive, so he spends it at the end.__ [216]
8.15 P The most famous scene comes from Amazon's early years, during a weekly review. A chart said customers waited under 60 seconds on the 1-800 helpline, yet customers kept complaining about long waits. The manager defended the number. So Bezos picked up the phone in the room, dialled the helpline, and everyone sat and waited in silence. [216]
8.16 P The wait ran to many minutes, he recalls, probably more than ==10==. The data was measuring the wrong thing, and that call forced the company to fix it. His rule from this is worth learning. When numbers and customer stories disagree, the stories are usually right, so go and check what you are really measuring. [216]
8.17 P He kept that line to customers open for years through a public address, jeff@amazon.com. He would forward a complaint to the responsible team with one added character, a question mark. Receivers reportedly had only a few hours to find the cause and explain it up the chain. One such email, about marketing mails sent to people who had browsed sensitive health products, led him to end that kind of mailing. [217,218]
8.18 NOTE One question mark, and a whole department drops everything.
8.19 H Three questions before hiring anyone
8.20 P In his 1998 letter to shareholders, Amazon had 2,100 people and Bezos ranked hiring as its most important advantage. He asked interviewers three things. Will you admire this person? Will they raise the average of the group they join? In what way could they be a superstar? His own example of that last one was a colleague who had been a National Spelling Bee champion. [219]
8.21 P He once told a colleague he would rather interview 50 people and hire nobody than hire the wrong one. Much of the method came from D. E. Shaw, the investment firm where he had worked, including odd problems such as estimating how many fax machines exist in the US. The point was to watch someone reason, not to hear the right number. [220,221,222]
8.22 P Amazon later turned this into a system. Specially trained employees called “bar raisers” sit on hiring panels from outside the hiring team. They can veto a candidate, though the veto is rarely used, and panel members must write their reports. Business Insider reported in 2014 that some spent 20 to 30 hours a week on it, on top of their real jobs. [215,221]
8.23 FACTS
   - 10 a.m. :: first meeting of his day, saved for the hardest thinking [207]
   - 6 pages :: the memo limit for a one-hour meeting [212]
   - 20 mins :: of silent reading before anyone talks [211]
   - < 10 :: people in a two-pizza team [223]
8.24 H Small teams, one owner
8.25 P Amazon's best-known team rule is that no group should be too big for two pizzas to feed. That usually means under 10 people. Each team owns one product or service, so it spends less time coordinating with others and more with its customers. Bryar and Carr describe Bezos as obsessed with reducing the coordination any project needs to move forward. [223,215]
8.26 P His smartest delegation tool may be the “shadow”. From 2002 a young executive named Andy Jassy followed him for about 18 months, into board meetings, phone calls and one-on-ones. Ann Hiatt, his executive assistant at the time, says the idea was to create a “brain double” who could challenge his thinking. Jassy later said the job showed him his own standards had been too low. [224,225]
8.27 NOTE He trained his successor by letting him sit in every meeting.
8.28 P Jassy noticed teams were stuck rebuilding the same computing basics, and in 2003 Bezos put him in charge of what became Amazon Web Services. In 2021, he handed Jassy the whole company. __He did not only delegate tasks. He let someone watch how decisions were made until that person could make them too.__ [225,226]
8.29 H What it is like to work for him
8.30 P Here the story gets harder. Brad Stone interviewed many current and former staff for his 2013 book The Everything Store, though Bezos did not sit for an interview. Employees told him about sudden outbursts they called “nutters”. Stone's list includes Bezos asking a manager whether the person was lazy or simply incompetent, and telling a team its document looked like the work of its second-string players. [218,227,217]
8.31 P Even Bezos's famous laugh could sting. Rick Dalzell, Amazon's former chief information officer, said on the record that it could disarm and punish you at the same time. Stone also reported something uncomfortable for critics: staff said the harsh remarks were often accurate. People who did well tended to be those who enjoyed constant argument. [217,218,228]
8.32 P Yet the same boss could be gentle after a real failure. When the Fire Phone flopped, Ian Freed, who helped lead it, says Bezos told him not to feel bad even for a minute and made him promise not to lose sleep. Freed's team then helped build the Echo. Bezos seems to be hardest on sloppy thinking and softest on brave bets that miss. [131]
8.33 NOTE Tough standards are worth copying. The insults are not.

=== CH9 mindset | What he believes | It Is Always Day 1
9.0 H Belief 1: Fear your customers, not your rivals
9.1 P In his 1998 letter, Bezos told shareholders he reminds staff to wake up afraid every morning. Not of competitors, but of customers. They stay loyal, he wrote, only until someone offers them something better. Nineteen years later his view had not changed: what amazes people this year bores them next year, because human expectations only go up. [219,214]
9.2 P In November 2018, at an all-hands meeting, an employee asked what the Sears bankruptcy could teach Amazon. Bezos answered that Amazon was not too big to fail. In fact, he predicted that Amazon would one day fail and go bankrupt, because big companies tend to last 30-odd years. Turning inward, he said, is how the end begins. [229]
9.3 EX For example: when The Times of India interviewed him in Bangalore in 2014, the paper counted that he used the word “customer-centric” about 30 times. His one-line reason, as the paper reported it, was that customers pay Amazon and rivals do not. [230]
9.4 NOTE He told his own staff Amazon will go bankrupt one day. Imagine that meeting.
9.5 H Belief 2: Stay in Day 1
9.6 P “Day 1” began as a line in his first shareholder letter in 1997, and he has repeated it for decades. He worked in an Amazon building named Day 1, and when he moved buildings he took the name with him. At one all-hands meeting someone finally asked him what Day 2 looks like. His answer is a four-step slide: standing still, then irrelevance, then a slow and painful decline, then death. [231,232]
9.7 P He gave four defences: obsess over customers, distrust stand-in measures, grab big outside trends early, and decide fast. The stand-in point is the subtle one. Over time, a process or metric can quietly replace the thing it was meant to serve, as with that helpline chart. Even his 2021 goodbye email as CEO ended by telling staff that it remains Day 1. [231,216,226]
9.8 H Belief 3: Play a longer game than everyone else
9.9 P Steven Levy of Wired asked him in 2011 why he had given ==$42 million== towards a clock built to run for 10,000 years. Bezos connected it to business. If your work must pay off in three years, you are competing with a crowd. Stretch that to seven years and most of the crowd disappears, because few companies will wait. [233]
9.10 P Amazon, he told Levy, likes things to work within five to seven years. He stays unbending about the goal and loose about the route. He used e-books and cloud services as his examples: certain to happen, with only the timing unknown. __Patience is a real competitive edge, because it is the one thing rivals refuse to buy.__ [233]
9.11 NOTE At 22, seven years feels like forever. That is exactly why it works.
9.12 H Belief 4: Fail bigger as you grow
9.13 QUOTE "failure and invention are inseparable twins" -- Jeff Bezos, 2015 letter to shareholders [234]
9.14 P His maths for this is worth copying into a notebook. A 10 percent chance of a 100-times payoff is a bet you should always take, even though you will lose it nine times out of ten. In baseball the best swing scores four runs. In business, he wrote, one swing can occasionally score ==1,000==. [234]
9.15 P Amazon's seller marketplace started as a flop. Bezos has joked that Amazon Auctions drew about seven visitors, counting his parents and siblings. Its follow-up, zShops, also found no customers. The third try worked so well that independent sellers grew to 58 percent of the physical goods sold on Amazon by 2018. [235,236]
9.16 P The same logic explains his reaction to the Fire Phone. Asked about it by his own newspaper's editor in 2016, he said Amazon was working on far bigger failures and he was not joking. In the 2018 letter he wrote that a company Amazon's size should sometimes have multibillion-dollar failures. He also pointed out that the phone's lessons and engineers sped up the Echo. [237,236]
9.17 NOTE Seven visitors. Including his parents. Then it became most of Amazon's sales.
9.18 H Belief 5: Ask whether the door swings back
9.19 P Bezos splits decisions into two kinds. A one-way door is hard or impossible to undo, so go slowly and consult widely. He calls these “Type 1”. Most decisions are two-way doors, “Type 2”: if the room is bad, you walk back out. Those should be made fast, by one good person or a small group. [238]
9.20 P Big companies fall sick, he wrote, when they treat every choice like a one-way door. Young founders often make the opposite error, and he notes that firms which rush their one-way decisions rarely live long enough to get big. His speed rule is to decide with about ==70 percent== of the information you wish you had. Waiting for 90 usually means you are being slow. [238,231]
9.21 EX For example: Amazon Studios once wanted to make an original he doubted. He thought it might not be interesting enough, would be complicated to produce, and had weak business terms. The team disagreed. He wrote back at once that he disagreed, would commit anyway, and hoped it became their most-watched show. That is “disagree and commit”. [231]
9.22 H Belief 6: High standards can be taught
9.23 P His best teaching story is about a handstand. A close friend wanted a perfect one, so she took a workshop and eventually hired a coach. The coach told her most people assume two weeks and it really takes about six months of daily practice. If you expect two weeks, you quit. __Most people don't lack talent. They just guess wrongly about how long hard things take.__ [214]
9.24 P He applied this to himself too. He has written that he started Amazon with high standards on inventing, customer care and hiring, but not on running operations, such as keeping a fixed problem fixed. His colleagues had to teach him. So having high standards in one area, he argues, tells you nothing about your blind spot in the next. [214]
9.25 NOTE Very important for first-time founders! Good at code doesn't mean good at operations.
9.26 H Belief 7: Wander on purpose
9.27 P Efficiency works when you know where you are going. Bezos argues that the giant discoveries need something else, which he calls wandering: following a hunch before you can justify it. Nobody asked for AWS. In his telling, customers did not know they wanted it until it existed. [236]
9.28 EX For example: no one asked for the Echo either. Bezos wrote that if you had gone to a customer in 2013 and offered an always-on black cylinder, about the size of a Pringles can, to talk to in the kitchen, they would have looked at you strangely and said no. More than 100 million Alexa devices had been bought by the time he wrote that. [236]
9.29 H Belief 8: Be cheap about the right things, and create more than you take
9.30 P In the summer of 1995, Amazon needed desks and sat across from a Home Depot. Doors cost less than desks, so Bezos bought doors and screwed legs onto them. Nico Lovejoy, an early employee, has said he later had to fix the hardware on the ones Bezos built, and that no one should hire him as a carpenter. Amazon still gives a Door Desk Award for ideas that keep prices low. [239]
9.31 P In his last letter as CEO he gave a rule for business and life: create more than you consume. He then added up 2020 in his own way, with ==$21 billion== for shareholders, $91 billion for employees, $25 billion for sellers and $164 billion for customers. These are Amazon's estimates, and critics would argue with them. But the habit of asking what you gave everyone else is a good one. [232]
9.32 FACTS
   - 70% :: of the information you want, then decide [231]
   - 7 years :: his preferred horizon, instead of 3 [233]
   - 10% × 100 :: the kind of bet he says you always take [234]
   - $301B :: value Amazon estimated it created in 2020 [232]

=== CH10 personal-life | Family, crises, giving | The Man Behind the Laugh
10.0 H Looking for someone resourceful
10.1 P In his New York years Bezos treated dating like a Wall Street pipeline and joked about it to Wired in 1999. He told friends he wanted a partner resourceful enough to get him out of a prison in a poor country, because nobody knows what “resourceful” means until you put it that way. Then he met someone at work instead. [240,241]
10.2 P MacKenzie Tuttle, a Princeton graduate who had assisted the novelist Toni Morrison, joined D. E. Shaw as a research associate, and Bezos was her first interviewer. She later told Vogue that her office was next to his and she fell for his laugh all day long. They were engaged within three months and married within six, in 1993. A year later they drove west to start Amazon. [240,241]
10.3 P They had four children. In January 2019 they announced together on Twitter that after a long trial separation they were divorcing and would stay friends, and they said that even knowing how it would end they would do it all again. The statement is unusually warm for two people about to divide what was then the world's largest fortune. [242,13,243]
10.4 TIMELINE
   - 1993 :: Jeff Bezos and MacKenzie Tuttle marry. [241]
   - Jan 2019 :: They announce their divorce after 25 years. [242]
   - Feb 2019 :: He publishes “No thank you, Mr. Pecker”. [244]
   - Apr 2019 :: Settlement: she receives about 4% of Amazon; he keeps 75% of their shares and voting control over hers. [245,243]
   - Jun 2025 :: He marries Lauren Sánchez in Venice. [246]
   - Aug 2025 :: His mother, Jackie Bezos, dies. [247]
10.5 H The settlement
10.6 P Amazon's filing in April 2019 gave the bones. About ==4 percent== of the company would go into her name, a stake The New York Times put at almost $36 billion. He kept 75 percent of their Amazon shares and all of their stakes in The Washington Post and Blue Origin. He also kept sole voting power over her shares unless she sold or donated them. [245,243]
10.7 P So control of Amazon did not change hands in the split. That detail matters, because votes and money are two separate things. MacKenzie Scott has since signed the Giving Pledge, a public promise by billionaires to give away most of their wealth, which he has not done. [245,248]
10.8 NOTE Votes and money are separate things. Learn this before your first term sheet.
10.9 H The tabloid fight
10.10 P In January 2019, right after the divorce news, The National Enquirer published a story about his relationship with Lauren Sánchez, including private text messages. Bezos hired the security expert Gavin de Becker to find out how the paper got them. On 7 February 2019, he did something few people in his position would do: he published the tabloid company's emails in a Medium post titled “No thank you, Mr. Pecker”. [249,244,250]
10.11 P He alleged that American Media, the Enquirer's owner, threatened to publish intimate photos unless he publicly said its coverage was not politically motivated. He called it extortion and blackmail. The company said it had acted lawfully and would investigate. Federal prosecutors were reported to be reviewing whether this broke an earlier immunity deal, and we found no reliable report that any charge followed. [244,251,250]
10.12 P The source turned out to be close to home. The Wall Street Journal reported that Michael Sánchez, Lauren's brother, was paid $200,000 by the publisher, which he disputed in part. He later sued Bezos and his security consultant for defamation. A court threw the case out for lack of admissible evidence, ordered about ==$218,000== in legal fees plus costs, and a California appeal court upheld that in 2022. [252,253,254]
10.13 NOTE He chose embarrassment over being controlled. Few people would make that trade.
10.14 H The phone
10.15 P Then came a stranger chapter. In 2020 two independent UN experts said a forensic report, commissioned by Bezos's own team, assessed with medium to high confidence that his iPhone was infiltrated in May 2018 after a video arrived from a WhatsApp account used by Saudi Crown Prince Mohammed bin Salman. Saudi Arabia called the claim absurd and asked for its own investigation. [255,256,257]
10.16 P Bezos owned The Washington Post, and its columnist Jamal Khashoggi was murdered later that year. The experts said they suspected an effort to influence the paper's coverage. This remains an allegation, not a court finding, and that report did not show the hacked material reached the Enquirer. That is a reminder that owning a newspaper can have very personal costs. [255,249]
10.17 H Almost a silly death
10.18 P In 2003, long before all this, Bezos was scouting West Texas land for rocket tests by helicopter. A gust pushed the small aircraft towards trees, it hit the ground, rolled, and ended upside down in a creek. He was aboard with his lawyer, a local guide and the pilot, and all of them survived. [258]
10.19 P He played it down for years. In 2004 he told Fast Company he had a few seconds to think on the way down and mostly felt it would be a foolish way to die. His main lesson, he said with a laugh, was to avoid helicopters. Blue Origin's Texas land purchase went ahead anyway. [259,258]
10.20 NOTE He was 39, upside down in a creek, and laughed about it later.
10.21 H Parents
10.22 P His parents stayed close to his life and work. When he left Seattle for Miami in 2023 after nearly 30 years, he said one reason was to be nearer them, along with Blue Origin's launches in Florida. His mother Jackie, who co-founded the Bezos Family Foundation, died on ==14 August 2025==. [260,247]
10.23 H Giving away the money
10.24 P For years Bezos was criticised for giving little compared with his wealth. Bloomberg counted about $1.4 billion in lifetime giving by early 2021, a tiny share of his fortune. He has not signed the Giving Pledge. In 2022, he told CNN for the first time that he would give away most of his money in his lifetime, without saying how or when. [261,262,248]
10.25 P Two big vehicles now carry the effort. The Bezos Earth Fund has a ==$10 billion== pledge for climate and nature by 2030, with about $2.4 billion granted so far, by Forbes's count. The Day 1 Fund began in 2018 with a $2 billion promise, split between homelessness and nonprofit preschools. Its families arm has given more than $850 million through 280 awards, under the motto “no child sleeps outside”. [13,263,29]
10.26 NOTE A promise is easy. Always check how much has actually been paid.
10.27 P He also hands out giant prizes that winners pass on to charities. The Courage and Civility awards gave $100 million each to chef José Andrés and to CNN's Van Jones in 2021, and $100 million to Dolly Parton in 2022. Inside Philanthropy still rates his giving as less comprehensive than that of some peers, including his former wife. [264,248,157]
10.28 P His test for a gift, as he explained on CNBC in May 2026, is whether it builds independence or dependence. He prefers family shelters because stays tend to be short and parents learn skills. He also argued that if he does his job well, his for-profit companies will do far more good than his charity. Not everyone agrees, but that is plainly what he believes. [265]
10.29 EX For example: in 2025 the Day 1 Families Fund gave $102.5 million to 32 groups in 20 states, Washington DC and Guam, with awards from $1.25 million to $5 million. One of them is Mary's Place, the Seattle shelter whose vision statement the fund adopted. [29]
10.30 H How he spends and plays
10.31 P The boy who loved Star Trek got his wish in 2016, playing an alien in Star Trek Beyond. He arrived with so much security that star Chris Pine, who had no idea who he was, remembers the bodyguards and limos. He has joked that his ideal job would be bartender, since he prides himself on craft cocktails, and that washing the dishes each night is the sexiest thing he does. [266,267,249]
10.32 P The bigger toys draw more anger. His sailing yacht Koru reportedly cost about $500 million. In 2022 Rotterdam residents threatened to pelt it with rotten eggs over a plan to take apart a historic bridge so it could pass. The builder dropped the permit request. Reuters estimated that his three-day Venice wedding in 2025 cost about $50 million. [268,269,260,246]
10.33 NOTE Same man: doors for desks in 1995, a $500 million yacht by 2022.

=== CH11 the-brand | Image and influence | Khakis to Cowboy Hat
11.0 H The nerd with the laugh
11.1 P In December 1999 TIME made Bezos its Person of the Year. At 35 he was the fourth-youngest ever, behind Charles Lindbergh, Queen Elizabeth II and Martin Luther King Jr. TIME's profile even dwelt on that high, giggling laugh. __For almost two decades that was the brand: a slightly goofy, obsessive genius who loved customers.__ [270,267]
11.2 P For most of that time he kept away from the spotlight on purpose. The Independent, carrying New York Times reporting, says he wanted Amazon out of politics, kept lobbying small and was reluctant even to take photos with politicians. Staying out of the glare, the report says, also had real business benefits. [267]
11.3 H He picks his moments
11.4 P When Bezos did speak, it was usually theatre. On 1 December 2013, the holiday shopping season was opening when 60 Minutes aired his surprise for Charlie Rose. He led the reporter into a room with small flying machines he called octocopters and promised half-hour delivery of packages under five pounds, which he said covered 86 percent of Amazon's items. [271,272]
11.5 P Even the CBS team had not been told beforehand. Bezos guessed it might take four or five years and warned he was an optimist. Whatever you think of the timeline, he had turned a warehouse story into a science-fiction one on a Sunday night. [272,271]
11.6 NOTE Notice the timing. He launches stories like products.
11.7 H The new body, the new life
11.8 P In July 2017, at a business conference in Sun Valley, Idaho, a photo showed a suddenly muscular Bezos in a tight shirt. The internet named him “Swole Jeff Bezos”, and comparison pictures of the 1990s bookseller spread fast. He had already turned more public on Twitter in late 2015, posting his lucky cowboy boots and a video from the top of a wind turbine. [273,267]
11.9 P In 2019 the image changed again, and not by choice. After the Enquirer story, he became a regular in celebrity news, with yachts and parties photographed for the world. Once tabloids reported the purple octopus swimming trunks he wore, the $260 pair quickly sold out. People who had worked with him for years told The New York Times it felt unreal. [249]
11.10 H A short social-media record
11.11 P Bezos posts rarely but knows how to pick a fight. In December 2015, only his fourth tweet ever, he replied to Donald Trump's attacks on Amazon and the Post by offering him a seat on a Blue Origin rocket, with the hashtag #sendDonaldtospace. In May 2022, he mocked a Biden post linking corporate taxes to inflation, saying that combining the two was a trick to mislead people. [274,275]
11.12 NOTE Only his fourth tweet ever, and already a fight with Trump.
11.13 P That Trump joke later looks very different. Amazon claimed in court that Trump's bias cost it a Pentagon cloud contract worth up to ==$10 billion== that went to Microsoft in 2019. By May 2026 Bezos was describing Trump on CNBC as more mature and disciplined than in his first term, and said business leaders must work with every president. [276,265]
11.14 H The cowboy hat problem
11.15 P On 20 July 2021 he flew to the edge of space in his own rocket, wearing a large cowboy hat on landing. At the press conference, he thanked every Amazon employee and customer for having paid for it all. He seemed sincere, and the room chuckled. Much of the internet did not. [264,277,278]
11.16 P Critics tied the line to warehouse conditions and to reports about his taxes. A branding consultant told AP it was rocket fuel for anyone upset about inequality. That same day he announced his $200 million in civility awards, but the thank-you is what stuck. __One careless sentence can undo a year of careful image-building.__ [277,264]
11.17 P Later public moments followed the pattern. Venice protesters hung “No Space for Bezos” banners in 2025 over the wedding, tourism and inequality. New York saw protests in May 2026 over his sponsorship of the Met Gala, reported at $10 million, where he and his wife were honorary chairs. When Andrew Ross Sorkin raised those headlines, Bezos spoke about his parents' climb, and he called the vilification a distraction. [279,280,281,265]
11.18 NOTE Thank your team, yes. Never remind them who paid for your rocket.
11.19 H India: the truck and the protest
11.20 P India has seen both Bezos brands. In September 2014, on a Sunday in Bangalore, he climbed on a brightly painted truck in a bandhgala jacket and handed a giant ==$2 billion== cheque to Amazon's India head, Amit Agarwal. Forbes noted that local rival Flipkart had just raised $1 billion. Bangalore was Flipkart's home city, which made the stunt pointed. [230,282]
11.21 P His next big trip, in January 2020, went differently. He paid his respects at the Gandhi memorial, flew a kite, wore traditional dress and told a Delhi crowd that this century would belong to India. He pledged $1 billion to bring small businesses online. In Mumbai, he posed with Shah Rukh Khan and Zoya Akhtar around a Prime Video event. [283,284,285]
11.22 NOTE A truck and a cheque in 2014. Protests in 300 cities by 2020.
11.23 P Outside, traders from a body claiming 70 million members held protests across 300 cities with “Jeff Bezos Go Back” signs. India's competition regulator had just opened a probe. Commerce Minister Piyush Goyal said publicly that Amazon was doing India no favour if the money simply funded its losses, then said a day later that he had been misread and that lawful investment was welcome. [283,286,287]
11.24 EX For example: Reuters reported at the time that he was unlikely to get the meeting he wanted with Prime Minister Narendra Modi. The richest man in the world could fill a Mumbai carpet with film stars and still not get a government appointment. In India, local shopkeepers are a political force, and any founder entering the market should study that. [285,286]
11.25 H How people see him
11.26 P Amazon itself often scores very well with the public, as Bezos noted when it ranked first in a Harris Poll on reputation in 2018. He as a person draws a sharper reaction, especially from those worried about inequality. Sorkin told him in 2026 that coverage of the very rich had become unusually harsh. Representative Alexandria Ocasio-Cortez has argued that a billion dollars cannot be earned. [214,265]
11.27 P His reply was a burger shop: one store, then two, then a thousand because people love the food, and at no point did that money become unethical. __You may disagree, but notice his method: he explains big ideas with tiny stories.__ It works in shareholder letters, on stage and on television. [265]
11.28 NOTE Handstands, pizzas, burger shops. He teaches with small objects. Steal this.

=== CH12 what-went-wrong | Mistakes and controversies | Where He Got It Wrong
12.0 H The phone he built for himself
12.1 P The Fire Phone launched in 2014 at $199 with a two-year contract. Its big trick was a 3D-like display driven by four cameras. Within months the price fell to 99 cents on contract, and Amazon took a ==$170 million== charge, with another $83 million in unsold phones. Sales stopped in 2015. [237,288,235]
12.2 P Fast Company interviewed more than three dozen people involved and reported that Bezos drove every part of it. One designer said the team ended up building it for Jeff rather than for customers. Teams tried to predict his reactions rather than the market's. It is the sharpest irony in his career: __the man who preached starting from the customer became the customer.__ [289,290]
12.3 NOTE Your own excitement is not market research. Very important for founders!
12.4 H Two small apologies that teach a lot
12.5 P In September 2000 customers comparing notes online found Amazon charging different prices for the same DVDs. It was a test on 68 titles over five days, with discounts from 20 to 40 percent. Amazon insisted it was random, not based on personal data, and Bezos said on CBS the company had not planned to tell anyone. It refunded 6,896 customers an average of ==$3.10==. [291,292]
12.6 P In July 2009 Amazon reached into customers' Kindles and deleted George Orwell's 1984 and Animal Farm after a seller without rights had listed them. Yes, 1984. Bezos posted a public apology calling the fix foolish, careless and against the company's own principles, and admitting the mess was entirely self-made. [293,294,295]
12.7 EX For example: Justin Gawronski, a 17-year-old Michigan student, lost the use of his homework notes when his copy vanished. He sued. Amazon agreed to pay $150,000 and promised not to delete purchased books in the US except in limited cases, such as with the user's consent or a court order. A teenager's homework changed company policy. [293,295]
12.8 H The bruising workplace
12.9 P In August 2015 The New York Times described a white-collar culture of tearing apart colleagues' ideas, emails past midnight and an internal tool for sending secret feedback to someone's boss. Bezos replied by memo that this was not the Amazon he knew, told staff to report such cases to HR or to him directly, and said tolerance for such treatment should be zero. [296,297,298]
12.10 P Then it got messy. Two months later, Amazon's Jay Carney attacked the piece, saying a key quoted former employee had left after an investigation into falsified records. The man denied this, and the Times's editor stood by the story, saying its reporters interviewed more than 100 people. Hold both sides up against what Bezos told Princeton graduates in 2010: that kindness is a choice, and a harder one than being clever. [299,300,301,33]
12.11 H The warehouses
12.12 P The hardest criticism comes from Amazon's hourly workforce. In 2020, during the first Covid wave, it fired Chris Smalls, a Staten Island worker who had protested over safety, saying he broke a quarantine rule. Leaked notes showed its general counsel, David Zapolsky, calling Smalls not smart or articulate. Zapolsky later apologised. In April 2022 Smalls' Amazon Labor Union won the company's first US union vote, ==2,654 to 2,131==. [302,303,304]
12.13 P Vice president Tim Bray quit in May 2020 in protest, giving up about a million dollars and writing that the firings were meant to create fear. A year later, Amazon's news account mocked a congressman's claim that workers urinate in bottles. Days later it apologised, admitting drivers can struggle to find toilets. [302,305,306,307]
12.14 NOTE One cocky tweet from a company account ended in a public apology.
12.15 P A Senate committee's majority staff reported in December 2024 that Amazon's internal studies linked speed to injuries, and that it rejected recommendations that might slow work. Amazon called the report wrong and said its injury rates had fallen. That same month it settled with US workplace regulators, agreeing to company-wide ergonomic steps and a $145,000 penalty, while noting that nine of ten complaints were withdrawn. [308,309,310,311,312]
12.16 P To be fair, Bezos did move, and sometimes admitted it. In 2018 Amazon set a $15 minimum wage, and he said it had listened to its critics. In his final CEO letter he wrote that Amazon needed to do a better job for employees and promised to make it “Earth's Best Employer”. __It took years of outside pressure to get there, and a younger founder can save those years.__ [313,232]
12.17 NOTE He changed after the criticism, not before. You can do better.
12.18 H Competing hard, or too hard?
12.19 P Brad Stone reported a line Bezos used about small book publishers: Amazon should go after them as a cheetah chases a sick gazelle. A lieutenant insisted it was a joke, but it became the name of a real negotiating programme until lawyers made staff rename it. Asked about it under oath in 2020, Bezos said he could not comment because he did not remember it. [314,315]
12.20 P At the same hearing, lawmakers raised Diapers.com. Quidsi, its parent, turned down Amazon in 2009. Amazon then cut baby-product prices sharply, and internal estimates at Quidsi put Amazon's losses at about $100 million in three months. Quidsi sold to Amazon in 2010. Regulators cleared the deal at the time, but it became a textbook example for critics. [316,315]
12.21 P The Wall Street Journal also reported that staff had used individual sellers' data to design Amazon's own products, despite a company ban and earlier assurances to Congress. Representative Pramila Jayapal pressed him on it. His answer, given under oath, became the headline. [317,318]
12.22 QUOTE "But I can't guarantee you that that policy has never been violated." -- Jeff Bezos, testifying to the US House antitrust subcommittee, 29 July 2020 [318,317,319]
12.23 H The bills that came due
12.24 P In September 2025 Amazon agreed to pay ==$2.5 billion== to settle FTC allegations that it enrolled people in Prime without clear consent and made cancelling hard: $1 billion in penalty and $1.5 billion in refunds for an estimated 35 million customers. Two executives were named in the case, and Bezos was not. A separate FTC-led antitrust case had a trial set for 9 February 2027, and it is unresolved. [320,321]
12.25 NOTE Hard to cancel means expensive later. A .5 billion lesson in design.
12.26 P India brought its own trouble. Reuters reported in 2021, from internal documents, that Amazon helped a few big sellers grow and copied products while steering search results in India, which Amazon has denied. India's regulator suspended a 2019 Future Group deal and imposed a penalty of about ₹200 crore, later upheld on appeal. Investigators' reports seen by Reuters in 2024 said it broke competition law, and Amazon did not respond to Reuters about them. [322,323,324,325,326]
12.27 P Not every fight ended badly. Amazon won its European tax case in 2023 and avoided about €250 million in back taxes. But it walked away from a second headquarters in New York in February 2019 after a backlash over nearly $3 billion in incentives. The city lost a promised 25,000 jobs, and the fight showed that public money attracts public anger. [327,328,329]
12.28 H His taxes
12.29 P ProPublica reported in 2021, from leaked IRS data, that Bezos paid no federal income tax in 2007 and 2011. In 2011 he even claimed a ==$4,000== child tax credit. From 2014 to 2018 his federal income taxes equalled 0.98 percent of his wealth gain. None of this was shown to be illegal, and his representatives did not answer ProPublica's questions. [330,331]
12.30 P In 2023 he moved to Florida, naming family and rockets as reasons. CNBC noted that Washington State had just begun a 7 percent capital-gains tax and calculated he would save at least $610 million on one planned stock sale. On CNBC in May 2026 he said he pays billions in taxes, including when he sells stock to fund Blue Origin, and that it is fine to debate how much. [260,265]
12.31 NOTE Legal is not the same as popular. Plan for both.
12.32 H Bets that have not paid
12.33 P Alexa is his great unfinished story. Business Insider reported that Amazon was on pace to lose about $10 billion in 2022 on Alexa and devices, and The Wall Street Journal saw documents showing losses of $5 billion a year in the devices unit in some years. It sits in millions of homes without a matching profit. By his own rule that may be a fair bet, but it is an expensive one. [332,333,334]
12.34 P Blue Origin spent 25 years reaching orbit, doing so only in early 2025. In 2021 it lost its lawsuit against NASA over a $2.9 billion lunar lander contract given to SpaceX. That September, 21 current and former staff, led by former communications head Alexandra Abrams, published an essay alleging sexism and ignored safety worries. Blue Origin rejected the charges, said she had been dismissed for cause, and the FAA said it would review the safety concerns. [89,335,336,337,338]
12.35 FACTS
   - $170M :: Fire Phone charge [237]
   - $2.5B :: FTC Prime settlement [320]
   - ₹200 crore :: Indian penalty over the Future deal [325]
   - 375,000+ :: Washington Post cancellations, by NPR's count [339]
12.36 H The newspaper
12.37 P He bought The Washington Post in 2013 for $250 million, and for years the story was a happy one. He says it was profitable for six years, and the newsroom grew. Then, eleven days before the 2024 election, the paper said it would stop presidential endorsements. Bezos wrote that endorsements create a perception of bias. He admitted the late timing was poor planning and denied any deal with anyone. [265,340,341,342,343]
12.38 P Readers did not accept it. More than 250,000 subscribers cancelled within days, and NPR counts more than 375,000 cancellations in all, about 15 percent of digital subscribers, including those who left after he refocused the opinion pages on personal liberties and free markets. In February 2026 the Post laid off about a third of its staff, closing its sports section and cutting foreign bureaus. [344,339,345]
12.39 P Journalists had appealed to him directly and he did not reply, NPR reported. His defence on CNBC was that the paper must earn its living, and that he told managers to follow the data, except on investigative reporting. He also denied any role in Amazon's $40 million deal for a documentary on Melania Trump. Critics see a billionaire protecting his federal contracts, a charge he rejects. [339,265,341]
12.40 NOTE Decades to build trust. One late decision cost hundreds of thousands of readers.
12.41 H What a young founder should not copy
12.42 P So here is our short list, and each item has a price tag attached in the pages above. Do not insult people and call it high standards. Do not fall in love with your own product. Do not treat your lowest-paid workers as a cost to manage and only fix things once regulators and reporters arrive. Do not assume that being right about customers makes you right about power. [218,289,232,342]

=== CH13 today | As of 2 November 2026 | Sixty-Two and Back to Work
13.0 P In February 2021 Bezos told staff he would step back as Amazon's CEO, insisted this was not retirement and said he had never had more energy. Five years later that looks like an honest statement. At 62 he is running a startup again, and he told CNBC that it is a grind he enjoys. [226,346]
13.1 FACTS
   - $361.3B :: Forbes real-time net worth, 1 Nov 2026; #2 in the world [13]
   - 8% :: his Amazon stake, the largest single part of his fortune [13,160]
   - $41B :: Prometheus valuation after its June 2026 round [64]
   - $30B :: reported lifetime personal investment in Blue Origin [89]
13.2 H His roles
13.3 P He remains executive chair of Amazon and its largest shareholder. He is co-CEO of Prometheus, owner of The Washington Post and the founder and controlling owner of Blue Origin, which Dave Limp runs day to day. He is also executive chair of the Bezos Earth Fund. Asked how he divides his time, he said the common thread is AI. [64,265,13,347,263]
13.4 H The new bet: Prometheus
13.5 P Prometheus launched in November 2025 with $6.2 billion, and it was his first CEO job since Amazon. His co-CEO is Vik Bajaj, who previously co-founded Alphabet's life-sciences lab, Verily. In June 2026 it raised another ==$12 billion== at about $41 billion. It has around 150 people across San Francisco, London and Zurich. Bezos said he became so impressed as a founding investor from late 2024 that he had to jump in fully. [64,348]
13.6 P He corrected people who assumed it was a robotics company. The aim, he says, is an “artificial general engineer”: tools that speed up designing and making physical things, from bridges to chips. Think of it as a very modern version of computer-aided design, though he says even that oversimplifies it. No product timeline has been given, and he has said it is too early to discuss an IPO. [265,348]
13.7 P Reuters, citing The Wall Street Journal, reported in March 2026 that he was in early talks to raise $100 billion for a fund to buy manufacturing firms and use AI to speed them up. He later said only that Prometheus might buy parts of companies that could benefit from its tools. He calls the whole experience “Type 2 fun”: hard while climbing, enjoyable once you are looking back. [349,348,346]
13.8 NOTE Worth over $300 billion, and still choosing the grind. Think about that.
13.9 H The old dream: Blue Origin
13.10 P Blue Origin's 2026 has been dramatic. In January it paused its New Shepard tourist flights for at least two years to focus on the Moon, after 38 flights and 98 people. It announced TeraWave, with more than 5,400 planned satellites, and Project Sunrise, with about 56,000, for computing in space. In April a New Glenn booster landed, yet the satellite on board went into the wrong orbit and was lost. [350,89]
13.11 P On ==28 May 2026== a New Glenn rocket exploded during a test on its Florida launch pad and badly damaged the site. Bezos called it a very bad day for the team and promised flights before year-end. By late October, SpaceNews reported the rebuilt pad should be ready by the first half of December, while Space.com noted a Reuters report suggesting early 2027. [64,92,351]
13.12 P Then the money arrived. After funding the company himself for 26 years, he allowed outside investors in for the first time. The Wall Street Journal reported about $10 billion raised, including $2 billion from Bezos. Limp said the round was oversubscribed but did not confirm numbers. That jump in value helped push Bezos back to number two on the Forbes list in the autumn. [89,347]

13.14 H What he says he wants next
13.15 P Space first, as ever. He told CNBC, from Blue Origin's factory floor in Florida, that heavy industry should move off Earth, which he calls the garden planet, and that the Moon is a gift because of its resources. He held up a solar cell made from simulated Moon dust. In Turin in 2025, he predicted millions of people would live in space within a couple of decades, by choice. [265,352]
13.16 P On AI he is an unapologetic optimist. He called the investment boom an industrial bubble in 2025, the kind that can leave useful things behind when it pops. He says people fearing job losses are wrong and predicts a labour shortage, comparing AI to swapping a shovel for a bulldozer. Those are predictions from someone with billions riding on the answer, so read them that way. [353,352,265]
13.17 P And he has new political opinions. In May 2026 he proposed ending income tax for the bottom half of US earners, using the example of a Queens nurse earning $75,000. He rejected the idea that billionaires are the root of the problem, and said he would keep giving away billions, with most of his wealth going before he dies. He admitted he does not yet know how to do that at such scale. [265,354]
13.18 P So where does he stand? He is the world's second-richest person, facing a harsher public mood than a decade ago, by his own account, and working on three companies at once. Elon Musk, his old rival, even applauded his Blue Origin spending in October. __Whatever you think of him, he is still doing the thing he told shareholders in 1997: playing for the long term.__ [13,160,265,355,233]

=== LESSONS
0 Guard your best hours.: Bezos set his first meeting for 10 a.m. and gave the hardest problems to that slot. By 5 p.m. he stopped trying to solve them. Find your own clear-headed hours and put your biggest decision there.
1 Sleep before you decide.: He worked out that cutting sleep would give him about 33 percent more decisions, then said the extra ones were not worth it if they were worse. Three good decisions a day were enough for him. You are paid for quality, not for looking exhausted.
2 Write it before you say it.: In June 2004 he banned PowerPoint from his top team's meetings and made people write full sentences. The first memos were bad, and teams learned that a good one takes a week. If you cannot write your plan clearly, you do not yet have a plan.
3 Speak last.: Bezos says he talks last in every meeting because even strong people bend once the boss has spoken. Ask your youngest teammate first. You will hear things nobody would otherwise tell you.
4 Call your own helpline.: A chart said customers waited under a minute, so he dialled the number in the middle of the meeting and everyone sat through a wait of many minutes. Do this with your own product this week. When numbers and customers disagree, go and check the numbers.
5 Hire slowly.: In 1998 he asked three things about every hire: will you admire them, will they raise the average, and where could they be a superstar? He said he would rather interview 50 people and hire nobody than hire wrongly. Your first ten people become your culture.
6 Keep teams small and give one person the job.: Two-pizza teams stay under about 10 people and own one thing. Andy Jassy shadowed Bezos for 18 months and later ran the whole company. Teach people how you think, then get out of their way.
7 Think in seven-year blocks.: He told Wired that a three-year goal puts you in a crowd and a seven-year goal removes most of it. He even funded a clock meant to run for 10,000 years to keep that idea alive. At your age, patience is the cheapest advantage you own.
8 Ask if the door swings back.: Bezos sorts choices into one-way and two-way doors. Reversible ones should be made fast with about 70 percent of the information you want. Save your slow thinking for the few you cannot undo.
9 Bet small enough to bet again.: Amazon Auctions drew about seven visitors, zShops did no better, and the third attempt became Amazon's biggest engine. He could keep trying because no single flop killed the company. Failing is fine as long as you are still standing for the next swing.
10 Do not build for yourself.: The Fire Phone had features Bezos adored and customers did not need, and one designer said they built it for Jeff. Amazon took a $170 million charge. Whenever you love your own idea most, go and find someone who has to pay for it.
11 Say sorry fast, and plainly.: When Amazon deleted 1984 from Kindles, Bezos publicly called his company's fix foolish and self-inflicted, and Amazon changed its rules. When it denied the bottle stories, it had to retract them days later. A quick, plain apology costs far less than a slow defence.
12 Do not confuse rudeness with standards.: Staff described “nutters” and cutting lines from Bezos, yet his grandfather taught him that kindness takes more effort than cleverness. Take the high bar from him and leave the insults. People should fear shipping bad work, not fear you.
13 Take care of the people doing the hardest work.: Amazon spent years fighting critics over warehouses before it raised pay, and Bezos only admitted in his final CEO letter that it needed a better vision for workers. A union, Senate report and federal settlement followed anyway. Fix it early, before someone else makes you.
14 Watch what your money signals.: His thank-you after his 2021 spaceflight, a $500 million yacht and a Venice wedding all turned into stories about inequality. The late endorsement decision at his newspaper cost hundreds of thousands of readers. As you grow, your choices start speaking for you, so check what they are saying.
