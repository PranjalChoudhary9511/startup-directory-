Close Speak to a specialist Latest market news Reliance, Aramco deal delayed further Market : Crude oil, Oil products, Petrochemicals 15/07/20 State-controlled Saudi Aramco's planned purchase of a 20pc stake in Indian private-sector conglomerate Reliance Industries' (RIL) refining and petrochemical assets is facing further delays. "Due to unforeseen circumstances in the energy market and the Covid-19 situation, the deal has not progressed according to the original timeline," RIL chairman Mukesh Ambani said today, without giving more details. "Our equity requirements have already been met." RIL had planned to offload a stake in its oil-to-chemical (O2C) business to reduce its overall debt, which was incurred to expand its oil business, and after investing in retail and telecom ventures. It is not clear if the deal will still go ahead. "RIL values our over two-decade relationship with Saudi Aramco and is committed to a long-term partnership," Ambani said. RIL's capital needs were met by 14 investors led by Facebook and Google, which together invested 1.52 trillion rupees ($20bn). RIL will approach the government for permission to spin off its O2C business into a separate subsidiary to help facilitate any partnership opportunities. Other investors besides Aramco are interested in the O2C business, a company official said, without giving details. RIL expects to complete the spin-off by early 2021. The O2C business will also own the fuel retail joint venture established between RIL and BP this month. BP paid $1bn to RIL for a 49pc stake in the joint venture, with RIL holding 51pc. The venture, RBML, will expand its fuel retail network to 5,500 over the next five years from more than 1,400 retail sites now and aims to increase its presence from 30 to 45 airports. RIL said in August last year that the deal with Aramco, which values RIL's oil and chemical divisions at $75bn including debt, would be concluded by 31 March 2020. It later said the deal would be delayed beyond March. The Aramco investment covers all of RIL's refineries and petrochemical assets, including its 51pc stake in the retail joint venture with BP. Aramco will supply 500,000 b/d of crude to RIL's 1.36mn b/d Jamnagar refining complex as part of the arrangement. Ownership in India's downstream sector is poised for further change, with the government planning to sell a 54pc controlling stake in state-controlled refiner Bharat Petroleum (BPCL). Saudi Arabia's participation has been sought in the proposed sale. It is unclear if RIL will bid for BPCL. There is also uncertainty over the planned 1.2mn b/d refinery and petrochemicals project in Maharashtra state, which is expected to cost over $55bn. Aramco and Abu Dhabi's state-owned Adnoc have a 50pc share in the project, the agreement for which expires this year. RIL's gross refining margins rose to $8.90/bl in the January-March quarter from $8.20/bl a year earlier but fell from $9.20/bl in the preceding quarter. The company's 1.36mn b/d Jamnagar refinery processed 1.47mn b/d in January-March from 1.29mn b/d a year earlier. News 02/11/26 Lula may lean more on Petrobras in fourth term Lula may lean more on Petrobras in fourth term Sao Paulo, 2 November (Argus) — Incumbent Luiz Inacio Lula da Silva&#x27;s surprising win in Brazil&#x27;s presidential election is likely to mean a steady course for the country&#x27;s hydrocarbons agenda and the strategy of state-controlled Petrobras. But with a recently elected right-wing majority in congress likely to push back against most of his policies, the president may rely more heavily on Petrobras and its profits to support his wider agenda. Lula beat senator Flavio Bolsonaro in the presidential election run-off after trailing the challenger in the first round. His fourth term in office — he also served two in 2003-11 — is expected to be one of continuity for the oil and natural gas sector. The government is likely to focus on increasing crude production for export and pursuing lower domestic gas prices by increasing competition and production. Brazil&#x27;s crude output has been rising steadily through Lula&#x27;s third term, reaching consecutive records. August production reached almost 4.6mn b/d, up from 3.8mn b/d in the same month in 2025, hydrocarbons regulator ANP says. The country expects to increase oil production to 4.9mn b/d by 2030, according to energy research firm EPE&#x27;s latest projections. It is still driving output from its prolific pre-salt fields, but it will also keep looking at other exploration frontiers such as the southern Pelotas basin and the equatorial margin. Lula has been a strong advocate of increasing production in the pre-salt and the new frontiers. Production was also likely to keep growing under Bolsonaro or any other candidate. Crude surpassed soy as Brazil&#x27;s largest export product for the first time in 2024. But Bolsonaro&#x27;s and Lula&#x27;s plan for the energy sector diverged mostly in their approach to state-controlled Petrobras. During his election campaign, Lula spoke of Petrobras re-entering the fuel distribution market, which it exited after it sold its stake in BR Distribuidora in 2021, during the presidency of Flavio&#x27;s father Jair Bolsonaro. Lula&#x27;s last administration also halted Petrobras&#x27; divestment programme, but stopped short of repurchasing sold-off refineries or returning to fuel distribution. The Lula government has framed state involvement in fuel distribution as a price-control mechanism in the face of rising consumer prices. But Petrobras&#x27; business plan is focused on upstream investment, and fiscal pressures may leave the government little room to manoeuvre on downstream pricing. "I don&#x27;t see much scope for very heterodox policies in the oil and gas sector," said Edmar Almeida, a professor at the PUC-Rio university&#x27;s Institute of Energy. Vote of confidence Foreign oil companies gathered at a conference in Rio de Janeiro before the elections pointed to Brazil as an increasingly attractive investment in deepwater pre-salt fields and new offshore exploration frontiers. Lula&#x27;s shock re-election does not change this, particularly given ongoing risks and challenges to business in Russia, the Middle East and Venezuela, Almeida said. Petrobras is also likely to continue expanding its biofuels business under chief executive Magda Chambriard, including a planned return to ethanol after exiting the sector in 2017. While Chambriard has stressed the importance of fossil fuels, her management has embraced Lula&#x27;s view that oil and gas revenues can finance Petrobras&#x27; energy transition and investment in lower-carbon businesses. Keeping Chambriard at the helm would maintain a degree of management stability for Petrobras that it lacked in 2018-24, which is likely to be welcomed by investors. A clearer indication of whether the firm&#x27;s priorities will change in Lula&#x27;s new term is likely to come with Petrobras&#x27; next five-year business plan, due to be unveiled in November. Find out more News Oil services firms see momentum building into 2027 02/11/26 News 02/11/26 Oil services firms see momentum building into 2027 New York, 2 November (Argus) — Growth prospects for the top oil field services providers are improving heading into 2027, driven by international expansion in offshore developments, renewed exploration and efforts to boost production from existing assets. Deepwater projects remain a bright spot with activity broadening across Latin America, Africa, the eastern Mediterranean and Asia, and final investment decisions expected to increase by more than 30pc in 2026, according to the world&#x27;s biggest oil field services contractor SLB. A growing number of US shale producers that are gearing up for international expansion will also prove a catalyst for growth. Venezuela is now open for business and companies are racing to partner with oil and gas producers to help revive the nation&#x27;s energy industry following the US capture of former president Nicolas Maduro earlier this year. While the pace of the recovery in the Middle East remains uncertain given the ongoing conflict, the region is also shaping up to be a significant driver of growth once activity returns to normal. SLB forecasts that global oil field services market activity will expand in the mid-single digit range next year. That outlook assumes that Middle East activity holds steady in the second half of 2026 and that there is no escalation in the US-Iran conflict. But any resolution to the war could help unlock deferred demand and serve as a "powerful catalyst" for further growth in 2027, SLB says. "We see a very compelling outlook for 2027, with further upside as Middle East activity recovers," chief executive Olivier Le Peuch says. Halliburton also remains upbeat on oil services demand following the supply shock caused by the war in the Middle East. "We are another three months into one of the largest energy disruptions in history," chief executive Jeff Miller says. "The work to restore production, rebuild inventories and strengthen energy security will drive multi-year demand growth for oil field services." Halliburton expects mid-single digit revenue growth for 2027, as well as operating margins of about 15pc, up by two percentage points from this year. "This outlook assumes a flat North America market and does not require any recovery in North America hydraulic fracturing," Miller adds. "To us, that recovery is purely upside." Baker Hughes expects upstream spending to pick up going into 2027 after remaining flat this year. Supply disruptions across key producing regions have tightened inventories and boosted commodity prices, reinforcing the need for further investment in energy production and infrastructure. "While the need for longer-term investment remains clear, customers continue to take a disciplined approach to near-term spending," chief executive Lorenzo Simonelli says. Back in business The re-opening of Venezuela&#x27;s energy market has also sparked interest from the sector, with firms scrambling to secure contracts. SLB expects its revenue from the South American country to more than double this year. The firm says that over time it will be able to reach or even surpass the $1bn in sales that Venezuela used to be worth for SLB. "We&#x27;re well prepared, we&#x27;re adding resource, adding equipment," Le Peuch says. Halliburton says it is "open for business" in Venezuela, where it is already fielding multiple enquiries from potential customers. "The work that we started with has been primarily [well] interventions," chief operating officer Shannon Slocum says, but the company sees "solid growth" ahead. Baker Hughes has recently signed two strategic agreements in Venezuela. And Weatherford International says it is "furiously" taking on new staff in the country, supported by greater visibility with regard to contracting work. "We see potential for Venezuela to ramp up in 2027 to triple-digit revenues, with further increases in 2028," chief executive Girish Saligram says. News US plants run hard, margins triple 02/11/26 News 02/11/26 US plants run hard, margins triple Houston, 2 November (Argus) — US independent refiners ran refineries at near capacity and margins tripled in the third quarter, as they reaped the benefits from war-related supply disruptions. Refinery crack spreads have eased in recent weeks, but refiners expect the respite to be temporary as high global freight rates exacerbate steep heavy crude discounts, pressuring crude prices relative to products. Some of the downward pressure on margins is really related to headlines around strategic reserve releases and easing sanctions on Russia, among others, Valero chief operating officer Gary Simmons says, but he suggests that supply remains tight. "When you digest the data and you look at how much volume is actually coming out, and then lifting of sanctions is more just a change in trade flows, I think we&#x27;ll see a recovery in cracks moving forward," he says. Phillips 66 expects high refining margins to continue for most of next year on lingering global supply shortages as well as higher freight rates, executive vice-president of marketing and commercial Brian Mandell says. Freight rates should be stronger in the fourth quarter than in the third, with the very large crude carrier market tight and some products vessels being used for crude, he says. At the same time, US gasoline demand has declined by about 2.5pc and diesel demand by about 1.5pc, but this is not enough to balance the market, Mandell says. High global freight rates combined with more Venezuelan crude reaching the US Gulf coast market are weighing on Canadian heavy sour grade WCS. Canadian output is also outpacing the capacity of pipelines to carry it to market, further pressuring heavy crude prices, US independent refiner HF Sinclair says. Current WCS discounts of $25-30/bl to the WTI benchmark should eventually moderate while remaining wider than average "at least through 2027", HF Sinclair chief operating officer Steven Ledbetter says. Phillips 66 ran its refineries at 98pc of capacity in the third quarter, with distillate yields at 41pc, a record quarterly high. The refiner plans to keep runs high in the mid-90pc range across its system in the fourth quarter. Phillips 66 reported refining margins of $38/bl in the third quarter, more than three times higher than a year earlier. Valero also ran at near-record rates in the third quarter and its margins nearly tripled on the year to $39.24/bl. Light and shade Valero does not plan to restart its idled 145,000 b/d Benicia refinery in California despite new White House plans for recently closed facilities to reopen, the company says. The refinery has been idled since April and there are no plans for it to resume operations, Valero general counsel Rich Walsh says. "It&#x27;s just a tough environment to operate in the west coast and we don&#x27;t see the potential for that," Walsh says. But Valero has agreed to buy an idled refinery in Houston, Texas, from LyondellBasell and restart production by the end of 2027. The US administration is looking for opportunities to help reopen and expand refineries in an effort to reduce diesel and gasoline prices. But any refinery restart would take a while, making it "very difficult for them to help the prompt situation", Valero chief executive Lane Riggs says. The idea "that you can just sort of walk in and flip the lights" and a refinery starts running is not exactly the case, he says. Refiners are also sceptical that Russian diesel will help ease prices in the near term. Trump on 9 October said he had reached a deal with Russian president Vladimir Putin for some exports of Russian diesel to resume to the US and global markets. "We don&#x27;t think that Russia has enough distillate to make much of a difference," Mandell says. News Adnoc CEO warns energy supply buffers have ‘eroded’ 02/11/26 News 02/11/26 Adnoc CEO warns energy supply buffers have ‘eroded’ Abu Dhabi, 2 November (Argus) — The chief executive of Abu Dhabi&#x27;s state-owned Adnoc, Sultan al-Jaber, warned today that the world&#x27;s energy supply buffers have been all but "eroded" as governments have been forced to draw on their strategic stocks to compensate for the loss of supplies from the Mideast Gulf region. Speaking at the opening of the Adipec conference in Abu Dhabi, al-Jaber called on the world to learn from the lessons of the current crisis, which has seen shipments through the critically important strait of Hormuz disrupted for more than eight months. "Since the strait of Hormuz was disrupted, nearly 1bn barrels [of oil] have been drained from global inventories," al-Jaber said. "In some markets, stocks of diesel are at their lowest seasonal level in 35 years, and others are facing a 600bn ft³ LNG supply gap." Shipments through the strait have recovered somewhat since the early days of the US-Iran war, with the latest Kpler data putting flows of crude and condensate at 8.45mn b/d in October, close to 60pc of what was going through in February, just before the war began. But the availability of supply is only half the story, al-Jaber said, highlighting the steep jump in the cost of moving this oil and energy from the Mideast Gulf to consumers. "Before the disruption, shipping and logistics were less than 5pc of the cost of a barrel. Today, they are more than 30pc," al-Jaber said. "Energy security is not only measured at the point of production. It is now also measured at the point of delivery." Global energy systems need to focus more on making energy "accessible," al-Jaber said, highlighting the need for expanded strategic storage, larger stockpiles of products and more refining capacity that is located closer to consumer markets. He also stressed the importance of having more ways of getting energy to market. "Supply only counts when it arrives," al-Jaber said. "Infrastructure must be built before it is needed and enabled by advanced logistics." The UAE was one of just two Mideast Gulf countries, along with Saudi Arabia, that had oil export infrastructure capable of bypassing the Hormuz strait when the war began, putting it in a far better position than most to navigate this period. The latest data shows that the UAE has not only recovered its production from the early days of the Hormuz crisis, but recently lifted it to above pre-war levels. Argus estimated UAE production at around 3.9mn b/d in September, up from 3.53mn b/d in February, before the war started, and as low as 1.9mn b/d in March, the first month of the crisis. News Opec+ to keep output targets unchanged in December 01/11/26 News 01/11/26 Opec+ to keep output targets unchanged in December Dubai, 1 November (Argus) — A core group of seven Opec+ producers have again chosen to keep their crude production quotas unchanged, as the US-Iran war continues to disrupt supplies from several members. Saudi Arabia, Russia, Iraq, Kuwait, Kazakhstan, Algeria and Oman said after their monthly virtual meeting on Sunday that they would roll over their November quotas into December. The seven last updated their output targets in September, when they finished unwinding a set of large production cuts. The Mideast Gulf members of the wider alliance have boosted production in recent months by shuttling more crude through the strait of Hormuz, but their ability to sustain this is largely tied to the course of the Iran war and the conflict in Yemen, from where the Houthi militants continue to attack Saudi Arabia, including its civilian and energy infrastructure. Opec+ production by members with targets rose by 2.47mn on the month in September but remained 4.21mn b/d below their collective target and 3.16mn b/d below the pre-war level in February, Argus estimates. Iran&#x27;s production in September was around 1.55mn b/d below its pre-war February level, driven by the US naval blockade of its exports. US president Donald Trump has indicated there may be a resumption of direct strikes against Iran following the US midterm elections on 3 November. Iran&#x27;s supreme leader Ayatollah Mojtaba Khamenei said a renewed attack would be met with an "immediate, impactful, and geographically unrestricted response." Front month Ice Brent crude futures closed at $117.64/bl on 30 October, the highest since late April, while refined product prices remain at elevated levels.
