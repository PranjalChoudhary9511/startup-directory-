MUMBAI: The leopard-the sleekest of the great cats that hunt for prey in the African Savannah-is an unlikely source of inspiration for Mukesh Ambani, a man whose name is usually preceded by the adjective billionaire. But in an interview in his office on the fourth floor of Maker Chambers IV-the traditional seat of power of the business dynasty founded by his father, the late Dhirubhai, in 1958-wildlife enthusiast Ambani says the leopard’s hunting skills are a metaphor for what RIL, India’s most valuable private company, should take a few lessons from. “I love the leopard. It sits on top of a tree. It doesn’t take any risk. Whenever it sees it can make a kill, it rapidly makes the kill. It just happens in a flash,” said Ambani, who claims to have sat observing leopards for hours in many forests. The skills that Ambani wants RIL to internalise, the brilliant execution that has always been its forte coupled with a new-found emphasis on safety and total regulatory compliance, are part of what Ambani, 54, describes as his mission to “institutionalise” RIL. “After getting the kill, the leopard goes back to the top of the tree and sits there aaram se,” he adds.Much has changed in Reliance since the 1970s, then symbolised by bare-knuckled aggression of the legendary Dhirubhai Ambani who rose from a petrol pump attendant at a gas station in Aden into one of the biggest business tycoons in independent India. As RIL strives to be a multinational, this raw and brazen aggression is fast being replaced by a culture of calculated risk-taking. Ambani, India’s richest and the world’s sixth richest, who was in an expansive and relaxed mood, spoke to ET for over two hours on his vision of Reliance over the next decade. He advocates caution for RIL in betting the company’s enormous cash pile of `42,000 crore, or $9 billion, into new businesses. “It’s important to keep the balance sheet capability to do this stuff but we shouldn’t be in a hurry. We should feel absolutely safe that we are not going to hurt ourselves. And we’ll never do it for ego’s sake. Our watchword is: does it generate value for our shareholders on a sustainable and long-term basis?”Analysts have been hungry for guidance on what Reliance plans to do with its mountain of cash and on the future of the KG-D6 basin. The perception of uncertainty has hit the RIL stock, which closed at Rs 947 on Wednesday. It had hit a 52-week high of Rs 1,187 and a low of Rs 841. Ambani's vision to tap new outlets for RIL's entrepreneurial energies include an intriguing mix of old economy businesses and the newer ones close to the cutting-edge frontiers of technology. He calls these two business buckets 'energy' and 'consumer'. For now, 99% of the revenues come from the energy bucket, but it's likely to change significantly in future.  Energy is a 30-year-old business for us, but consumer is just two years old,  he said. First, the immediate plans: Over the next five years, RIL plans to invest $10-12 billion in what Ambani describes as  value-added chemicals . It plans to create a huge rubber business from scratch, with the intention of catering to the Asian tyre industry.  In technological innovation, we will see a lot of development potential in what I call value-added chemicals. The quality of life is improving in India, so we see a lot of growth in polyester and plastics. Therefore, we are reinvesting in these traditional businesses of RIL. We are developing a whole new rubber business. We will make RIL one of the world's largest players in rubber as the whole tyre industry moves to Asia. The big trend is that if you look at the next 10 years, all projections for automobile growth are China, India and Asia-focused.  Beyond these forays into what most analysts would consider commoditised sectors, Ambani is planning to have a second go at selling directly to the consumer. These include a foray into financial services, a renewed emphasis on retail and an ambitious plan to sell  digital services  to consumers. In the five years after 2005 when the undivided Reliance Empire was split between Mukesh and younger brother Anil, consumer businesses such as telecom, financial services and power were off-limits. In 2010, the brothers signed a second agreement scrapping such restrictions. ENERGY AND CONSUMER At a macro level, Ambani says the sprawling business empire can be categorised into two broad areas-energy and consumer, which includes businesses such as retail, financial services and even sports ventures like the Mumbai Indians IPL team that have a direct consumer outreach. For the past few months, Ambani and his trusted aide Manoj Modi have been moderating 'fierce debates' within a team of eight or nine people in the company who are trying to figure out the contours of the company's next big area of growth-digital services. Ambani says it is still early days for this new venture, but concedes that the digital services venture would have 'significant scale' and be on a par with similar offerings globally, in terms of technology and services.  Business opportunity (in digital services) lies in what you cannot do. What you can do, there's no opportunity there. Do you know how many bank accounts you have? Would you like one screen in which your debit, credit balances show up? Would you not like to see it? We just asked ourselves the same questions last week,  says the RIL chairman, adding that the company is considering delivering financial services, healthcare, media & entertainment services through a digital platform. For the record, Reliance has licences to supply wireless broadband services throughout the country.  The digital venture should be about scale. Doing it for 100 people or even just a hundred thousand people is not enough. It has to have scale. We are still figuring out the scale. This revolution is coming and let me assure you won't be 15 years behind, unlike the cellular revolution,  says Ambani.  We want to be the Walmart of digital distribution,  he says. LEADERSHIP PIPELINE The leaders to head these putative multi-billion-dollar businesses will emerge, Ambani says, from the crafting of organisational architecture that will 'institutionalise Reliance'.  I want to put together an institutional framework which will breed technology, innovation,  he explains. For this task, he has turned to memos written by late management guru CK Prahalad for inspiration besides hiring the services of the usual suspects among management consultants.  At an operational level, we are working with the best consultancies in the world, including the likes of McKinsey & Co, AT Kearney and Booz.  A significant part of this effort is also focusing on building a leadership pipeline within the organisation. When Reliance signed the $7.2-billion deal with BP in London, Mukesh Ambani's son Akash was also present, leading to speculation if the latter was being groomed as a successor. However, Ambani points out that three other trainees from Reliance were also present when the deal was signed.  I asked them to come along and see how it feels,  he says. Reliance says it has put in place an accelerated programme for grooming leadership talent. As part of the programme, high-performing talents in the 30-35 years age bracket are picked and trained as future leaders of the company. Management experts say Reliance's plan to build a leadership pipeline bodes well for the company as it is expanding into new areas of business.  If you look at GE, they entered several new businesses over the years, and managed to do well in each one of these areas, primarily because of their managerial depth and strength,  says Debashis Chatterjee, director, IIM-Kozhikode.  Reliance's success in new areas will depend on their depth of their managerial talent and adapting to the new cultures that these businesses will require,  he adds. Reliance has always projected itself as a company with one clear leader, be it Dhirubhai Ambani or Mukesh. The second rung-the likes of Manoj Modi, PMS Prasad, Nikhil and Hital Meswani-has always been people who are seen as old loyalists of the family. Will Reliance take a leaf out of the Tata Group and hire marquee names, including expatriates, to head some of its new businesses? Ambani says he is not particularly enamoured of 'well-publicised talent' and would rather focus on creating institutions and processes within the company which will enable leaders to perform.  We are not in the movie business. We don't need stars. At the end of the day, hum zero se hero banate hain,  argues Ambani, adding that he is not averse to hiring a marquee name, but the values and operating culture of Reliance are more important than an individual.  Our movie will not be a hit because it's got Shah Rukh Khan. It will become a hit anyway. We are not going to abdicate our business principles for one guy. Our businesses are now set. They can run without any one of us,  sums up Ambani. AIMING FOR THE SKY Apart from watching cricket and cheering for Mumbai Indians with wife Nita, he's also taking son Akash for business events such as the signing of the BP deal recently, setting off speculation in media of a succession plan.  It's too early for Akash or my other kids to get involved with business. Our job is to give them the best possible exposure, after which they have to fend for themselves. There's no doubt that they will inherit my wealth, but the board finally decides on the management of the company. It's still early days.  Is there anything he would like to do beyond business, a life after RIL if you will?  I like to think in short-term cycles and for me short term is 12-18 months and my next goal is to institutionalise RIL. That's really going to be my focus,  he said.
