Yet he has taken **zero salary** from his own company, Reliance, for five straight years, from 2020-21 to 2024-25. Before that, he capped his own pay at ==₹15 crore a year== for twelve years, from 2008-09 to 2019-20. [0,5,6]

=== C0 origins | Ages 0–12 | Born by a Harbour, Raised in a Chawl
0.0 p: Picture a busy British-run port at the bottom tip of Arabia in the mid-1950s. At a petrol pump, a young man from a small Gujarati village is filling tanks. His name is Dhirajlal Hirachand Ambani, and he is known as Dhirubhai. [16,18,19]
0.1 p: The port was Aden, then a British colony, today part of Yemen. Dhirubhai first worked at a pedhi, a small Indian trading firm, and then moved to a job with Shell that began at the petrol pump. A journalist whose own father worked in Aden remembered him as junior gas-station staff at Besse, the biggest trading company in the colony. [16,19]
0.2 photo: {"url":"https://upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/6/6d/CO_1069-677-81.jpg","page":"https://commons.wikimedia.org/wiki/File:CO_1069-677-81.jpg","caption":"Steamer Point, Aden, in 1952: the tower, the club and the port buildings of the colony where Mukesh Ambani was born a few years later.","author":"The National Archives UK","license":"No restrictions","year":1952}
0.3 p: Mukesh Dhirubhai Ambani was born in Aden in ==1957==. Decades later, an interviewer asked him how long the family had lived there. His answer was short: "From 1954 to 1957. I was born there." His mother, Kokilaben, was with Dhirubhai in Aden through those years. [16,18,0]
0.4 note: The richest man in Asia was born abroad, to a petrol-pump worker.
0.5 h: A village boy who sold bhajias
0.6 p: To understand Mukesh, you have to meet his father first. Dhirubhai grew up in Chorwad, near Junagadh in Saurashtra, the son of a village school teacher. He studied at Bahadur Kanji High School in Junagadh. [16,18]
0.7 p: While still a schoolboy, Dhirubhai opened a stall at the foot of Mount Girnar, a hill that pilgrims climb. On weekends he sold bhajias, fried snacks, to the pilgrims. He passed his school-leaving exam, the SSC, only on his second try, and then left for Aden with a job recommendation in his pocket. [16]
0.8 Q: Stories about his childhood have always inspired us, taught us how to cope with life's vicissitudes. -- Mukesh Ambani on his father, Rediff interview with Pritish Nandy, 1998 [16]
0.9 p: By the time Mukesh was born, Dhirubhai had saved about ==Rs 50,000==. Mukesh later said that sum "seemed to him, in those days, to be a pretty neat kitty to come back." So the family returned home to Bombay, now Mumbai. Reliance itself dates that return to 1957, while other accounts say 1958. [16,18,21]
0.10 p: In Bombay, Dhirubhai went into trading. He teamed up with his second cousin Champaklal Damani, who had also worked in Aden. They imported polyester yarn and exported spices to Yemen. That small trading firm is the seed of what became Reliance. [18,19]
0.11 example: For example: the Rs 50,000 did not go into a nice flat. The family moved into a one-bedroom home in a crowded chawl, and the money went into the business. __Dhirubhai put his savings into the company, not into comfort.__ [16,17,18]

He was Mukesh's first and biggest teacher.","author":"India Post, Government of India","license":"GODL-India","year":2002}
0.13 h: Seven people, one bedroom, one shared toilet
0.14 p: The chawl was called Jai Hind Estate, in Kabootarkhana, Bhuleshwar. Bhuleshwar is a congested old commercial area of Bombay, packed with traders. The Ambani flat was on the fourth floor, up a flight of 50 to 60 steps, as Mukesh's younger brother Anil later described it. [14,17]
0.15 p: Inside there was one bedroom. Seven people lived there, counting the grandmother. Anil remembered the bathroom most of all, because it was not theirs alone. [17]
0.16 Q: There was a common bathroom and toilet for a hundred families together in the chawl. -- Anil Ambani, interview with Vir Sanghvi, 2002 [17]
0.17 p: Mukesh does not remember those years as a hardship story. In a TV interview with Simi Garewal, he called Jai Hind Estate his earliest cherished memory, from when he was about four or five. A 2019 report of that interview describes the family as having felt secure and happy there. [20]
0.18 note: His first memory is one crowded room. Not a mansion. Remember that.
0.19 p: There were four children: Mukesh, Anil, and two sisters, Dipti and Nina. Their mother Kokilaben ran the home. Anil later said the job of keeping the children in line fell to her, "who was just a high-school graduate," and he gave her the credit for how the family turned out. [14,17,18]
0.20 h: A teacher who taught nothing from textbooks
0.21 p: Here is a detail almost nobody knows. In the mid-1960s, when Mukesh was about seven or eight, Dhirubhai put an advertisement in the newspaper for a teacher. But the ad said the job was non-academic. He did not want a tutor for marks. He wanted someone to widen his children's world. [14]
0.22 p: He interviewed several people and picked Mahendrabhai Vyas, who taught at the New Era School. Vyas came every evening and stayed until about 6:30 or 7. He played hockey and football with the children, took them to watch matches at the Cooperage ground, and rode buses and trains with them all over Bombay. [14]

As a boy, Mukesh was taken to watch matches here.","author":"AroundTheGlobe","license":"CC BY-SA 3.0","year":2011}
0.24 note: A paid teacher just for curiosity. Very important for future parents too!
0.25 p: Sundays belonged to the family. Mukesh remembered his father taking them out to forests and streams. Every year the family went camping and stayed in a village for 10 to 15 days. [14]
0.26 Q: However busy he may have been, whatever the pressure, Sunday was for his wife and kids. -- Mukesh Ambani on Dhirubhai, MoneyLIFE interview, 2007 [14]
0.27 p: Yet Dhirubhai was not a parent who hovered. Mukesh said his father never came to their school, not even once. He cared about the children growing in every direction, but he left the daily details to them and their mother. [14]
0.28 Q: There were boundaries, of course, but within those, we were not micro-managed. -- Mukesh Ambani, MoneyLIFE interview, 2007 [14]
0.29 h: The five-rupee rule
0.30 p: As the business grew, the family was no longer short of money. But the children still did not get to spend freely. Anil put it this way: "We had no lack of monetary resources, but we were given sort of goal-oriented finances." [17]
0.31 example: For example: after an outing, their father offered a choice. Either one drink and one snack, or two snacks and no drink. The budget was ==five rupees==, and Anil said it "didn't change for a long time." [17]
0.32 note: Money was there. The lesson was learning to choose.
0.33 h: Two days in the garage
0.34 p: Not every lesson was gentle. Mukesh told Simi Garewal about an evening when guests came to the house. He was 10 or 11, and Anil was 9. Every time their mother brought food out for the guests, the two boys ate it before the guests could. [20]
0.35 p: At first Dhirubhai stayed calm. Mukesh remembered him patiently saying, "OK! Sit down." The boys kept misbehaving. So their father told them they would stay in the garage for the next two days, until they learned to behave. [20]
0.36 Q: We really spent two days in the garage on merely water and roti. -- Mukesh Ambani, as reported from his interview with Simi Garewal [20]
0.37 h: Meanwhile, the business grew up too
0.38 p: While Mukesh was a small boy, his father's trading firm was turning into a manufacturer. In ==1966==, when Mukesh was about nine, Dhirubhai set up his first textile mill at Naroda, near Ahmedabad. It made polyester cloth sold under the brand Vimal. A visiting World Bank technical team later praised the mill. [18,0]
0.39 p: Mukesh later explained the bet behind it. His father had studied textiles deeply, learned the difference between fibres, and gambled on polyester when others did not. By the mid-1960s, Mukesh said, Dhirubhai had become the largest polyester trader around, and that was the foundation of Reliance. [16]
0.40 p: Around 1969 the family finally left the chawl. Anil said he was 10 when they moved out, which made Mukesh about 12. The new home was a flat in a building called Usha Kiran. [17,20]

0.42 p: So by the age of 12, Mukesh had seen two worlds. He had lived in one room with a shared toilet, and he had watched his father build a factory. __He learned early that money is a tool for building, not a prize for showing off.__ [14,17,18]
0.43 L: Comfort can wait. The business cannot.: Dhirubhai could have moved his family into a better home much sooner. He chose to keep living small and put money into the next machine and the next trade. For a first-time founder, every rupee spent on looking successful is a rupee not spent on becoming successful.

=== C1 growing-up | Ages 13–18 | The Boy Who Spent Weekends at the Office
1.0 p: Moving out of the chawl did not mean an easy life. The Ambani children now lived in a bigger flat, but their father's rules came with them. He wanted his sons to know the country they lived in, not just the rooms they lived in. [17]
1.1 p: Anil remembered one trip in the early 1970s, during mango season. Dhirubhai sent the boys to Goa on the steamer from Bombay, and not in a cabin. They travelled on the lower deck, with no reservation, and in Anil's words "everybody was puking." Their father could easily have paid for a plane. [17]
1.2 p: Why mangoes? Anil explained the logic in one line: "the only ways you can get a full box of mangoes is if you travel by the lower deck on the Bombay to Goa steamer." In other words, the hard, crowded route was also the one that got you the prize. It was a small lesson about how ordinary Indians travelled and traded. [17]
1.3 note: Rich kids on the lower deck. He was teaching them India.
1.4 p: The family's habits were simple too. Anil once said people were shocked to learn that neither he nor Mukesh drank or smoked, that both were vegetarian, and that they did not gamble. Anil said the core values their father passed on were being down to earth, humble and very simple. [17]
1.5 h: Two brothers, two memories
1.6 p: Here is something interesting. The two brothers remember their father's time very differently. Mukesh talks about Sundays outdoors and family camping trips. Anil, two years younger, remembered a father who was almost always at work. [14,17,20]
1.7 Q: I don't think I recall -- during my entire school or college career -- my father spending time with me. -- Anil Ambani, interview with Vir Sanghvi, 2002 [17]
1.8 p: Both memories can be true. A father can keep Sundays sacred and still be absent the other six days. What Dhirubhai did make clear was what he expected. Anil said his father told them, "I believe you have the brains so you should study hard." Dhirubhai had wanted to study more himself but never had the money. [17]
1.9 note: Same house, same father, different stories. Memory is personal.
1.10 h: Friends for life
1.11 p: At school, Mukesh made a friend who would stay close for the rest of his life. "Anand and I have been friends since the sixth standard," he said, speaking of Anand Jain. Anand later took commerce while Mukesh took science, but the two stayed tight, and decades later Anand was leading big projects for Reliance. [14]
1.12 p: His circle of friends was small and loyal. It included Ajay Parekh, who would go on to run the company Pidilite, and Kiran Manelkar, who became a doctor. Several of these boys ended up at the same college together. [14]
1.13 example: For example: the friend Mukesh made at about age 11 in sixth standard was still working beside him more than 30 years later. __The people you trust at 15 can become the team you build with at 40.__ [14]
1.14 h: Weekends at the office
1.15 p: Most teenagers in Bombay spent their weekends at the beach or the cinema. Mukesh spent his at his father's office. Dhirubhai shared his passion for business with his son from early on, and Mukesh soaked it up. [14]
1.16 Q: Even when I was in high school, I used to spend long hours at office on weekends. -- Mukesh Ambani, MoneyLIFE interview, 2007 [14]
1.17 p: He was also watching his father's character up close. "Papa was always very responsible and enterprising," Mukesh said in 1998. What stood out most to him was a spirit of adventure. Even decades later, he said, his mother still talked about that spirit in Dhirubhai. [16]
1.18 p: What was he watching? A company that bought, made and sold polyester. Reliance imported polyester and exported spices, and its Naroda mill was turning out Vimal cloth. A teenager at that office could see every part of a business at once: buying raw material, running machines, and selling to customers. [15,18]
1.19 note: Your first business school can be your parents' work. Watch closely.
1.20 h: The day of the raid
1.21 p: Then came a moment that tested him. When Mukesh was about 16 or 17, around 1973 or 1974, the business was raided by the authorities. His father was away in the United States. Someone in the family had to deal with it. [14]
1.22 p: That someone was the teenager. "I was literally in charge, handling the problem," Mukesh said years later. He did not say in that interview which agency carried out the raid or how it ended, and we have not found a reliable record of it, so we will not guess. [14]
1.23 example: For example: at the age when most Indian students are worrying about Class 11 and 12 exams, Mukesh was the family's point person during a raid, with his father on the other side of the world. [14]
1.24 note: Sixteen. Facing a raid alone. Pressure training started early.
1.25 h: One line from a movie
1.26 p: What do you study when your father's business is cloth? Everyone at home thought the answer was obvious. Vimal was a successful textile brand, so Mukesh would do textile engineering and join the mill. [14]
1.27 p: But a single line from a Hollywood film had stuck in his head. In The Graduate, a young man fresh out of college is handed career advice in one famous line: "There's a great future in plastics." Mukesh said that line "left a mark on my mind." Chemical engineering, he decided, "was supposed to be the future." [14]
1.28 Q: I shocked them by saying that I would go to IIT. -- Mukesh Ambani, on telling his family he would not study textiles, 2007 [14]
1.29 p: He chose science and he chose his college himself. In those days the IIT entrance results came out before the Inter-science exam results, so he cleared the entrance and joined IIT Bombay. Then, a few weeks later, the Inter-science results arrived. [14]
1.30 p: Mukesh did something few students would do. He left IIT Bombay and joined UDCT, the University Department of Chemical Technology in Matunga, where his friends were going. __He picked the place that fit his goal, not the place with the bigger name.__ [14,22]
1.31 note: He walked out of IIT Bombay. Read that again.
1.32 facts: 16–17 | Mukesh's age when he handled a raid while his father was in the US [14]
1.32 facts: 6th std | When he met Anand Jain, a friend for life [14]
1.32 facts: Rs 5 | The outing budget the Ambani children got, for years [17]
1.32 facts: Weeks | How long he stayed at IIT Bombay before switching to UDCT [14]
1.33 p: Looking back, the teenage years gave Mukesh three things. He knew the business from the inside, because he had spent his weekends in it. He had faced a real crisis before he was an adult. And he had chosen his own path in studies, even when it surprised his family. [14]
1.34 L: Pick your field for where the world is going: Mukesh's family expected textiles because textiles paid the bills. He picked chemical engineering because he believed it was the future, and that bet later turned Reliance from a cloth company into a polyester, petrochemicals and refining giant. When you choose what to study, ask what will matter in 20 years, not just what matters at home today.

=== C2 first-steps | Ages 18–24 | The Degree He Never Finished
2.0 p: UDCT in Matunga was not glamorous. It was a serious, hard-working place for chemistry and chemical engineering. For Mukesh, it was exactly the right room, and on day one he met the teacher who would shape his thinking for life. [14,22]

2.16 h: Stanford and the professor's question

2.20 h: The message from Bombay
2.21 p: Then the plan fell apart, in the best way. While Mukesh was at Stanford, Reliance won a government licence to make polyester itself instead of only trading it. Dhirubhai was not going to wait a year and a half for his son to finish the World Bank programme. [14]

2.23 p: Notice what Dhirubhai did not say. He did not order his son home. He praised the plan and simply told him the train was leaving. Mukesh got the message: "So, I decided to come back immediately." He gave the year as ==1981==. He never finished the MBA. [14,0]
2.24 note: Read this twice. He chose the project over the degree.
2.25 example: For example: waiting for the World Bank programme would have cost about 18 months. Reliance says its first petrochemicals and polyester filament yarn plant, at Patalganga, was built between 1980 and 1982 "in record time." Had he waited, most of that build would have happened without him. [14,21]
2.26 h: His first boss was not his father
2.27 p: When Mukesh walked into Reliance, he did not walk into his father's office. "Rasikbhai Meswani, Nikhil and Hital's father, was my first boss," he said. Rasikbhai was running the company's polyester business. [14]
2.28 p: That business was still mostly trading and processing. It imported polyester fibre, texturised it, and sold it to textile mills. Mukesh started there, learning how the existing business made money before building the new one. [14]
2.29 p: Then he moved to the polyester filament yarn project. His job sounds simple on paper and is hard in real life: organise the work, set up reporting structures so everyone knew who answered to whom, and keep people motivated. [14]
2.30 note: He was about 24 here. Twenty-four. Building a factory team.
2.31 h: Rule number one: start from zero
2.32 p: There was a hard rule attached to the new business. Mukesh was told to build it without borrowing people from the existing company. In his words, the instruction was: "Build this business from scratch, without taking anyone from Reliance." [15]
2.33 p: That forced discipline. He had to hire, train and organise a brand new team. Mukesh later said the first 200 or so people who built Patalganga with him were still around decades later, running different businesses for the group. [14,15]
2.34 p: Reliance also refused to cut corners on technology. It paid the American chemical giant DuPont ==$5 million== for its know-how, at a time when rivals were paying far less for their licences. Mukesh's reason was simple: "we wanted to work with the best in the world." [15]
2.35 Q: Getting a licence is nothing; you have to build a sustainable business. -- Mukesh Ambani, MoneyLIFE interview, 2007 [15]
2.36 p: That sentence matters because of the era. Back then, winning a government licence could feel like the finish line. __Mukesh learned that the licence only opens the door; the real work is building something that lasts.__ [15]
2.37 h: The big break, and a big loss
2.38 p: Patalganga was the start of something much larger. Reliance credits Mukesh with starting its backward integration journey: from making cloth, to making the polyester fibre that goes into cloth, to the petrochemicals that make the fibre, to refining oil, and finally to finding oil and gas. [21,2]
2.39 p: The early years had pain too. Rasikbhai Meswani, his first boss, died on 30 August 1985. "That was a huge blow," Mukesh said. From 1985 a former public-sector manager, K. K. Malhotra, worked with Reliance for about 15 years and helped it adopt best practices, and Mukesh called him "my guru." [15]

=== C3 every-company | Every venture, win or lose | Every Company He Built, Bought or Lost
3.0 p: Here is the first surprise. Mukesh Ambani did not found Reliance. His father, Dhirubhai Ambani, started it in 1966 as a small textile maker. So Mukesh is not a classic start-up founder. He is something rarer: a builder who kept starting brand-new industries inside a company he inherited, and kept betting the whole company on them. [0]
3.1 p: This chapter walks through every big thing he built, bought or tried to buy, in order. Some became giants. Some were taken away from him in a family split. Some were very public failures: a gas field that ran dry early, a shale gas adventure in America, a takeover bid he lost, and two mega-deals that collapsed in court rooms and board rooms. __Read the failures as carefully as the wins. They are where the lessons are.__ [0,32,52,53,55,58,59]
3.2 note: Watch how each project feeds the next one. That is the whole game.
3.3 V: Patalganga polyester yarn plant (1981–present) running
  idea: In the late 1970s Reliance mostly made and sold polyester cloth from its Naroda textile mill, which earned 60–70% of the company's profits. The problem: Reliance bought the yarn it wove. The idea was to make the polyester filament yarn (PFY) itself at a new plant at Patalganga, near Mumbai, so Reliance controlled its own raw material and its own margin.
  decision: Mukesh was studying for an MBA at Stanford when Reliance won the licence for the polyester plant. His plan was to finish Stanford in about six months, do the World Bank's one-year Young Professionals Programme, and then come home. He told his father the plan. Dhirubhai said the plan was sensible, but that he was starting work on the plant now and would not wait one and a half years. Mukesh dropped the plan and came back immediately. That choice, a degree versus a live project, set the pattern for his whole career.
  howStarted: When he joined formally, his father insisted he should have a boss. He was put under Rasikbhai Meswani, a senior family member who ran the polyester business. Mukesh later said he used his chemical engineering and business-school training to organise the project, set up reporting lines and motivate people. He was about 24. The first team was roughly 200 people.
  funding: Reliance was already a listed company (its 1977 share sale was oversubscribed about seven times), so the money for projects like this came largely from the public: Mukesh has said Reliance raised money from ordinary investors up to mid-1985 and again in 1989.
  whatHappened: The plant worked, and it became the first step in what business people call backward integration: making your own inputs, one step further up the chain each time. From yarn, Reliance moved on to the chemicals that make the yarn, and later to the oil that makes the chemicals. Mukesh said in 2007 that many of the first 200-odd people who built Patalganga with him were still at Reliance, running different businesses.
  lesson: Say yes to the real project early. Mukesh learned more building one factory at 24 than an MBA would have taught him, and he kept the people who built it with him.
  [25,28,29]
3.4 Q: The first 200-odd people who built Patalganga with me are still around, running different businesses. -- Mukesh Ambani, interview with MoneyLIFE on Rediff, 2007 [25]
3.5 note: He kept his first team for 25 years. Loyalty is a strategy.
3.6 p: After Patalganga, the chain kept growing backwards. A big petrochemicals complex at Hazira in Gujarat came on stream around 1991–92, making the building blocks for polyester. Each plant made the next one possible: the profits and the know-how from one funded and staffed the next. Then came the biggest step of all. [29]
3.7 V: Jamnagar refinery (1999–present) running
  idea: A refinery turns crude oil into petrol, diesel, jet fuel and the chemicals that Reliance's plants needed. India imported much of its fuel, and state companies dominated refining. The idea was to build a giant, modern refinery on the Gujarat coast, a short tanker trip from the Persian Gulf, and become one of the cheapest refiners on earth.
  decision: Consultants told Reliance the project was not viable. One key move made it work: Reliance got an exemption from a rule that forced crude imports to go through state-owned Indian Oil, which gave it better prices and steadier supply. Mukesh chose to build one huge plant from scratch, rather than a smaller one to expand slowly, so that its size would make every barrel cheaper.
  howStarted: Mukesh ran the construction himself. Forbes reported that for three years he flew from Mumbai to Jamnagar four times a week in a small Beechcraft propeller plane, a 90-minute trip each way. In 1998 a cyclone badly damaged the site. About 85,000 workers got it running again within a few weeks. The refinery started in 1999 with a capacity of 18 million tonnes of crude a year.
  funding: Forbes put the refinery's cost at $3.4 billion (other reports put the whole project at about $6 billion). Part of the money came from an unusual source: a $100 million bond sold in the US that would not be repaid for 100 years, paying 10.25% interest a year. A bond is simply a loan from investors that the company promises to pay back with interest.
  whatHappened: Forbes reported it came in about 30% cheaper than a comparable BP refinery in Malaysia, and earned about $10 a barrel in 2005 against about $7 for an average Singapore refinery. A second refinery was added next to it in a Special Economic Zone in 2008. Together they can process about 1.24 million barrels a day across more than 7,500 acres, with their own 2,880 MW power plant and ports.
  lesson: Scale is a weapon. If you are going to build, build big enough that nobody can match your costs. But note that it took a policy exemption to make the numbers work.
  [26,27,81]
3.8 example: For example: to meet green-belt rules around the refinery, the family planted about 102,000 mango trees on the land. Forbes said that was more than Emperor Akbar's famous orchard 400 years earlier. [26,81]
3.9 note: A 100-year loan. Read that twice.
3.10 V: Reliance Infocomm (mobile phones) (2002–2005) failed
  idea: In 2002 mobile calls in India were expensive and mostly for the rich. Reliance Infocomm was built to change that: a nationwide phone network on cheap CDMA handsets, plus a fibre-optic backbone for data.
  decision: Reliance chose to come in with enormous scale and very low prices instead of copying the existing operators. It laid the network first and priced for volume. Infocomm was launched on 28 December 2002, which would have been Dhirubhai's 70th birthday; he had died earlier that year.
  howStarted: By launch day Reliance had already spent about Rs 11,000 crore and had laid about 60,000 km of optical fibre, with total planned spending of Rs 25,000 crore. The first offer, the Dhirubhai Ambani Pioneer offer, asked Rs 3,000 upfront plus Rs 600 a month for three years, with a free handset. In 2003 came Monsoon Hungama: a phone and connection for Rs 501 upfront. About half a million people signed up.
  funding: The money came from Reliance Industries' own balance sheet, earned in polyester, petrochemicals and refining.
  whatHappened: Infocomm shook up Indian telecom, but Mukesh lost it. In the 2005 family settlement it went to his younger brother Anil, along with Reliance Energy and Reliance Capital. Under Anil it became Reliance Communications (RCom). After Jio's later price war, RCom stopped voice calls in 2017 and filed for bankruptcy in February 2019, with debt of about Rs 50,000 crore. Mukesh had no role in that decline.
  lesson: You can build something brilliant and still lose it if ownership and control are not settled. Sort out who owns what before success makes it hard.
  [30,31,85,32,33,75,79]
3.11 Q: This is the largest infrastructure and services rolled out by any new entrant anywhere in the world. -- Mukesh Ambani at the Reliance Infocomm launch, Dec 2002 (Rediff) [30]
3.12 V: IPCL takeover (2002–2008) merged
  idea: Indian Petrochemicals Corporation Ltd (IPCL) was a big government-owned petrochemicals company and a direct rival. Buying it would remove a competitor and add plants.
  decision: When the government sold its controlling stake in its privatisation drive in 2002, Reliance bid and won. Buying a rival's plants was faster than building new ones.
  howStarted: Reliance took management control of IPCL after buying the government's majority stake in 2002–03.
  funding: Paid from Reliance's own cash and borrowings.
  whatHappened: In the 2005 split IPCL went to Mukesh along with Reliance Industries. It was later merged into Reliance Industries, around 2007–08.
  lesson: When a rival comes up for sale, buying can be cheaper and faster than fighting.
  [29,32]
3.13 V: KG-D6 gas field (2002–2020) shut
  idea: Reliance began exploring for oil and gas under the sea off India's east coast, in the Krishna-Godavari basin. If it found gas, it could feed India's power plants and fertiliser factories and its own refinery.
  decision: In a 1999 government auction of exploration blocks, Reliance won 12 of 25 blocks, more than the state-owned ONGC's 8. Mukesh told Forbes in 2006 that oil is found in the minds of men: you have to believe it is there before you drill.
  howStarted: The Dhirubhai-1 and Dhirubhai-3 fields in block KG-D6 became India's first and largest deepwater gas finds to be brought into production. Reliance first proposed spending $2.47 billion to produce 40 million standard cubic metres of gas a day (mmscmd). In 2006 it raised the plan to $8.18 billion for a peak of 80 mmscmd from 31 wells.
  funding: Reliance owned 60%, BP 30% and Canada's Niko Resources 10% (BP bought its 30% for about $7.2 billion). After Niko left, Reliance held about two-thirds.
  whatHappened: Output began to fall short of plans from 2010, the second year of production. Only 22 wells were drilled and 18 put into production. It fell from 35.33 mmscmd in 2011–12 to 9.77 in 2013–14, and the fields stopped producing in February 2020. Estimated reserves were cut from 10.03 to 3.10 trillion cubic feet; Reliance blamed unexpected sand and water. The government blamed too few wells, disallowed $3.02 billion of costs, and in arbitration now seeks more than $30 billion from Reliance and BP. As of late 2025, the tribunal's award was still awaited.
  lesson: Never build a company's story on a single forecast. Nature does not read your investor presentation.
  [26,55]

3.16 V: Reliance Petroleum (second Jamnagar refinery) (2006–2009) merged
  idea: A second, export-focused refinery right next to the first one at Jamnagar, inside a Special Economic Zone. The plan would roughly double Jamnagar into the largest refining complex on earth.
  decision: Instead of funding it all from Reliance Industries, Mukesh put the project in a separate company, Reliance Petroleum Ltd (RPL), and sold shares in it to the public. That shared the cost and risk.
  howStarted: RPL listed on the stock market in 2006 at Rs 60 a share. The US engineering firm Bechtel built the refinery, which started in 2008 with a crude capacity of 580,000 barrels a day.
  funding: Public shareholders through the 2006 share sale, plus Reliance Industries' own stake. Forbes reported a $6 billion budget for doubling Jamnagar.
  whatHappened: RPL was merged back into Reliance Industries in 2009. But the share sales around it led to a long legal fight. SEBI, India's market regulator, alleged manipulative trading by Reliance in RPL shares in November 2007. That case is told in the near-death chapter.
  lesson: Separate companies can raise money for one big project, but every share trade around them gets watched closely.
  [26,27,28,29,66]
3.17 V: Reliance Retail (2006–present) running
  idea: In 2006 almost all of India's shopping happened in small family shops. Mukesh wanted to build a chain of modern stores, starting with fresh fruit and vegetables bought straight from farmers, then going into electronics, fashion and groceries.
  decision: At the June 2006 AGM he committed Rs 25,000 crore and said organised retail would be the overarching theme of Reliance's growth. He chose to start with food, the thing every family buys every week, rather than luxury.
  howStarted: On 3 November 2006 Reliance opened 11 Reliance Fresh stores in Hyderabad, the first in Banjara Hills. The targets were huge: $25 billion of revenue in four years, a presence in 784 cities and 6,000 rural market towns by 2010, and about one million jobs.
  funding: An initial Rs 10,000 crore of equity from Reliance Industries, to grow to Rs 25,000 crore. Much later, in 2020, it sold stakes to Silver Lake, KKR, GIC, TPG, Mubadala and General Atlantic at a $58.5 billion pre-money valuation, and in 2023 to Qatar's QIA and Abu Dhabi's ADIA at a $100 billion valuation.
  whatHappened: The start was rough. In August 2007 traders attacked new stores in Lucknow and the Uttar Pradesh government ordered Reliance stores closed, and Reliance cut hundreds of jobs there. The 2010 targets were missed by years. But it kept going, bought brands such as Hamleys (2019, about 68 million pounds), Netmeds, Urban Ladder, Zivame, Just Dial and Metro Cash and Carry India, and in 2025–26 had revenue of Rs 3,71,085 crore and 387 million registered customers.
  lesson: Big targets are fine, but local politics and small traders are real forces. Survive the bad first years and keep compounding.
  [34,35,36,37,46,47,71,73]
3.18 Q: Organised retailing will be the overarching theme of the expansion and growth of Reliance in the near-term future. -- Mukesh Ambani, Reliance AGM, June 2006 (Rediff) [34]
3.19 V: Petrol pumps and Jio-bp (2000s–present) running
  idea: With a giant refinery, Reliance had fuel to sell. Its own branded petrol pumps would let it sell straight to drivers.
  decision: By 2006 Reliance had about 1,200 pumps and Mukesh planned to grow to 6,000 for $1.5 billion. Later, he chose to bring in a partner rather than run the business alone.
  howStarted: The pumps ran alongside a highway truck-stop chain, A1 Plaza, that had about 100 restaurants by 2006.
  funding: Reliance's own money at first. In 2020 BP bought into the fuel retail business as part of the cash Reliance raised to cut its debt. Reliance owns 51% and BP 49% of the venture, now called Jio-bp.
  whatHappened: Jio-bp operates over 1,700 fuel stations, far below the 6,000 that Mukesh planned in 2006.
  lesson: When a business needs a skill you lack, a partner with that skill can be worth half the company.

3.20 V: Mumbai Indians (cricket team) (2008–present) running
  idea: In 2008 the Indian Premier League (IPL) auctioned city cricket teams. Owning one put a brand in front of hundreds of millions of fans.
  decision: Reliance bid for the Mumbai franchise, its home city.
  howStarted: Reliance won the team in the January 2008 auction.
  funding: The winning bid was $111.9 million, paid by Reliance.
  whatHappened: The team gave Reliance a permanent place in Indian cricket. Years later, IPL streaming rights also sat inside Reliance's media business.
  lesson: Brands are built where attention already lives. Cricket is where India's attention lives.

3.21 V: LyondellBasell takeover bid (2009–2010) failed
  idea: LyondellBasell, a huge US–Dutch chemicals and refining company, was in US bankruptcy. Buying it would have made Reliance a global petrochemicals giant overnight.
  decision: Mukesh decided to bid while the company was weak. In November 2009 Reliance made an unsolicited, non-binding offer of about $12 billion for control.
  howStarted: Reliance's team reportedly visited the company around 20 times and revised its offer twice.
  funding: The offer was planned with Reliance's cash and borrowing.
  whatHappened: Reliance raised its offer to about $13.5 billion in January 2010 and about $14.5 billion in February. LyondellBasell's board said no and went with its own rescue plan, which valued it at $15.5 billion. Its largest owner, Len Blavatnik's Access Industries, called Reliance's offer too low. In March 2010 Reliance said it would not raise the bid again, saying market conditions did not justify it.
  lesson: Know your walk-away price before you start. Walking away from a deal can be the winning move.
  [53,54]
3.22 V: US shale gas (2010–2021) sold
  idea: Around 2010 a new drilling method opened up huge gas fields in shale rock in the US. Reliance wanted to learn the technology and own a piece of the boom.
  decision: Instead of buying whole companies, Reliance bought minority stakes in joint ventures with experienced American operators: Chevron, Pioneer Natural Resources and Carrizo.
  howStarted: In 2010 it paid $392 million for a 60% stake in a Marcellus shale venture in Pennsylvania, and $46 million for 49.9% of a pipeline venture in Texas's Eagle Ford region, later putting in another $208 million.
  funding: Reliance's own balance sheet.
  whatHappened: When oil and gas prices crashed after 2014, shale lost its shine. Reliance sold the Eagle Ford pipeline venture for $1 billion in 2015, a good result. But it sold the Marcellus assets bought for $392 million for $126 million in 2017, then other Marcellus assets for $250 million in 2021, and finally exited Eagle Ford in November 2021.
  lesson: Buying into a hot trend at its peak is risky. The one profit came from the boring pipeline, not the exciting drilling.
  [52]
3.23 note: Bought for $392 million. Sold for $126 million. Ouch.
3.24 V: Reliance Jio (2010–present) public
  idea: In 2010 Indians used their phones mostly for calls; data was slow and costly. Mukesh's idea was a 4G network built only for data, with voice calls carried as data and given away free, so that hundreds of millions of people could get online cheaply.
  decision: After the 2005 split a non-compete deal kept him out of telecom. Within a month of the brothers ending that pact in 2010, he moved. He chose to buy a little-known company that had just won spectrum (the radio waves a phone network needs) rather than bid in his own name.
  howStarted: In June 2010 Infotel Broadband, a small firm promoted by the son of HFCL's founder, became the only bidder to win broadband spectrum in all 22 telecom circles, for Rs 12,848 crore. About an hour after the auction closed, Reliance said it would buy 95% of Infotel for Rs 4,800 crore. It was later renamed Reliance Jio Infocomm. Commercial service started in September 2016.
  funding: Reliance Industries funded the network from refinery profits and heavy borrowing, a bet The Economist put at about $25 billion by 2017. In 2020 it sold about a third of Jio Platforms to Facebook, Google and others for about Rs 1.52 lakh crore.
  whatHappened: Jio offered free voice and very cheap data. It reached 100 million users in 170 days and passed 200 million in under two years. Rivals were forced to merge or exit. By 2025–26 it had more than 524 million subscribers, and in October 2026 Jio Platforms listed on the stock market in India's largest IPO.
  lesson: If you can make something ten times cheaper, you do not compete in the market. You create a new one. But it needs patient money.
  [39,40,82,41,42,43,44,45,71,49]
3.25 Q: Jio has added seven customers every single second of every single day. -- Mukesh Ambani, February 2017 (TelecomTalk) [41]
3.26 V: Network18 and media (2012–present) running
  idea: A data network needs things to watch. Owning TV channels and news sites would give Jio content and Reliance a voice in media.
  decision: In 2012 Network18, a debt-laden media group run by Raghav Bahl, needed money. Reliance chose to fund it indirectly instead of buying it outright at first.
  howStarted: Reliance set up a trust, the Independent Media Trust, which channelled money into Network18 and TV18 in 2012, with Network18 receiving a net Rs 4,000 crore. As part of the deal Network18 bought stakes in the ETV regional channels.
  funding: Reliance Industries' cash through the trust.
  whatHappened: In July 2014 Reliance took formal control of Network18 and TV18, and the founder left. A plan to merge its Viacom18 venture with Sony's India channels was dropped in 2020. Viacom18 later became the base for the much bigger Disney deal in 2024.
  lesson: Control often comes in two steps: first you lend, then you own.

3.27 V: Saudi Aramco deal for the oil business (2019–2021) failed
  idea: Sell 20% of Reliance's oil-to-chemicals (O2C) business, the refineries and petrochemical plants, to Saudi Aramco, the world's biggest oil company. Reliance would get cash to cut debt, and a guaranteed partner for crude.
  decision: Mukesh announced the plan in August 2019, valuing the business at $75 billion including debt. Aramco would also supply 500,000 barrels a day of crude to Jamnagar. It was one of the largest foreign investments ever planned for India.
  howStarted: The two sides signed only a non-binding letter of intent, subject to checks and a final agreement.
  funding: Aramco was to pay roughly $15 billion for the 20%.
  whatHappened: Reliance first hoped to close by March 2020. It missed that date, and in July 2020 Mukesh blamed the energy market and Covid-19. In November 2021 the two companies said they would re-evaluate the deal, and Reliance withdrew its application to split off the O2C business. Reliance said its new green-energy plans had changed the picture.
  lesson: A headline deal is not a deal until the money moves. Do not count it in your plan before it closes.
  [56,57,58]
3.28 Q: Our equity requirements have already been met. -- Mukesh Ambani on the delayed Aramco deal, July 2020 (Argus Media) [56]
3.29 note: He had already raised the money elsewhere. Always have a plan B.
3.30 V: Future Group deal (2020–2022) failed
  idea: Future Group, owner of Big Bazaar, was a large Indian retailer and deep in debt (over $4 billion by 2022). Buying its stores, warehouses and logistics would make Reliance Retail far bigger, fast.
  decision: In August 2020 Reliance agreed to buy Future's retail, wholesale, logistics and warehousing businesses for Rs 24,713 crore (about $3.4 billion).
  howStarted: There was a problem. In 2019 Amazon had bought 49% of an unlisted Future company, with a right to buy into Future Retail later. Amazon said the Reliance deal broke that contract.
  funding: The price was to come from Reliance Retail, which was raising billions from global investors at the same time.
  whatHappened: A Singapore arbitration panel told Future to put the deal on hold in October 2020. India's Supreme Court ruled for Amazon in August 2021. In early 2022 Reliance took over many Future stores directly from landlords when Future could not pay rent. In April 2022 Future's secured lenders voted against the deal, and Reliance called it off on 23 April 2022.
  lesson: Check every contract the seller has signed. One clause in someone else's deal can freeze yours for years.
  [59,60]
3.31 V: New Energy giga-factories (2021–present) running
  idea: Reliance earns most of its oldest money from oil. Mukesh's plan is to build the factories for the next energy system: solar panels, batteries, electrolysers that make green hydrogen, and fuel cells.
  decision: At the June 2021 AGM he committed Rs 75,000 crore: Rs 60,000 crore over three years for four giga-factories, and Rs 15,000 crore for partnerships and new technology. He chose to buy technology instead of inventing all of it.
  howStarted: Work began on the Dhirubhai Ambani Green Energy Giga Complex on 5,000 acres at Jamnagar. Reliance bought Norway's REC Solar for an enterprise value of $771 million and Britain's sodium-ion battery firm Faradion for about 100 million pounds, both in 2021.
  funding: Reliance's own cash flow from its oil, telecom and retail businesses.
  whatHappened: By the August 2025 AGM his son Anant described the Jamnagar site as the world's largest new-energy complex, still being built. Forbes reports Reliance plans to spend about $80 billion on renewables over roughly a decade.
  lesson: Use today's cash cow to build tomorrow's business before you are forced to.
  [61,62,63,0]
3.32 V: Campa Cola and consumer brands (2022–present) running
  idea: Campa Cola was an old Indian soft-drink brand. Reviving a nostalgic brand gave Reliance a fast way into packaged drinks and food.
  decision: Buy an old brand with memories attached, instead of building a new one from zero.
  howStarted: Reliance bought Campa in 2022 for about Rs 22 crore and relaunched it in March 2023 in three flavours, through its consumer products arm.
  funding: Reliance's own cash.
  whatHappened: Campa now sits in Reliance Consumer Products, a unit of Reliance Retail Ventures, selling through Reliance's own stores and others.
  lesson: Nostalgia is an asset you can buy cheaply. Rs 22 crore is pocket change for a household name.
  [73]
3.33 V: Jio Financial Services (2023–present) public
  idea: Reliance's huge customer base could be offered loans, payments, insurance and investment products.
  decision: Instead of keeping it inside Reliance Industries, Mukesh spun the finance arm off as a separate listed company. Every Reliance shareholder got one share of the new company for each Reliance share.
  howStarted: It was demerged in July 2023 with about Rs 20,700 crore of cash and liquid assets, and formed a partnership with the US fund giant BlackRock for asset management.
  funding: Cash and investments transferred from Reliance Industries.
  whatHappened: It listed on 21 August 2023 at a price of Rs 262 set in a special trading session, then hit its daily lower limit, valuing it at about $19 billion.
  lesson: Spinning off a business lets investors value it on its own, but a weak first day can follow.
  [70,76,83,0]
3.34 V: JioStar (merger with Disney's Star India) (2024–present) merged
  idea: Combine Reliance's Viacom18 and JioCinema with Disney's Star India and Hotstar to create India's largest entertainment company.
  decision: First announced in February 2024. Reliance chose a merger in which it held control, instead of fighting Disney for cricket and film rights.
  howStarted: After India's competition regulator approved it in August 2024, the merger took effect on 14 November 2024, with about 120 TV channels and two streaming services.
  funding: Reliance put in Rs 11,500 crore. The joint venture was valued at Rs 70,352 crore (about $8.5 billion).
  whatHappened: Viacom18 holds 46.82%, Disney 36.84% and Reliance directly about 16.34%, so Reliance controls about 63%. Nita Ambani is the chairperson.
  lesson: Sometimes your strongest rival becomes your partner. Ask who controls the board.
  [64,65]
3.35 p: Put the list together and a pattern jumps out. __Almost every win came from building something huge where Reliance controlled the whole chain: the yarn, the refinery, the network, the stores.__ Almost every loss came from things Mukesh could not control: geology under the sea, gas prices in America, a rival's contract, or a family split. [52,55,59,32]
3.36 note: Control the chain, and you control the outcome. Mostly.

=== C4 the-big-bets | 1981–2005 | Build It Bigger Than Anyone Believes
4.0 p: Picture Mukesh in 1981. He is about 24, a chemical engineer who has just walked away from Stanford. His father would not wait a year and a half for him to finish, so he came home. Now he is on a construction site at Patalganga, working under a senior relative, with a team of about 200 people and one job: get a polyester yarn plant built. [25,28]
4.1 p: Reliance at the time was a fast-growing but still mid-sized company. Its money machine was a single textile mill at Naroda that made 60–70% of profits. The plan Dhirubhai and Mukesh followed was simple to say and very hard to do: stop buying from suppliers, and become the supplier. First yarn. Then the chemicals that make yarn. Then the oil that makes the chemicals. [25]
4.2 note: Write this down: become your own supplier, one step at a time.
4.3 h: Where the money came from
4.4 p: A start-up today raises money from venture capital funds. Reliance did something different. It raised money from ordinary Indians. When a company first sells its shares to the public, that is called an IPO, or initial public offering. Reliance did this in 1977, and the issue was oversubscribed about seven times, meaning people asked for seven times more shares than were on offer. [29]
4.5 p: Mukesh has said Reliance kept raising money from the public until mid-1985, and again in 1989. Each new plant was paid for by a mix of shareholder money, borrowing and the profits from the plant before. __The trick was that every project made the next one cheaper to fund, because it proved Reliance could finish what it started.__ [25]
4.6 example: For example: to help fund Jamnagar, Reliance sold a $100 million bond in the US that will only be repaid after 100 years, paying 10.25% a year. Investors were lending to a company they believed would still exist in the 2090s. [26]
4.7 h: The refinery nobody thought could be built
4.8 p: By the mid-1990s the next step was oil. Consultants said a giant private refinery in India was not viable. India also had a rule that crude imports had to go through state-owned Indian Oil. Reliance won an exemption, which gave it better prices and steadier supply. Without that one policy change, the numbers would not have worked. [26]
4.9 p: Mukesh ran the build like a man possessed. For three years he flew from Mumbai to Jamnagar four times a week. In 1998 a cyclone smashed the site. About 85,000 workers got it running again within weeks. The refinery opened in 1999, and Forbes reported it was built about 30% cheaper than a comparable BP plant in Malaysia. [26,27]
4.10 facts: ~85,000 | Workers who restored Jamnagar after the 1998 cyclone [26]
4.10 facts: 18 MMTPA | Jamnagar's starting crude capacity, 1999 [27]
4.10 facts: ~$10/barrel | Jamnagar's margin in 2005, vs ~$7 for an average Singapore refinery [26]
4.10 facts: 12 of 25 | Exploration blocks Reliance won in India's 1999 auction (ONGC won 8) [26]
4.11 p: That margin is the real secret of Reliance. A refinery's margin is how much it earns on each barrel after paying for the crude. A big, complex refinery can turn cheap, heavy crude into expensive products. Jamnagar became a cash machine, and that cash paid for almost everything that came after: gas exploration, telecom, retail and, much later, Jio. [26,27]
4.12 note: Every new business was paid for by the old one.
4.13 h: 2002: a death, a launch and a crack
4.14 p: Dhirubhai Ambani died in 2002. That December, on what would have been his 70th birthday, Mukesh launched Reliance Infocomm. Reliance had already spent about Rs 11,000 crore and laid 60,000 km of fibre. The next year's Rs 501 Monsoon Hungama offer pulled in about half a million customers. The same year, Reliance took control of the state-owned rival IPCL. [0,30,31,29]
4.15 p: After Dhirubhai's death, his two sons began fighting over who controlled what, and the row went public in late 2004. For about seven months, the feud ran in the headlines, while more than three million shareholders watched nervously. [32,0]
4.16 p: In June 2005 their mother, Kokilaben, announced the settlement. Mukesh kept Reliance Industries, with petrochemicals, refining, oil and gas and textiles, plus IPCL. Anil got Reliance Infocomm, Reliance Energy and Reliance Capital. The group was then worth about $23 billion in revenue, equal to more than 3% of India's economy. [32,33]
4.17 note: He lost the phone company he built. He was 48.
4.18 p: This was perhaps the hardest moment of his career. He had to give up Infocomm, the venture he had personally launched, and agree to stay out of his brother's businesses. __He kept the boring, giant cash machine, and that turned out to be the better half.__ [32,39]

=== C5 near-death | 2005–2016 | The Years Things Went Sideways
5.0 p: After the split, Mukesh had a giant company and a problem: what next? He could not go back into telecom, because of the non-compete agreement with his brother. So he bet on three things at once: more refining, natural gas under the sea, and shops. [39,82]
5.1 p: The refining bet worked. The second Jamnagar refinery, built through the separately listed Reliance Petroleum, started in 2008 and nearly doubled capacity. Together the two refineries can now handle about 1.24 million barrels a day, among the largest refining sites in the world. [27,81]
5.2 h: Shops, and stones thrown at them
5.3 p: The retail bet started badly. Reliance Fresh opened 11 stores in Hyderabad in November 2006 and talked about $25 billion of sales within four years. Small traders saw it as a threat to their living. In August 2007 traders attacked new stores in Lucknow, and Uttar Pradesh chief minister Mayawati's cabinet ordered Reliance stores in Lucknow and Varanasi closed, citing the protests. [35,36]
5.4 p: Reliance pulled back across the state. Business Today reported that 120 planned stores were scrapped and 870 staff laid off; another report put job cuts near 1,000. Protests spread to other states too. __It was a lesson that being big does not make you welcome.__ Reliance kept retail going elsewhere and quietly rebuilt over the next decade. [37,38]
5.5 note: Big companies still need local permission. Never forget the small shopkeeper.
5.6 h: The gas field that ran dry
5.7 p: The gas bet looked like a jackpot at first. Forbes reported in 2006 that the Bay of Bengal field was first estimated at 7 trillion cubic feet and later at about twice that. In 2006 Reliance raised its development plan to $8.18 billion for 80 million cubic metres a day. BP later paid about $7.2 billion for a 30% share. [26,55]
5.8 p: Then the numbers broke. Output began falling short from 2010. Reserve estimates were cut from 10.03 to 3.10 trillion cubic feet. By 2013–14 the field produced 9.77 million cubic metres a day, an eighth of the plan. In 2011 India's national auditor, the CAG, criticised the oil ministry for letting Reliance keep the whole block. The government disallowed billions of dollars of costs, and Reliance and BP went to arbitration. [55,77]
5.9 p: Reliance says the problem was unexpected sand and water, not mistakes, and that government officials sat on the committee that approved every dollar. The government says Reliance drilled far fewer wells than promised. In the arbitration it now seeks more than $30 billion. Hearings ended in November 2025, and as of that date the award was still awaited. [55]
5.10 facts: 10.03 → 3.10 Tcf | KG-D6 reserve estimate before and after the cut [55]
5.10 facts: 22 of 31 | Wells actually drilled vs planned in the 2006 plan [55]
5.10 facts: Feb 2020 | KG-D6 D1/D3 fields stop producing [55]
5.11 note: An eighth of the promise. Under-promise, over-deliver. Never the reverse.
5.12 h: Shopping abroad, and coming home empty-handed
5.13 p: With cash piling up, Mukesh looked abroad. In late 2009 he went after LyondellBasell, a bankrupt chemicals giant, offering about $12 billion, then $13.5 billion, then $14.5 billion. The board said no. In March 2010 he refused to go higher. Many investors were relieved: he had shown he could walk away. [53,54]
5.14 p: In 2010 Reliance also bought into American shale gas. It paid $392 million for 60% of a Pennsylvania venture. When gas prices collapsed after 2014, those assets lost much of their value. Reliance sold them in pieces, the first chunk in 2017 for $126 million. Only the Texas pipeline stake, sold for $1 billion in 2015, made a clear profit. By November 2021 Reliance had left US shale completely. [52]
5.15 h: The regulator comes calling
5.16 p: A legal shadow also began in this period. SEBI, India's stock market regulator, alleged that in November 2007 Reliance had traded shares of Reliance Petroleum in a manipulative way. In a 2017 order SEBI found manipulation and imposed a trading ban on Reliance, along with an order to give up about Rs 447 crore of gains plus interest. [28,80]
5.17 p: In January 2021 SEBI fined Reliance Rs 25 crore and Mukesh personally Rs 15 crore, with two other firms also fined. In December 2023 the Securities Appellate Tribunal set aside the penalties on Mukesh and two other firms. It noted that Reliance's board had given two other people the power to decide the share sale, and that a managing director is not automatically responsible for every breach. In November 2024 the Supreme Court dismissed SEBI's appeal against the relief given to Mukesh, while a part involving Reliance itself remained before the court. [66,67]
5.18 note: Cleared personally, after 16 years. Legal fights are slow and costly.
5.19 h: The all-in bet
5.20 p: By 2010 the brothers had ended their non-compete pact. Within a month, Mukesh was back in telecom. On 11 June 2010 Infotel Broadband, an almost unknown company, won broadband spectrum in all 22 circles for Rs 12,848 crore. Business Today asked the obvious question: who is Infotel, and how does it have that kind of money? An hour after the auction closed, the answer came. Reliance would buy 95% of it for Rs 4,800 crore. [39,40,82]
5.21 p: For six years Reliance built quietly. It renamed Infotel as Reliance Jio Infocomm in 2013, switched its own staff onto the network in December 2015, and kept pouring in money from Jamnagar and from loans. __This was the moment Mukesh bet the whole company again, just as his father had done with each new plant.__ [74,43]

=== C6 scale | 2016–2026 | Free Calls, Giant Cheques, Record IPO
6.0 p: On 1 September 2016, at Reliance's annual general meeting, Mukesh announced Jio's welcome offer: free voice calls, no roaming charges and very cheap data. Commercial service began days later. India had never seen anything like it. In the first month about 16 million people signed up. [41,74]
6.1 p: The growth was absurd. Jio hit 50 million users in 83 days and 100 million in 170 days. By February 2017 its users were consuming more than 100 crore GB of data a month. When Jio finally started charging, its Jio Prime plan cost Rs 303 a month for unlimited data. [41,74]
6.2 example: For example: Jio added customers at about seven every second, day and night, for its first 170 days. That is roughly the population of a small town every hour. [41]
6.3 note: He was 59 when he launched Jio. It is never too late.
6.4 h: The price war
6.5 p: A price war is when companies keep cutting prices to win customers, even if it means losses. Jio's free calls forced every rival to cut. Bharti Airtel reportedly spent more on its network in two years than in the previous 20. Vodafone and Idea merged to survive. Of the 16 or so operators in the market, most were pushed out or swallowed, leaving India heading towards just a handful. [31,42]
6.6 p: One of the casualties was close to home. Reliance Communications, the old Infocomm now run by Anil, stopped voice services in December 2017 and filed for bankruptcy in February 2019. __The brother who got the phone company lost it to the brother who was banned from phones for five years.__ [75,79]
6.7 facts: 170 days | Time for Jio to reach 100 million users (Feb 2017) [41]
6.7 facts: 200 million+ | Jio users by June 2018, under two years after launch [42]
6.7 facts: 524 million+ | Jio subscribers, FY 2025–26 [71]
6.8 h: The debt mountain
6.9 p: All this cost a fortune. By 31 March 2020 Reliance's net debt, meaning its loans minus its cash, had reached Rs 1,61,035 crore, about $21 billion. Mukesh had publicly promised shareholders he would make Reliance net-debt-free by 31 March 2021. Then Covid-19 hit, energy markets were thrown into turmoil, and the Aramco deal he was counting on stalled. [44,56]
6.10 note: A public promise, then a pandemic. Pressure like this breaks people.
6.11 h: 58 days that changed everything
6.12 p: What happened next is one of the great fundraising sprees in business history. In April 2020, in the middle of India's lockdown, Facebook agreed to pay Rs 43,574 crore (about $5.7 billion) for 9.99% of Jio Platforms, the holding company for Jio and Reliance's digital apps. Then came Silver Lake, Vista, General Atlantic, KKR, Mubadala, ADIA, TPG, L Catterton and Saudi Arabia's PIF, one after another, sometimes a few days apart. [44]
6.13 p: In June Reliance also raised about $7 billion from its own shareholders in a rights issue, which is when a company offers existing shareholders new shares at a discount. It was India's largest ever. On 19 June 2020 Reliance announced it had raised Rs 1,68,818 crore in 58 days and was net-debt-free, nine months ahead of the promise. [44,84]
6.14 Q: I have fulfilled my promise to the shareholders by making Reliance net debt-free much before our original schedule of 31st March 2021. -- Mukesh Ambani, 19 June 2020 (KrASIA) [44]
6.15 p: In July 2020 Google joined too, paying Rs 33,737 crore for 7.7% of Jio Platforms. In total, Jio sold about a third of itself for about Rs 1.52 lakh crore. The retail arm then raised about $4.4 billion more from GIC, TPG, Mubadala, General Atlantic, Silver Lake and KKR, at a $58.5 billion valuation before the new money. __The lesson: he sold minority stakes to the world's best investors, but never control.__ [45,46,48]

6.18 h: Two deals that died
6.19 p: Not everything worked. The Future Group purchase, agreed in August 2020 for Rs 24,713 crore, got stuck in Amazon's legal challenge. In April 2022 Future's secured lenders voted it down and Reliance walked away, though it had already taken over many Future stores from landlords. The Aramco deal, announced at a $75 billion valuation for the oil business, was shelved in November 2021. [59,60,56,58]
6.20 Q: The secured creditors of FRL have voted against the scheme. -- Reliance Industries stock exchange filing, 23 April 2022 (Scroll) [59]
6.21 h: Media, money and a new energy bet
6.22 p: Mukesh kept stacking businesses. In 2021 he committed Rs 75,000 crore to green energy. In 2023 he spun off Jio Financial Services, which listed at Rs 262 a share and immediately fell to its daily lower limit. In November 2024 Reliance and Disney merged their Indian media businesses into JioStar, valued at Rs 70,352 crore, with Reliance in control. [61,70,64,65]
6.23 p: Meanwhile the old oil business found a new and controversial edge. After Russia invaded Ukraine and Western countries capped the price of Russian oil, Reliance bought much more Russian crude. It rose from 3% of Jamnagar's crude imports in 2021 to an average of about 50% in 2025, according to the research group CREA. Reliance signed a 10-year supply deal with Rosneft, and in 2025 the US cited India's Russian oil purchases when it put an extra 25% tariff on Indian goods. [72]
6.24 note: Cheap crude is a great margin. It is also geopolitics.
6.25 h: October 2026: the Jio IPO
6.26 p: Back in 2019 Mukesh had said Jio and Retail should list within five years. Jio's turn came in October 2026. Jio Platforms filed its final prospectus on 14 October, offering 27 crore new shares at Rs 1,065–1,119 each, about Rs 30,213 crore in all. No existing shareholder sold. Only its own parent, Reliance Industries at about Rs 16.19 lakh crore, was bigger. A company that did not exist in 2010 was worth more than three-quarters of the empire that built it. [49]

6.31 h: How the fortune actually grew
6.32 p: Mukesh's wealth is mostly his family's controlling stake in Reliance Industries, which in 2012 was reported at about half the company. When Forbes profiled him in 2006, he was 49 and worth about $8.5 billion. On 29 October 2026, Forbes' real-time list put him at $80.5 billion, the 25th richest person in the world. [28,26,0]
6.33 p: The engine underneath is simple. In 2025–26 Reliance had revenue of Rs 11,75,919 crore, profit after tax of Rs 95,754 crore and 4,19,911 employees. Oil-to-chemicals still brought the most revenue, Rs 6,62,401 crore, but the digital business earned the most operating profit (EBITDA, earnings before interest, tax, depreciation and amortisation), Rs 76,560 crore, against Rs 60,546 crore for oil-to-chemicals. Retail added Rs 3,71,085 crore of revenue. [71]
6.34 p: __So the fortune grew in layers: polyester paid for petrochemicals, petrochemicals for the refinery, the refinery for Jio, and Jio, now listed, is the most valuable new layer.__ Forbes reports Mukesh announced plans in 2026 to invest about $110 billion in AI infrastructure across India over seven years. The next layer is already being poured. [0,26,71]

=== C7 how-they-work | Inside the machine | How Mukesh Works
7.0 p: Ask a simple question about Jio in 2018: who is the CEO? Reporters at The Ken found that almost nobody could answer it. The company had more than 100,000 employees and over 160 million subscribers, yet it had ==no CEO and no COO==. The biggest calls sat with Mukesh Ambani, his old friend Manoj Modi and his children Isha and Akash, and groups of presidents and vice-presidents carried them out. [86]
7.1 Q: But the buck stops with Mukesh Ambani and Manoj Modi. -- A former Jio employee, speaking anonymously to The Ken, 2018 [86]
7.2 p: Reliance told The Ken this was a choice, because it wanted Jio to behave like a startup. Compare that with Vodafone at the time, where the big decisions came from London and local chiefs executed them. Or with the Aditya Birla Group, where a named CEO ran the telecom arm. Jio does have a managing director today, Akash Ambani, but the instinct behind that old structure explains how his father works: a very small circle decides, and a very large machine executes. [86,87]
7.3 note: Org charts are optional. Knowing who decides is not.
7.4 h: The friend with no title
7.5 p: The most important person in that circle met him in college. Manoj Modi studied chemical engineering with Ambani at the University Department of Chemical Technology in Mumbai in the 1970s. When Modi once asked Dhirubhai Ambani for permission to go abroad for a finance master's degree, the patriarch refused. “It’s waste of time, do some work,” he told him. [88]
7.6 p: In 2013 The Economic Times reported that Modi's visiting card carried ==no designation== and that he was not on the board. It also said that if Reliance had a CEO, he would be the man. He was a director at Reliance Retail and Jio by 2020, and Bloomberg reported that a meeting with him often signalled final approval on a deal. In a rare public talk, he played it all down. [89,90]
7.7 Q: In fact, people internally know that I don’t even have a vision. -- Manoj Modi, at a Kalaari Capital conference, reported by Bloomberg, 2020 [90]
7.8 p: Do not believe the modesty. Air Deccan founder G. R. Gopinath, who sold a stake in his cargo airline to Reliance in 2010, called Modi “ruthlessly efficient”. Venture capitalist Vani Kola, who persuaded Modi to make that rare appearance, said the pair drive “relentless execution to the last level of detail”. __Ambani picks the mountain, and Modi makes sure every rope is tied.__ [90]
7.9 example: For example: when Jio was racing to launch and build its fibre network, one person in the talks told Bloomberg that select vendors were called to headquarters and tempted with the sheer size of the project. What the vendors did not know was that several Reliance teams were negotiating at the same moment in separate rooms, with Modi guiding all of them. [90]
7.10 note: Met at college. Decades later, still closing his biggest deals.
7.11 h: How a decision gets made
7.12 p: In 2011, long before anyone had heard the word Jio, The Economic Times got a two-hour look at the process. Ambani and Modi were moderating “fierce debates” among a team of just eight or nine people. They were trying to work out the shape of a digital business. The opening question was almost childish: do you know how many bank accounts you have, and would you not like one screen that shows them all? [91]
7.13 Q: Business opportunity (in digital services) lies in what you cannot do. What you can do, there’s no opportunity there. -- Mukesh Ambani, Economic Times, 2011 [91]
7.14 p: People who have sat across from him describe a listener. Lawyer Zia Mody says “he listens closely to the views of others, processes them and then takes his decision, and is fully accountable to his team for that.” KKR India's former chairman Sanjay Nayar remembers that “he’s all yours in a meeting.” These are business associates speaking warmly about an important client or partner, so read them that way. [92]
7.15 p: His attention is not spread evenly. Industrialist Harsh Goenka, a friend for almost four decades, says the kick for Ambani is a new venture, where he “often spends about 80 percent of his time.” During the Jio build, Modi ran things day to day while progress reports and briefings reached Ambani's desk at least once a week, according to a person quoted by The Economic Times. Jio was also built to be centralised, with most decisions taken in Mumbai and left to the regional circles only to execute. [92,93]
7.16 h: Why he banned the hero
7.17 p: His management idea was born in the early 1980s, when he came home to build the polyester plant. The rule from his father was blunt: build it from scratch, without taking anyone from Reliance. Within three months he noticed something odd. The whole industry ran on “heroes”, experts who wrote the temperatures and pressures in private notebooks and carried them home. [15]
7.18 Q: we don’t want people carrying their wisdom in notebooks as if it is some kind of secretive operation. -- Mukesh Ambani, MoneyLIFE interview, 2007 [15]
7.19 p: So he wrote everything into standard operating procedures that the whole plant could see. It looked like paperwork. Then came the test. Rasikbhai, one of the three people running the business, died on 30 August 1985, and Dhirubhai had a stroke the following February. For a while, Mukesh Ambani, then in his twenties, was alone at the top. [15]
7.20 Q: There was no sense of panic. The whole picture was in my head. -- Mukesh Ambani on the 1985–86 crisis, MoneyLIFE, 2007 [15]
7.21 note: Write it down. If only one person knows it, you have a hero problem.
7.22 p: The same thinking shaped how he bought knowledge. Competitors were paying half a million to a million dollars for licences, while Reliance, short of capital, agreed to pay DuPont ==$5 million== to work with the best. Then it did the clever part. It brought in a few DuPont experts and put 25-year-olds beside them to learn how to run the chemical processes. [15]
7.23 h: Young people, big jobs
7.24 p: His father's rule was “Always keep the average age of Reliance at 30”. In 1998, Ambani said nearly Rs 500 billion of assets had been built by a team with an average age under 35. The habit has survived. In 2023–24, ==53.9%== of Reliance Group employees were under 30, and so were 81.8% of new hires. [94,16,95]
7.25 p: Young does not mean unguided. For the Jio build, The Economic Times reported a plan to hire two sets of leaders for some senior roles: an Indian with domain knowledge and an expat who could advise on global practice. Reliance also says it picks high performers aged 30 to 35 and trains them as future leaders. [93,91]
7.26 example: For example: when Reliance signed its $7.2 billion deal with BP in London, people noticed Akash Ambani in the room and assumed the heir was being groomed. His father pointed out that three other Reliance trainees were there too. “I asked them to come along and see how it feels,” he said. [91]
7.27 p: And what does he look for? Not degrees first. In 2025 he told McKinsey that the people process focuses on character, because it matters that people can trust each other. His theory of motivation is just as simple: “if you can win a person’s heart, their mind will work for you.” [96]
7.28 Q: Character is even more important than competence because competence can be built. -- Mukesh Ambani, McKinsey interview, 2025 [96]
7.29 h: The other side of the machine
7.30 p: A company this size also churns people, and the numbers are public. In FY24 the Reliance headcount fell by ==42,052==, and retail alone accounted for 38,029 of that. Hiring was cut by more than a third. Reliance noted that retail has high turnover, especially in stores, and one staffing expert said some roles may simply have moved to third-party payrolls, so this was not 42,000 sackings. [95,97]
7.31 facts: 3,47,362 | Reliance Group workforce, FY24, down from 3,89,414 [95]
7.31 facts: 1,43,280 | People who left on their own in that one year [95]
7.31 facts: 1,71,116 | New hires the same year [97]
7.32 p: One retail consultant told The Economic Times that managements were now “highly objective, even ruthless” about shutting stores that did not pay. Harsh Goenka has noted that in the early retail years many professionals came and left. Modi could be rough too: The Economic Times described him dealing with vendors “often in an aggressive language” and growling at anyone who crossed the boss. [97,92,89]
7.33 p: Here we must be honest about a gap. Former Reliance employees rarely speak on the record, and we did not find a named one describing how Ambani hires or fires people in person. Reliance does not advertise its structure either, as Kola pointed out. So treat this picture as what it is: built from his own words, business associates, press reports and company numbers. [90]
7.34 h: His hours, his habits
7.35 p: Nobody has published a verified diary, and the reports do not agree. A 2009 Times of India profile had him reaching the office at noon and working until midnight, and Open magazine said in 2020 that he works late and starts before noon. Nita Ambani said in 2013 that he gets home very late, but they make sure to eat dinner together. Newer lifestyle pieces describe a 5:30 am yoga start, which we could not trace to a first-hand source, though she has said he does yoga and meditation regularly. [98,99,100,101,102]
7.36 p: Other details from that 2009 profile: he likes long walks with a colleague as brainstorming sessions, reads management books and tries out what they say, and colleagues claim they have never known him to shout. In 2011 he was studying memos by the management thinker C. K. Prahalad while working out how to “institutionalise” Reliance. A former executive told Open that Walter Isaacson is his favourite writer. [98,91,99]
7.37 note: Notice the pattern: read widely, walk, argue, then decide.

=== C8 mindset | What he believes | Eight Things He Won't Compromise On
8.0 p: Mukesh Ambani is not a man of many interviews, which makes the few he has given unusually useful. Read them side by side, from 1998 to 2025, and the same ideas keep returning. Here are eight of them, each with a story. [16,15,96]
8.1 h: 1. Chase a billion people, not a billion dollars
8.2 Q: My father used to say that if you want to start a business to be a billionaire, you are an idiot; you will never get there. -- Mukesh Ambani, quoting Dhirubhai Ambani, McKinsey interview, 2025 [96]
8.3 p: The other half of that line is the point. If you want to impact a billion people, his father said, you have a good chance of success, and you can make “a reasonable amount of money” as a by-product. Ambani says Reliance starts every new business with one question: what is the most critical need for India's development, and how can it be met at scale over a long arc of time? You can find that a bit grand, coming from one of the world's richest men. But it did tell him which businesses to enter. [96]
8.4 note: Read this twice. Impact first. The money is the side effect.
8.5 h: 2. First ask: can we survive the worst day?
8.6 p: People see Ambani as a gambler. His own rule is about survival. “The principle is whether you can survive in the worst-comes-to-the-worst situation,” he told McKinsey. You begin by imagining the worst that could happen, and then you make sure you can live through it. [96]
8.7 p: In 2011 he explained this using an animal. He told The Economic Times that he had spent hours watching leopards in forests, and that Reliance should learn from them. At the time he was sitting on a cash pile of Rs 42,000 crore, and he said “we’ll never do it for ego’s sake.” [91]
8.8 Q: It doesn’t take any risk. Whenever it sees it can make a kill, it rapidly makes the kill. -- Mukesh Ambani on the leopard, Economic Times, 2011 [91]
8.9 p: Five years later he made the biggest kill attempt of his life. Analysts doubted India was ready for advanced digital networks, so he told the board what the worst case looked like: a poor return on the company's own money, and a digitised India anyway. __He did not bet that nothing could go wrong. He decided in advance that he could live with what went wrong.__ [96]
8.10 Q: In the worst case, we will not earn much return. That’s okay because it’s our own money. -- Mukesh Ambani, recalling what he told the Reliance board about Jio, McKinsey, 2025 [96]
8.11 h: 3. Ordinary people plus a clear framework
8.12 p: He is suspicious of geniuses. In the 1980s his reference points were American chemical companies, especially DuPont, with a huge US bias and a handful of outstanding managers from India's public sector. One of them, K. K. Malhotra, stayed for 15 years from 1985. “He was my guru,” Ambani says, and he came from the public sector, not from Silicon Valley. [15]
8.13 Q: I always believe that so-called ordinary people can achieve extraordinary results as long they are given a sensible framework. -- Mukesh Ambani, MoneyLIFE, 2007 [15]
8.14 p: Eighteen years later he made the same point to McKinsey. He said that “individually, all of us have great potential, but we are limited by our habits,” and that organisations are limited by their systems and structures. He says the books already contain the right answers. The real work is closing the gap between what is written, what the shop floor does and what the marketplace wants. That, he says, is execution. [96,15]
8.15 h: 4. Say no to the good thing
8.16 p: Here is a detail few people know. In the 1990s, Reliance signed a joint venture with ==Microsoft==. Ambani had lived in Silicon Valley, knew software was a giant opportunity, and even spoke about it in a 1995 speech. Then he walked away from the Microsoft deal. [15]
8.17 Q: we have too much soap on our body and we need to take a bath in the chemicals business. -- Mukesh Ambani on why he passed on IT, MoneyLIFE, 2007 [15]
8.18 p: Asked whether that meant missing the bus, he did not flinch. “To my mind, I was right,” he said. You may disagree: India's IT services boom happened without Reliance. But the logic is clear. He would not start a second bath while still covered in soap from the first. [15]
8.19 p: The opposite move came when something threatened the core. Around 1996–97, Dow announced it would make plastics using E. coli. Reliance was already big in plastics and feared being ruined, so it quickly put four or five people on the problem. That defensive scramble is how it entered biotech. [15]
8.20 p: Then, in the late 1990s, with cash flowing, his father told him, “now it is your call, what you would like to do.” He chose to use the money for businesses of the future, and that is how Infocomm was born. [15]
8.21 note: Focus is not 'no forever'. It is 'not until the soap is off'.
8.22 h: 5. Let the young overrule you
8.23 p: Ambani says that when he was about 14, his father told him he was not under training: “You are my partner and this is your responsibility.” He says he has done the same with his children and with the 30-year-olds at Reliance. The story that proves it is now famous. Isha came home from Yale and said, “Dad, the internet in our house sucks.” His first reaction, as he told it in 2016, was to insist it was the best internet available. [94,103]
8.24 p: Then Akash told him, “Dad, you won’t get it.” In the old telecom world, Akash explained, you earn money only when one person phones another and the other picks up. In his world, he just talks to a server, and there is never a no-reply. A father worth billions took notes. That idea later became a $25 billion bet. [94,104,96]
8.25 Q: My children boss over me. It’s a new life. It’s good for me. -- Mukesh Ambani, 2016 [94]
8.26 note: In his fifties, he was learning telecom from his kids. What's your excuse?
8.27 h: 6. Own the technology
8.28 p: When he was young, Indian companies licensed technology from abroad and could “lose your shirt” if a plan failed. So he pushed his leaders to become owners of technology. For 5G, Reliance built most of the system itself and used Ericsson and Nokia for about 20%. His reason is a little wicked. [96]
8.29 Q: I also wanted to make sure that our people were not too full of themselves. -- Mukesh Ambani on keeping Ericsson and Nokia in the 5G build, McKinsey, 2025 [96]
8.30 p: He told his engineers, “You have to be better than these guys.” Keeping a world-class rival inside your own project is a test as well as a safety net. Every day, your team can see the standard. [96]
8.31 h: 7. The Rs 10 rule
8.32 p: This may be the most copyable idea in this book. He sets an impossible cost target before he has any idea how to reach it. Whatever America builds in dollars, he wants India to build as if a dollar were worth only Rs 10. “When we start off, this looks impossible,” he said. “Then we think through it, value-engineer it and come close to it.” [105]
8.33 Q: I always say whatever the US implements in dollars we should be able to do it at exchange rate of Rs 10, then we would be globally competitive. -- Mukesh Ambani, MoneyLIFE, 2007 [105]
8.34 example: For example: he also looks for price gaps that nobody is exploiting. “From Bill Gates to my driver, everybody eats potatoes,” he said in 2007. Farmers in Uttar Pradesh and Bihar got about Rs 4–5 a kilo, while the same potato was Rs 25–30 wholesale in the Middle East, Rs 90 at Sam's Club in the US and Rs 110 in Europe. That is a gap of 1:20, and to him it was the same kind of arbitrage that built India's software exports. [105]
8.35 h: 8. Build something that outlasts you
8.36 p: His mind keeps returning to a sentence from his father. “Reliance is a process. It’s an institution that should last.” He calls the promise that it will outlive him his commitment to his father, and he says he wants Reliance serving India after it turns 100. He also says business strategy should change every three, four or five years, while the goal stays fixed. [96]
8.37 p: That is why he talks about culture as a risk tool. About 30 or 40 years ago, he says, he made himself a personal principle: he must be able to look any employee in the eye. Reliance tells its top 100 leaders the same thing, that “we should be able to look at each other and say we are not embarrassed.” He calls this culture “our best insurance against any kind of risk.” As later chapters show, regulators and critics have not always been so sure, which is exactly why the test is worth remembering. [96]
8.38 Q: You come without anything into this world, and you leave without taking anything with you. -- Mukesh Ambani, McKinsey interview, 2025 [96]

=== C9 personal-life | Family, food and a very tall house | The Man Behind the Tower
9.0 p: Start with Sundays. Ambani says that for his father, Reliance was his whole life, yet “Sunday was for his wife and kids” no matter how much pressure there was, and it was not for homework. They walked through forests and bathed in streams. He says he tries to do the same with his own family, and he became a big nature lover himself. [14]
9.1 p: His father never came to his school, not even once, and left him to choose his own subjects. He tried to bring up his own children the same way and admitted he could not always manage it. When Isha and Akash were in class three, he said, the parents behaved as though it was their own exam. He joked that he could beat any parent his age in the ICSE syllabus, until his daughter told him she had to study it herself. [14]
9.2 note: A billionaire memorising the ICSE syllabus. His daughter told him to stop.
9.3 h: Nita
9.4 p: Nita Ambani grew up, as she has described it, a middle-class girl who travelled by bus and train in Mumbai. Hindustan Times reported that Dhirubhai noticed her at a dance performance. She became the public face of the family's giving, heading Reliance Foundation, and served on the Reliance board until she stepped down in 2023 to focus on it. In a 2018 speech, he publicly called her his life-partner and thanked her for her “steadfast support”. [100,106,104]
9.5 p: Her description of him is domestic. “He reaches home very late in the evening but we make it a point to have dinner together,” she said in 2013. They holidayed mostly in the jungles of India and Africa, she said. The paper also reported that the children got just Rs 5 each in pocket money when small and were not unfamiliar with public transport. Asked what she would change about him, she chose “his love for food!” [100]
9.6 h: Idli, films and no birthday parties
9.7 p: Food is his one admitted weakness. He told Upper Crust magazine that the family eats home-cooked vegetarian food, and that he also ate eggs. “Idli-sambar drives me wild!” he said, adding that he could eat it anywhere, even at small airport canteens. Nita said in 2024 that he remains on a strict vegetarian diet of mainly home food. [107,102]
9.8 p: His other escape is the screen. The Times of India reported in 2009 that he loved Hindi films, did not miss a single one, and that friends swore by his verdict on whether a film would be a hit. James Crabtree wrote of late-night Bollywood films in Antilia's home cinema, and a former executive told Open that his favourite film is The Godfather. The same 2009 profile says he had never celebrated a birthday except his 50th, with employees at the Jamnagar plant. [98,108,99]
9.9 h: The brother
9.10 p: The most painful chapter of his personal life was public from the start. After Dhirubhai died in 2002 without a will, the brothers fell out. Both lived in the same family tower, Sea Wind, on separate floors, and during the feud they ==co-ordinated their entry and exit== to avoid meeting in the lift. Their mother Kokilaben lived in the same building. The business story of that fight is in the next chapters. Here, what matters is how it ended, and who ended it. [108]
9.11 Q: With the blessings of Srinathji, I have today amicably resolved the issues between my two sons, Mukesh and Anil -- Kokilaben Ambani, statement, June 2005 [109,108]
9.12 p: Fourteen years later came a twist no scriptwriter would dare. In February 2019, the Supreme Court gave Anil Ambani four weeks to pay Swedish company Ericsson, or spend three months in jail. On 18 March, one day before the deadline, the money arrived. Anil issued a statement thanking his brother and sister-in-law. People familiar with the matter told ET Telecom that Mukesh contributed around ==Rs 480 crore==, as an outright payment and not a loan, though Reuters said the nature of the backing was unclear. [110,111,112]
9.13 Q: I and my family are grateful we have moved beyond the past, and are deeply touched with this gesture -- Anil Ambani, statement, 18 March 2019 [110,111]
9.14 note: Rivals for 15 years. He still paid before the jail door closed.
9.15 h: A son's health, and a father's tears
9.16 p: The family itself has spoken publicly about their youngest son's health, so we repeat only that. Nita Ambani said in 2013 that Anant was fighting obesity, and in a 2025 interview she linked his struggle to asthma. At his pre-wedding celebration in Jamnagar in March 2024, Anant spoke about it himself in front of the guests, and his father wept. [100,113,114,115]
9.17 Q: my life has not always been entirely a bed of roses. I have also experienced the pain of thorns. -- Anant Ambani, Jamnagar, March 2024 [114]
9.18 p: It was a rare public show of emotion from a very private man. It would not be the last. You will see him cry again in the final chapter of this book. [114,87]
9.19 h: The car outside the house
9.20 p: On 25 February 2021, a Scorpio SUV with 20 sticks of gelatin and a threatening letter was found near Antilia. The head of security at the residence later told investigators that Nita Ambani's planned trip to Jamnagar was rescheduled and then cancelled on his and the police's advice. The Ambani family did not suspect any particular person, he said. Then the case took a dark turn. The car's owner, Mansukh Hiran, was found dead on 5 March, and the first lead investigator, police officer Sachin Waze, was arrested. [116,117,118]
9.21 p: In August 2026 a special NIA court framed charges against ten people, including Waze, on charges including murder and terror offences. They have pleaded not guilty, and the trial is still ahead, so nothing here is a finding of guilt. The NIA alleges the motive was to terrorise wealthy people and extort money. These remain allegations. [118]
9.22 h: How he spends money
9.23 p: The first answer is surprising. From 2008–09 to 2019–20 he capped his own pay at Rs 15 crore, which the company described as a personal example of moderation. Since the Covid year he has taken ==nothing==: no salary, perks or stock options for six straight years to FY26. His money comes from owning the company, and his promoter group entities collected close to Rs 4,000 crore in dividends. A salary of zero is cheap when your family group holds half the firm, but plenty of owners still take the cheque. [119,5]
9.24 facts: Rs 15 crore | Self-imposed annual pay cap, FY09 to FY20 [5]
9.24 facts: Rs 0 | Salary every year from FY21 to FY26 [119]
9.24 facts: About Rs 4,000 crore | FY26 dividend income through promoter entities [119]
9.25 p: The second answer is impossible to miss. Antilia, finished around 2010, has 27 floors, and press reports put its value at $1 billion or more, though Bloomberg's wealth index lists his Mumbai residential property at just ==$410 million==. A guest told Crabtree, “The chandeliers have chandeliers.” And in 2009 he reportedly owned Bentley and Maybach cars while wearing white shirts and saying “I wear anything that fits me”. Clothes brands do not interest him, by that account. Scale does, and at home he built it. [108,120,98]
9.26 h: Giving
9.27 p: The EdelGive Hurun India Philanthropy List ranked Ambani and family ==second in India== for FY25 with Rs 626 crore, up Rs 219 crore. Shiv Nadar and family, far less wealthy, gave Rs 2,708 crore. By our rough maths, Rs 626 crore is about $75 million, or under 0.1% of a fortune of about $80 billion today. Fairness demands one addition: Reliance as a company separately spent Rs 1,309 crore on CSR that year, and Rs 2,248 crore in FY26, the highest of any Indian company, it says. [121,122,0,123]
9.28 p: Some gifts are personal. In June 2025 he gave Rs 151 crore to his old college, the Institute of Chemical Technology in Mumbai, as guru dakshina to his mentor, Professor M. M. Sharma. And in the terrible April of 2021, Reliance said it reconfigured plants at Jamnagar to make medical-grade liquid oxygen, going from nil to over 1,000 tonnes a day, and supplied it free to states. The company's own account says he personally supervised the work. [23,124]
9.29 note: A refinery became an oxygen plant. Scale can serve in a crisis too.

=== C10 the-brand | Silence as a strategy | The Shy Man Everyone Watches
10.0 p: In 2007, the MoneyLIFE editors opened their interview with a neat summary: Dhirubhai was a larger-than-life patriarch, Anil was the public face of Reliance, and Mukesh was “an enigma”. Very little was known of his beliefs. It was the most expansive interview he had given, and he was already 49 and running Reliance. [14]
10.1 p: Nine years later, in front of a packed Mumbai hall, journalist Shekhar Gupta asked him why he rarely did events like this. His answer, in a wonderfully dry line, was that nobody had asked. Then he gave the real reason. [103]
10.2 Q: I generally think that I should only speak by action and not by words -- Mukesh Ambani, Off The Cuff with Shekhar Gupta, 2016 [103]
10.3 note: Most founders post first and build later. He does the reverse.
10.4 p: Those around him share the habit. Reliance does not advertise its structure, and Manoj Modi would appear quietly at the side of the shareholder meeting to brief journalists, then vanish. “Why talk needlessly when you can show your capabilities in the ranbhoomi,” said Vani Kola, using the Hindi word for battlefield. We also found no verified personal social media account for him. Reliance's own pages speak instead. [108,90,125]
10.5 h: One big stage a year
10.6 p: So when does he speak? Mainly at one event. His father built that stage, addressing giant crowds of small shareholders at football grounds and cricket pitches. The son made the annual general meeting his main stage. In 2023 he used it to put all three of them on the board, and in 2026 he announced the Jio filing there, calling it “a deeply emotional moment”. [108,126,123]
10.7 p: Read the speeches and you see a style. Short sentences. Big round numbers in capital letters. A nation in every paragraph. “Our mission and passion at Reliance is to make India prosperous,” he said in London in 2018, then he told a story no finance director would ever have written. [104]
10.8 example: For example: accepting a Financial Times award in London in March 2018, he did not start with spectrum or capex. He started with his daughter complaining that “the internet in our house sucks”. That one line travelled far. It was still being retold in the international press that September. [104,127]
10.9 p: __Notice what the story does.__ It turns a $25 billion gamble by the country's richest family into a dad being told off by his kids. A young reader can picture it instantly. It is also, as he told it, slightly different each time: in 2016 he said 2010, and in 2018 he said 2011. Good stories get polished, so always check the dates. [94,104,96]
10.10 h: Saying nothing, loudly
10.11 p: Silence has limits, as Antilia showed. When the tower was finished, people asked what he was trying to prove. Then they asked why he had not moved in, and the New York Times reported that he and his spokesman declined to discuss it. Rumours about Vastu faults filled the gap. It was finally Nita who confirmed they had moved in, in a 2012 Vanity Fair interview, after months of what the writer called “nuclear-treaty-level negotiations”, according to James Crabtree. [128,108]
10.12 p: Then came a very public jab. In May 2011, The Times of London quoted Ratan Tata asking about the house, “It makes me wonder why someone would do that”. Tata Sons quickly said the report was out of context and sensationalised, and that the remarks were about wider inequality, while The Times stood by its story. A local journalist also got hold of Antilia's monthly energy bill, reportedly Rs 7 million, which caused its own outcry. [129,130,108]
10.13 note: Silence protects you until someone else starts writing the story.
10.14 h: Close to power
10.15 p: Few things shape his image in India more than his relationship with the government. In September 2016, Jio launched with advertisements showing Prime Minister Narendra Modi's photograph. In December that year, the government told Parliament that the Prime Minister's Office had not given permission. Critics, including Delhi Chief Minister Arvind Kejriwal, attacked the campaign sharply. [131,132]
10.16 p: At the 2026 AGM, his very first paragraph congratulated Modi on being the longest-serving elected Prime Minister of India. Opposition leaders have long said Modi is too close to tycoons like Ambani, and Reuters reported posters welcoming the Prime Minister lining the road to the venue of Anant's 2024 wedding. Ambani's supporters see an entrepreneur backing his country. His critics see something else. You should know that both views exist, and that neither is a court finding. [123,133,108]
10.17 p: Anger can leave the debating hall. During the 2020–21 farmer protests, angry at new farm laws, protesters in Punjab cut power and damaged Jio sites, and ==over 1,500 of 9,000 towers== there were knocked out in December. Farmers believed Reliance would profit from the laws. The company went to the Punjab and Haryana High Court, said it had “absolutely no plans” to enter contract farming, and blamed “vested interests” and business rivals. Airtel and Vodafone Idea called that baseless. [134,135,136]
10.18 h: Spectacle
10.19 p: Having stayed out of sight for decades, the family then became impossible to miss. The Guardian said they spent almost $100 million on Isha's 2018 wedding, where Beyoncé sang. For Anant's in 2024, Rihanna performed at a pre-wedding party, Bill Gates and Mark Zuckerberg were on the guest list, and Reliance's official Facebook page helped publicise the events. An unnamed Reliance executive called it a “powerful symbol of India’s growing stature on the global stage”. [137,115,125]
10.20 p: Not everyone agreed. “Legally it may be their money but such ostentatious expenditure is a sin against mother earth and [the] poor,” opposition politician Thomas Isaac wrote. Residents complained about closed roads during monsoon flooding. Nita Ambani's reply was the one any parent might give: “Every parent wants to do the best for their child,” she said, adding that it showcased Indian tradition. [138,113]
10.21 h: How the world sees him
10.22 p: At home, he is several people at once. To millions of small investors, he is the heir to a family that made them money: Reliance has returned about 900% since 2006, and over 1.3 crore people applied for the Jio IPO. To others, Antilia is a source of what campaigner Meera Sanyal described to Crabtree as “rage”. Crabtree's book made the house a symbol of India's new Gilded Age. [87,139,108]
10.23 p: Abroad, he is a statesman of sorts. The Guardian's headline called the family “the Windsors of India”, the FT gave Reliance its Drivers of Change award, and in October 2026 US Secretary of State Marco Rubio toured Jamnagar with him. Reuters and Bloomberg use the same shorthand: Asia's richest, or near it. Far fewer readers know the man who passed up Microsoft. Most know the tower. [137,104,140,87,141]

=== C11 what-went-wrong | The bad calls | What Not to Copy
11.0 p: It is easy to write this man's story as one long victory. It was not. Some of his biggest bets lost money or dragged through courts for almost twenty years. Some he still defends. Below is the record, with outcomes, because you will learn more from these pages than from any Jio success story. [55,145]
11.1 h: The family war
11.2 p: Dhirubhai left no will, assuming his sons would run Reliance together. In July 2004 a board resolution known as Item 17 redefined the managing directors' roles, and Anil protested that it let Mukesh cut him down. In November, Mukesh told a TV reporter about “ownership issues” in the “private domain”, which he later said was torn out of context. Anil publicly blamed “chamchas, chelas and cronies”. Mukesh's camp later accused Anil of a “self-serving media campaign”. [108,109,146]
11.3 p: Their mother split the empire in June 2005. Look at what it cost him. Reliance Infocomm, his own telecom project, went to Anil, along with a non-compete deal that shut him out of telecom until it was cancelled in May 2010. He then spent years in court over gas prices against his own brother, winning in the Supreme Court on 7 May 2010. __The fight over the past blocked him from the future for five years.__ [146,147,142,15,148]
11.4 note: Very important for co-founders: write it down while you still like each other.
11.5 p: He seems to have learned. In 2020 Mint reported he was setting up a family council, and in 2021 Bloomberg reported he had studied the Waltons and the Kochs for a structure that could prevent another war. Reliance has not formally confirmed these reports. [149,150]
11.6 h: The retail promises
11.7 p: In January 2007, freshly launched into retail, he told MoneyLIFE that organised retail could bring ==1.5 million jobs== in three years and raise farm incomes by 600–900%. He added a caveat: “We might be wrong in some of them”. Within months, thousands of vegetable sellers vandalised three Reliance Fresh stores in eastern India. In August 2007, Uttar Pradesh ordered Reliance stores in Lucknow and Varanasi closed, after traders attacked them the day they opened. [105,36]
11.8 p: What went wrong? Scale frightened the small traders and vendors whose livelihoods it touched, and big talk about farm incomes did not reassure them. Goenka has noted many professionals coming and going. The business survived, and Reliance now reports 20,160 stores. But it needed almost two decades, and in FY24 it still cut staff and closed stores that did not pay. Being right in 20 years does not save you from being attacked in the first one. [36,92,151,97]
11.9 h: The gas field that ran dry
11.10 p: In 2006 Reliance told the government its KG-D6 fields would produce ==80 million standard cubic metres of gas a day==, after raising the plan from $2.47 billion to $8.18 billion. Actual output was 35.33 in 2011–12, 20.88 the next year and 9.77 the year after. Reserve estimates were cut from 10.03 to 3.10 trillion cubic feet, and the D1 and D3 fields stopped producing in February 2020, far earlier than planned. Only 22 of 31 planned wells were drilled. [55,152]
11.11 p: Who is at fault is still being fought over. The government says Reliance failed to follow the approved plan, disallowed $3.02 billion of costs and has now sought more than $30 billion. Reliance and BP blame unexpected geology, and say all spending was approved by a committee that included government officials. The arbitrators finished hearings in November 2025 and we found no reported award yet. A separate 2011 government audit also criticised the oil ministry's oversight. [55,152,153]
11.12 p: There is a second, separate gas case. The government says Reliance and partners were unjustly enriched by gas that migrated from ONGC's neighbouring blocks. Arbitrators ruled for Reliance 2:1 in 2018, a High Court judge agreed in 2023, then a two-judge bench overturned it all in February 2025. The ministry raised a ==$2.81 billion== demand, which Reliance calls unsustainable. On 30 October 2026, the Supreme Court appointed former judge Ranjana Desai as mediator. It is unresolved. [154,155,156]
11.13 note: A forecast given to government becomes a promise. Promise only what you can drill.
11.14 h: Shopping in Texas at the top
11.15 p: In 2010, Reliance charged into American shale, buying into Atlas's Marcellus acreage for $1.7 billion, Pioneer's Eagle Ford for about $1.3 billion and a Carrizo venture for $392 million. Then oil crashed in late 2014. By 2016 it had put in about ==$8.2 billion== across its ventures. The Carrizo stake bought for $392 million sold for just $126 million in 2017, and Reliance took a Rs 3,261 crore impairment. It left American shale completely in November 2021. [157,158,159]
11.16 p: Be fair: one piece worked. The Eagle Ford midstream venture brought in about $1 billion on $254 million put in. And Reliance said its final sale was above the value on its books, after those write-downs. No public number gives a total loss, so we will not invent one. The lesson is simpler. A year before he praised the patient leopard, he had jumped down from the tree in the middle of someone else's excitement. [158,159,91]
11.17 h: Nineteen years in the regulator's shadow
11.18 p: In November 2007, Reliance sold about 5% of its subsidiary Reliance Petroleum. Before that, it signed agreements with 12 entities that took short positions in the futures market, with the profits flowing back to Reliance. In the last minutes of the settlement day, large share sales hit the market. In 2017 SEBI called this manipulation, ordered Reliance to give up Rs 447 crore plus 12% interest, and banned it from equity derivatives for a year. [160,161,162]
11.19 timeline: Mar 2017 | SEBI orders Rs 447 crore disgorgement and a one-year derivatives ban. Reliance serves the ban. [161,163]
11.19 timeline: Nov 2020 | Tribunal upholds SEBI by 2:1. Its chair dissents, saying fraud was not proved. [161,164]
11.19 timeline: Jan 2021 | SEBI fines Reliance Rs 25 crore and Mukesh Ambani personally Rs 15 crore. [165]
11.19 timeline: Dec 2023 | Tribunal quashes the Rs 15 crore fine on him. [166]
11.19 timeline: Nov 2024 | Supreme Court dismisses SEBI's appeal against that relief. “You can’t chase a person like this for years,” the bench says. [167]
11.19 timeline: May 2026 | Supreme Court overturns the fraud finding and the Rs 447 crore order, and orders Rs 250 crore refunded. [145,162]
11.20 p: So who won? Mostly Reliance. The Supreme Court held that the 12 agreements “were not a device used for fraud and manipulation”, and it said the tribunal made an “egregious error”. But the win was partial. The court agreed Reliance had broken disclosure rules on position limits and left the penalty in place. __Being cleared after 19 years is still 19 years.__ [145,168]
11.21 h: The deal he announced too early
11.22 p: In 2019 he told the world Saudi Aramco would buy 20% of Reliance's oil-to-chemicals business for about $15 billion, a major plank in his debt-cutting plan. It was a non-binding agreement. Two years of talks followed. On 19 November 2021 the companies said they would “re-evaluate”, and Reuters sources said valuation was the sticking point as the world moved away from fossil fuels. The shares fell 4.2%. [169,170]
11.23 p: He got away with it, because the Jio stake sales to Meta, Google and others made Reliance net-debt free anyway. But the Future Group deal showed the same weakness. Agreed in August 2020 for Rs 24,713 crore, it was attacked by Amazon in court and finally dead in April 2022, when Future's secured creditors voted it down. Big announcements and finished deals are two different things. [170,59]
11.24 h: When winning hurts others
11.25 p: Jio's free calls and cheap data were great for customers. For rivals, they were a brutal shock. Airtel filed a predatory pricing complaint, which the Competition Commission rejected in June 2017, because Jio held only 6.4% of subscribers and “providing free services cannot by itself raise competition concerns” without dominance. Legal, then. [171,172]
11.26 p: ET Telecom later reported smaller operators shutting and Idea and Vodafone merging. RCom, the company run by his own brother, was among the casualties. Then he helped keep that brother out of jail. [111]
11.27 p: A young founder should think hard about this. A strategy that needs about $25 billion of someone's cash pile is not one you can copy. And a market with very few survivors has its own price, which is more political attention than most founders would ever want. [96,135]
11.28 h: Family and governance
11.29 p: Critics also ask how a listed company picks its leaders. Proxy adviser IiAS urged shareholders to vote against Anant Ambani's 2023 board seat, because he was under 30. He won with 92.75% of the votes, against 98.21% for Isha and 98.05% for Akash. In 2025 IiAS and SES opposed his appointment as whole-time director, citing experience and unclear pay. Reliance replied that he had worked in the group since 2015. Promoters hold about half the votes, so ordinary resolutions like these tend to pass. [173,174]
11.30 p: One more case deserves careful wording. Anant's animal rescue centre, Vantara, faced complaints of smuggling and mistreatment. A Supreme Court-appointed team, led by a former judge, found no legal violation, and on 15 September 2025 the Court accepted that and closed the complaints. Weeks later, a report by the CITES Secretariat did not find imports without permits, but said India's due diligence had gaps and urged a pause on some imports. The Court cleared it, and an international body still had questions. [175,176,177]
11.31 note: Winning in court and winning public trust are separate exams.

=== C12 today | As of 2 November 2026 | Where He Stands Now
12.0 p: Mumbai, 28 October 2026. At the National Stock Exchange, the Ambani family gathers on stage. Before walking up to speak, Akash, the 35-year-old managing director of Jio Platforms, touches his parents' feet. All three children speak. Their father, 69, does not give a speech. He sits in the audience, next to Nita, and he is in tears. [87,178]
12.1 p: Isha reminded the room that Reliance's 1977 IPO raised Rs 2.82 crore. Jio's raised around ==Rs 30,213 crore==, the largest in India's history. The issue filled in 95 minutes and drew Rs 7.5 lakh crore of demand. Shares offered at Rs 1,119 closed the first day at Rs 1,417.50, and Jio became India's second most valuable listed company, behind only its parent. [139,178,87]
12.2 Q: Today, the bell at the Exchange will ring for me like a temple bell -- Anant Ambani, Jio listing ceremony, 28 October 2026 [139]
12.3 note: He built it for ten years. Then he let his kids hold the mic.
12.4 h: His job now
12.5 p: He remains Chairman and Managing Director of Reliance Industries. At the August 2023 AGM he committed to five more years, “with greater vigour”, and listed his three duties: groom next-generation leaders, mentor Akash, Isha and Anant so they can lead together, and enrich the culture. India Today read that as stepping down at about 71, around 2028–29. We found no published handover date. [126,106]
12.6 p: The split is already visible: Akash at Jio, Isha at retail, Anant in new energy and now an executive director. Back at Family Day in December 2021, he had described his duty in words any good teacher would recognise. [126,140,179]
12.7 Q: We should guide them, enable them, encourage them and empower them... and sit back and applaud as they perform better than us -- Mukesh Ambani, Reliance Family Day, December 2021 [179]
12.8 h: The numbers behind him
12.9 facts: Rs 11,75,919 crore | Reliance FY26 revenue ($124.0 billion), a record [123]
12.9 facts: Rs 95,754 crore | FY26 net profit, up 17.8% [123]
12.9 facts: Rs 2,07,911 crore | FY26 EBITDA, more than double the FY21 figure, as he promised [123]
12.9 facts: 540 million+ | Jio customers, per Akash Ambani [139]
12.10 h: The bets on the table
12.11 p: The first is artificial intelligence. On 19 February 2026, at the India AI Impact Summit in New Delhi, he pledged ==Rs 10 lakh crore over seven years==, about $110 billion. He made the same argument he made about data ten years ago: compute is scarce and costly, and “India cannot afford to rent intelligence.” Reuters noted the sum is modest beside what American giants plan. The first 120 megawatts at Jamnagar are due by the end of 2026, with Google and Meta as partners. [180,181,182,151]
12.12 p: The second is clean energy, which he calls Reliance's most ambitious generational undertaking. At the 2026 AGM, he reported nearly 1 GW of solar modules made at Jamnagar and a 40 GWh battery plant due this year, to be expanded to 120 GWh. He also described a renewable hub on 5,50,000 acres in Kutch and a $3 billion green ammonia contract with Samsung C&T. He admits it has been harder than any roadmap anticipated. [123]
12.13 Q: We ask for your continued trust. And we intend to earn it – quarter by quarter. -- Mukesh Ambani on new energy, 49th AGM, June 2026 [123]
12.14 p: The third is the old business. Retail and digital now make nearly half of EBITDA, which means oil, chemicals and everything else still make about half. Forbes blamed margin pressure at retail for part of his wealth decline this year, and Nikkei flagged volatility in the core oil business around the IPO. Reuters says the plan is to turn Reliance into an “everything company”. Rubio's tour of Jamnagar in October, which covered both conventional and new energy, shows the refinery still gets the world's attention. [123,183,184,87,140]
12.15 h: What he is worth, and why it is a bit strange
12.16 p: Forbes put him at ==$80.3 billion, ranked 25th in the world,== on 1 November 2026. Bloomberg put him at $77.3 billion and 24th, down about $27 billion from a year ago. So just after his greatest triumph, he was getting poorer on paper: on the morning after the listing, Forbes showed him as one of the day's biggest wealth losers. The Forbes India list, published in late September and counting family fortunes, placed Gautam Adani's family first at $112.7 billion and his second at $86.3 billion. [0,120,185,183]
12.17 p: Why does a record IPO not make him richer overnight? Reliance already owned Jio, and that value was already inside Reliance's share price. The Indian Express worked out a notional promoter holding of about $121 billion if you add Jio separately, and an analyst warned that this counts the same asset twice. __Headlines add, and accountants subtract.__ [186]
12.18 h: What he says he wants
12.19 p: In 2027, Reliance celebrates its golden jubilee. He told McKinsey he wants it serving India “even after completing 100 years”. In 2026, he told shareholders that Reliance's “best chapters lie ahead” and laid out the claim for the next decade: AI for all, in 22 languages, and energy India no longer has to import. [96,123,151]
12.20 Q: The world built its old energy on Middle Eastern oil. The world will now build its new energy on Indian sunshine. -- Mukesh Ambani, 49th AGM, June 2026 [123]
12.21 p: Three loose ends remain. The gas disputes are unresolved. The children have not yet been tested without him above them. And both AI and new energy are huge bets whose returns no one can yet see. He said at 59 that “as usual” he would prove the financial community wrong. At 69, he is making the same promise again, this time with his children holding the microphone. [154,103,181]

=== LESSONS
0 Aim at a billion people, not a billion dollars.: Dhirubhai told his son that starting a business to become a billionaire makes you an idiot. Jio's plan was to bring affordable data to all of India, and the fortune followed. Pick a problem that is big enough to pull talent and customers toward you.
1 Price the worst day before you bet.: Before Jio, Mukesh Ambani told his board the worst case: little return on their own money and a digitised India anyway. He was ready for that outcome before he spent a rupee. Never bet more than you can survive losing.
2 Write the knowledge down.: In the 1980s polyester business, experts carried plant secrets home in private notebooks, and he replaced them with open procedures. When tragedy hit Reliance's leadership in 1985–86, there was no panic because the plan did not live in one head. If your startup dies when one person quits, fix that first.
3 Put young people beside the best in the world.: Short of money, Reliance still paid DuPont $5 million and sat 25-year-olds next to its experts. It bought skill and spread it. Spend on learning from the best before you spend on anything that looks impressive.
4 Hire character. Train competence.: He told McKinsey character matters more than competence, because competence can be built. Decades of old friends like Manoj Modi and Anand Jain show what trust is worth at scale. Skills you can teach, and honesty you cannot.
5 Finish one bath before you start another.: He signed a Microsoft joint venture in the 1990s and walked away because there was “too much soap” still on him from chemicals. Reasonable people may say he missed a boom. Even so, turning down a good thing to finish the main thing is what most founders never manage.
6 Let the young overrule you.: A Yale student's complaint and a son's “Dad, you won’t get it” helped push him into Jio at a time when his own world ran on oil and voice calls. He says he is happy to be overruled. Find someone who can tell you that you are out of date, then actually listen.
7 Set a price that sounds impossible.: His rule was to build whatever America builds in dollars as if a dollar were worth Rs 10. It forces new design instead of small savings. Set the cost target first, and let it make you rethink everything.
8 Find your Manoj Modi.: Ambani's college classmate has spent decades turning his ideas into contracts, networks and stores. A visionary without an executor is just a talker. Look for the friend who enjoys the hard, boring work you avoid, and treat them like gold.
9 Put it on paper while you still like each other.: Dhirubhai left no will, and his sons fought for years, in public and in court. The war cost Mukesh his telecom business until 2010. Write down your shareholding and exit rules on the day your co-founder is still your best friend.
10 Do not celebrate a deal before it closes.: The $15 billion Aramco sale was announced in 2019, was non-binding, and was gone by 2021, while the Rs 24,713 crore Future deal died at the hands of creditors. He could survive both because Jio raised money elsewhere. You almost certainly could not, so keep a second path ready.
11 Promise only what you can actually deliver.: Reliance's KG-D6 plan promised 80 million cubic metres of gas a day, and output collapsed to under 10 within years. The promise is still costing lawyers and headlines, with a government claim above $30 billion being contested. Your forecast becomes your reputation the moment someone else relies on it.
12 Be careful buying into someone else's boom.: He praised the patient leopard in 2011, yet a year earlier he had rushed into American shale while the industry was bullish. A stake bought for $392 million later sold for $126 million. When everyone is shopping in the same aisle, slow down.
13 Respect the small rules.: Reliance's 2007 trades through 12 agents led to 19 years of cases, and the Supreme Court finally threw out the fraud finding in 2026. Yet the penalty for breaking disclosure rules stayed. A shortcut that saves a little today can follow you for two decades.
14 When family needs you, show up.: After 15 years of rivalry, he helped with Anil's Ericsson dues in 2019, a day before the deadline that could have sent his brother to jail. One report says it was an outright payment and not a loan. Businesses can be rebuilt, and many relationships cannot, so know which fight to end.
