
#### [0] origins | Ages 0–12 | The Boy Above the Dentist's Office
0.0 p: Mark Zuckerberg was born in ==1984== in White Plains, New York. He grew up nearby in Dobbs Ferry, a small village in Westchester County, New York. He was the second of four children and the only boy. {28,23,27}
0.1 p: His father, Edward, was a dentist. His mother, Karen Kempner, trained as a psychiatrist. The two met on a blind date while they were students in New York: Edward was studying dentistry at NYU and Karen was at Brooklyn College. They married in 1979 and moved to Dobbs Ferry in 1980. {27}
0.2 p: The four children arrived over five years. Randi was born in 1982, Mark in 1984, Donna in 1985 and Arielle in 1987. Randi later worked at Facebook as a marketing head, Donna became a classics scholar, and Arielle became a venture capitalist. {27,23}
0.3 note: Four kids. One house. And the family business was downstairs.
0.4 h: A dental clinic on the ground floor
0.5 p: Here is the detail that explains a lot. Edward did not commute to an office. He ran his dental practice from the ground floor of the family home, and Karen handled the business side of it. Patients came and went below while the kids grew up above. {27,26}
0.6 QUOTE: "I had the unique situation of practicing in my house." -- Edward Zuckerberg, Mark's father, 2016 {26}
0.7 p: His practice was called Painless Dr. Z, and its slogan was =="We cater to cowards."== Edward later wrote that he and Karen developed ways to treat patients who were scared of the dentist, and that Karen's psychiatry training shaped how he worked. A clinic built around nervous patients is, at heart, a business about understanding people. {23,27}
0.8 p: Edward also loved gadgets. He started computerizing his office in the mid-1980s, around the time Mark was born. "My kids all grew up around the office and were all exposed to computers," he told a Westchester radio station in 2011. {25,39}
0.9 example: For example: the family's early Atari 800 home computer "came with a disk for programming," Edward recalled. Mark also had a book on programming, but his father says that, in the end, Mark's skill was self-taught. {25,39}
0.10 photo: https://commons.wikimedia.org/wiki/File:Atari-800-Computer-FL.jpg :: An Atari 800, the kind of early home computer Edward Zuckerberg says he owned. His came with a programming disk.
0.11 h: A computer of his own
0.12 p: Mark got his own computer young. His father puts it at about age 8 or 9, around the same time his older sister Randi got one. Mark himself told the Harvard Crimson he started programming when he got his first computer in sixth grade. TIME reported that he began coding on a Quantex 486DX running Windows 3.1. {26,11,24}
0.13 p: The kids' computers and the clinic shared one internet line, so they competed for it. The office needed the connection for insurance claims, so the rule was half an hour per person, and the children's turn did not start until the office closed at 5 p.m. In 1996 Edward fixed it the expensive way: he wired the whole house and brought in a T-1 line, a fast business connection that he says cost about ==$150 to $200 a month==. {26}
0.14 note: A dad who paid for fast internet in 1996. That's a head start.
0.15 p: By about 12, his father says, Mark had full-time internet access and could talk to the other computers in the house. Randi, about two and a half years older, had been given a computer at around the same time as Mark. Edward's view was simple: "There are advantages to being exposed to computers early on." {26,25}
0.16 h: ZuckNet: the first network he built
0.17 p: Around age 12, Mark built a home network he called ZuckNet. It let the computers in the house and in the dental office send messages to each other. A professional did the physical wiring; Mark wrote the software that made the machines talk. {24,26}
0.18 example: For example: instead of shouting across the house, the front desk could send a message that a patient had arrived, and the hygienist could say she would be ready for an exam in five minutes. Edward says the office used ZuckNet this way in 1996. {26}
0.19 p: Edward likes to call ZuckNet =="the world's first social media network, theoretically."== That is a proud father talking, not a historian. But the real point is simpler. __Mark's first program was not a game or a homework tool. It was a way for people in one building to talk to each other.__ {26}
0.20 QUOTE: "He learned the meaning of people being social and interacting with each other at a very early age." -- Edward Zuckerberg on Mark, 2016 {26}
0.21 h: What he was like as a boy
0.22 p: Ask his father what young Mark was like and you get two words: =="strong-willed and relentless."== Edward added a line that every parent of a stubborn kid will recognise. {23}
0.23 QUOTE: "For Mark, if he asked for something, yes by itself would work, but no required much more." -- Edward Zuckerberg, to TIME, 2010 {23}
0.24 p: That stubbornness came with a playful streak. Mark and his older sister Randi liked pranks and home-made movies, as the next chapter shows. His father described him as a good student with "a special affinity for math and sciences," and as humble and quiet. {24,25,39}
0.25 note: Strong-willed and quiet. Not the loud kid. The relentless one.
0.26 p: Edward's own parenting rule was not to push his children down one path. He told callers on that 2011 radio show that the job of a parent is "to recognize what their strengths are and support their strengths," with a balance of work and play. For Mark, the strength was obvious early: computers. {25,39}
0.27 p: It also mattered that his parents put so much into their children's schooling. "My parents worked really hard to make sure me and my sisters could go to good schools," he has said. {27}
0.28 facts: (1984) Born in White Plains, New York; raised in Dobbs Ferry {28,23} | (4) Children in the family: Randi, Mark, Donna, Arielle {27} | (1996) House wired with a T-1 line; ZuckNet links home and clinic {26} | (~12) Mark's age when he built ZuckNet {24}
0.29 timeline: (1979) Edward Zuckerberg and Karen Kempner marry. {27} | (1980) The couple move to Dobbs Ferry, New York; Edward's dental clinic runs from their home. {27} | (1982) Older sister Randi is born. {27} | (1984) Mark is born in White Plains, New York. {28,27} | (Mid-1980s) Edward starts computerizing his dental office. {25,39} | (c. 1992–96) Mark gets his first computer (age 8–9 by his father's account, sixth grade by his own). {26,11} | (1996) The house gets a T-1 line; Mark's ZuckNet lets the home and clinic message each other. {26,24}
0.30 p: So by 12, the pattern was already set. A boy who lived above a busy clinic, surrounded by computers his father paid for, had written software whose whole purpose was to connect people. He did not yet know that this was a business. He just thought it was fun. {26,24}
0.31 p: There is an honest lesson here for any young reader. Mark's start was not a rags-to-riches story. He had two professional parents, a computer at home before most kids, and a father who treated technology as part of the family business. __Talent mattered, but so did access. If you lack the access, the fix is to find it: a library, a school lab, a friend's machine, a free online course.__ {27,26,25}
0.32 note: Lesson: notice what you build for fun. It's often your real talent.

#### [1] growing-up | Ages 13–18 | Games, Latin and a Music Robot
1.0 p: At 13, Mark had a Star Wars-themed bar mitzvah. It fits. His teenage years were a mix of science fiction, video games and building things on his computer late into the night. {24}
1.1 p: He and his sister Randi made their own movies. One winter break they filmed a home-made Star Wars parody they called ==The Star Wars Sill-ogy==. Their younger sister played the robot R2-D2. "We took our job very seriously," Randi told TIME. {24}
1.2 example: For example: on New Year's Eve 1999, when many adults feared the "Y2K bug" would crash computers at midnight, Mark, then 15, and Randi switched off the house's power right at midnight. Their parents were worried about exactly that. {24}
1.3 note: Fifteen, and already pranking adults about computers crashing.
1.4 h: Some kids played games. Mark wrote them.
1.5 p: Mark's real hobby was writing his own games. He wrote a computer version of Monopoly set at his middle school. He also wrote a version of the board game Risk set in the ancient Roman Empire, which he later called the first serious program he ever designed. {24,11}
1.6 QUOTE: "You played against Julius Caesar. He was good, and I was never able to win." -- Mark Zuckerberg, Harvard Crimson, 2004 {11}
1.7 p: How did he learn? His first formal lesson came from a book, ==C++ For Dummies==. But he told the Crimson that most of what he knew came from friends. __He learned by building things he wanted to use, and by asking people who knew more.__ {11}
1.8 note: No bootcamp. One 'Dummies' book and friends. Start with what you have.
1.9 p: He also had heroes. One childhood hero was Justin Frankel, the programmer who created the Winamp music player. Keep that name in mind. Mark's first famous project would be built as a Winamp plug-in. {33}
1.10 h: Outgrowing the local school
1.11 p: Mark went to Ardsley High School, the public school near home, until his junior year. He felt it was too small for what he wanted to learn. {11}
1.12 QUOTE: "[Ardsley] didn't have a lot of computer courses or a lot of the higher math courses." -- Mark Zuckerberg, Harvard Crimson, 2004 {11}
1.13 p: So at about 16 he transferred to Phillips Exeter Academy, an old and famous boarding school in New Hampshire. Here is the surprise. He did not pick Exeter for its computers. ==Its biggest selling point for him was the Latin program.== He even planned to study classics, the languages and history of ancient Greece and Rome, at Harvard before he changed course. {11}
1.14 photo: https://commons.wikimedia.org/wiki/File:Phillips_Exeter_Academy_building.jpg :: The Academy Building at Phillips Exeter Academy in New Hampshire. Mark transferred here for his last two years of school, drawn by its Latin program.
1.15 p: At Exeter he stood out for two things that have nothing to do with code: ancient languages and fencing. The love of Rome stuck. Remember, his first big game was set in the Roman Empire, and he played against Caesar. {24,11}
1.16 note: A coder who chose a school for Latin. Interests can mix.
1.17 p: The move itself is worth noticing. At 16, Mark decided his school was holding him back and left home for a tougher one. __When your environment runs out of things to teach you, changing it is a real decision, not a failure.__ {11}
1.18 h: Synapse: the music player that learned your taste
1.19 p: In his senior year at Exeter, Mark did an independent project with a classmate, Adam D'Angelo. Together they built ==Synapse==, a music player that watched what you listened to and learned your taste. Then it built playlists of songs you were likely to enjoy. They gave it away free as a plug-in for Winamp, the player made by Mark's hero. {11,10}
1.20 example: For example: you play a few songs you love, the software spots the pattern, and it lines up similar tracks for you. Two teenagers at a boarding school built that idea around 2002, using machine learning. {11,35}
1.21 p: In April 2003, when Mark was already a freshman at Harvard, the tech news site Slashdot posted about the project under the headline "Machine Learning and MP3s." Big companies noticed. TIME reported that AOL and Microsoft both tried to buy Synapse for around ==$1 million==. The Crimson reported that Winamp was interested too, and that a second offer reached about $2 million. {35,24,11,10}
1.22 QUOTE: "Some companies offered us right off the bat up to one million." -- Mark Zuckerberg, Harvard Crimson, 2004 {11}
1.23 p: His first account, in 2003, was simple. One company offered $950,000 if the pair would work for it for three years. "We wanted to go to college, so we said no," he told the Crimson. TIME later described it the same way: he would have had to drop out of school to develop the program, and he chose Harvard. {10,24}
1.24 p: Then came a twist. After they started college, the two decided they would sell after all. But by then the buyer had lost interest, and the deal was gone. __A hot offer has a short shelf life. If you say no, say no because you have a better plan, not because you expect the offer to wait.__ {11}
1.25 QUOTE: "We were pretty naïve about it." -- Mark Zuckerberg on the Synapse offers, 2004 {11}
1.26 note: Read this twice. He walked away from a million at 18.
1.27 p: He took a lesson from it. By 2004 he said he kept a lawyer for his products, so he would not be so naive again. He also kept Adam D'Angelo as a close friend. {11}
1.28 h: The email from Harvard
1.29 p: Near the end of his time at Exeter, the Harvard admissions email arrived. Mark later told Harvard's graduates exactly where he was that day. {13}
1.30 QUOTE: "I was playing Civilization and I ran downstairs, got my dad, and for some reason, his reaction was to video me opening the email." -- Mark Zuckerberg, Harvard Commencement, 2017 {13}
1.31 p: He got in. "That could have been a really sad video," he joked. He also said that getting into Harvard was still the thing his parents were most proud of him for, even after everything else he did. {13}
1.32 facts: (~16) Age when he left Ardsley High for Phillips Exeter Academy {11} | (Latin) His main reason for choosing Exeter {11} | (~$1M) Reported offers from AOL and Microsoft for Synapse {24,11} | (0) Dollars he made from Synapse after turning the offers down {11}
1.33 timeline: (c. 1997) Star Wars-themed bar mitzvah at about 13. {24} | (Middle school) Writes a Monopoly game set at his school and a Roman Empire version of Risk. {24,11} | (31 Dec 1999) Mark and Randi cut the house's power at midnight as a Y2K prank. {24} | (c. 2000) Leaves Ardsley High in junior year and moves to Phillips Exeter Academy. {11} | (2001–02) Senior-year project: Synapse music player with Adam D'Angelo. {11,10} | (Sept 2002) Synapse goes online as a free download; Mark starts at Harvard. {10,29} | (Apr 2003) Slashdot features Synapse. Offers arrive, and the pair say no. {35,10} | (Later) The pair decide to sell, but the buyer has lost interest. {11}
1.34 p: Look at the teenage Mark as a whole. He wasn't a lonely genius in a basement. He made movies with his sister, pranked his parents, fenced and read Latin. But one thread ran through all of it. He kept building things, giving them away, and watching how people used them. {24,11}
1.35 note: Lesson: ship small things often. Synapse got noticed because it was public.

#### [2] first-steps | Ages 18–20 | Harvard, a Prank and a Week of Code
2.0 p: Mark arrived at Harvard in the autumn of 2002 as a member of the class of 2006. He studied computer science. Fifteen years later, Harvard's student paper noted that his very first class there was CS 121, a computer science course taught by Harry Lewis, a former dean of the college. {29,28,34}
2.1 p: In his second year he lived in Kirkland House, one of Harvard's undergraduate houses. His dorm room there became the most famous dorm room in tech history. {33,13}
2.2 photo: https://commons.wikimedia.org/wiki/File:Kirkland_House.JPG :: Kirkland House at Harvard. Mark launched thefacebook.com from his dorm room here in February 2004.
2.3 h: Side project one: CourseMatch
2.4 p: At the start of his sophomore year, in the autumn of 2003, he built a site called CourseMatch. Students could choose to share which courses they were taking, so you could see who else was in your classes. {29,11}
2.5 p: It was a small tool. But it planted an idea: people care a lot about who else is around them. That idea was later folded straight into thefacebook.com. {11}
2.6 note: Small tools teach you what people really want. CourseMatch did.
2.7 p: CourseMatch was not alone. The Crimson later listed other Zuckerberg experiments from this period that never launched: a screensaver that showed your friends' AOL Instant Messenger away messages, and software to keep music in sync across computers. He even dreamed of getting all of Harvard to play the same song at the same moment. He never did it. {11}
2.8 p: Notice the theme. An away message is a tiny public status update. A shared song is a shared moment. Almost every one of these toys was about seeing what other people were doing, right now. {11}
2.9 h: Side project two: Facemash, and the trouble it caused
2.10 p: On a Tuesday night in late October 2003, Mark was bored. At ==8:13 p.m.== he started a new project and wrote about it as he went in an online journal. The idea copied a site called Hot or Not: show two students' photos side by side and let visitors vote on who was more attractive. {29,30}
2.11 p: To get the photos, he broke into the online student directories, called facebooks, that Harvard's houses kept. He started with Kirkland and went through the houses one by one. Just before 1 a.m. he posted, =="Let the hacking begin."== He called the hacking "child's play." By around 4 a.m. he had the photos, and the site was done just before 7:30 a.m. on Halloween. {29,31}
2.12 p: He sent the link to a few friends for feedback. It spread to mailing lists, including the Institute of Politics. Traffic more than quadrupled within hours. His computer slowed so badly that it was stuck on its screen saver. {30,29}
2.13 facts: (~450) Visitors to Facemash before it was shut down {29,31} | (22,000+) Votes cast on photos {29,31} | (3) Charges at the Ad Board: security, copyright, privacy {31} | (0) Days he was forced to leave Harvard {31}
2.14 p: The backlash was fast and fair. Student groups including Fuerza Latina and the Association of Black Harvard Women criticised the site, because students' photos were being used and ranked without their permission. Mark shut it down and emailed apologies to both groups. {29,31}
2.15 QUOTE: "I hope you understand, this is not how I meant for things to go, and I apologize for any harm done" -- Mark Zuckerberg, quoted in the Harvard Crimson, Nov 2003 {29}
2.16 p: Harvard's Administrative Board, its main disciplinary body, called him in. It accused him of breaching security, violating copyrights and violating individual privacy. The outcome: he was not forced to withdraw or leave school for any length of time. He would not say whether he got a smaller penalty. {31,34}
2.17 p: His own words at the time show both sides of him. He said his real interest was "the algorithms and math behind it." He also said, "Issues about violating people's privacy don't seem to be surmountable," and that he was "not willing to risk insulting anyone." __He was brilliant at building, and careless about the people inside what he built. That tension would follow him for twenty years.__ {29,31}
2.18 note: Very important: speed without consent creates enemies. Remember this one.
2.19 h: The going-away party
2.20 p: Facemash had an unexpected side effect. Everyone expected the worst from the Ad Board. As he tells it, his parents came to help him pack, and his friends threw him a going-away party. A student named Priscilla Chan was at that party with a friend. {13,34}
2.21 QUOTE: "I'm going to get kicked out in three days, so we need to go on a date quickly." -- Mark Zuckerberg, recalling meeting Priscilla Chan, Harvard Commencement 2017 {13}
2.22 p: He was not kicked out. Priscilla later became his wife, and he told Harvard's 2017 graduates she was the most important person in his life. He also pushed back on the Hollywood version of these months: the movie, he said, made it seem like Facemash was central to creating Facebook, and it wasn't. {13,34}
2.23 h: A gap nobody at Harvard was filling
2.24 p: Here is the business gap. In late 2003 Harvard had no single online directory of all its students. Each house kept its own restricted version. The university said it wanted a campus-wide one, but privacy worries had slowed it down, and Facemash had made those worries worse. In December an official admitted, "No staff or technical resources have been assigned yet." {32}
2.25 p: Meanwhile, three seniors, the twins Cameron and Tyler Winklevoss and Divya Narendra, asked Mark to help program their own site for Harvard students, later called ConnectU. He worked on it briefly, then left. Soon after, his own site launched. The three later sued, claiming he stole their idea; Mark denied it and called their claims baseless. {11,37}
2.26 example: For example of how that dispute ended: the two sides eventually signed a settlement in which the Winklevosses gave up ConnectU in exchange for cash and a piece of Facebook. When they later tried to undo it, a US appeals court refused in April 2011. {37,86}
2.27 h: One week of code
2.28 p: Early in 2004, Mark sat down to build the directory himself. He wanted something better than a phone book with faces. Students would make profiles, list their classes and interests, and connect with friends. He took inspiration from Friendster, an early social network. Money came from a classmate, Eduardo Saverin, who put in ==$1,000==, enough, they reckoned, to keep the site live for at least two months. {12,11,33}
2.29 p: He almost didn't launch it. He told the Crimson that if he hadn't launched it that day, he was ready to drop it and move on. Then, on ==4 February 2004==, a Wednesday, he put thefacebook.com live from his Kirkland House room. He was 19. {11,33,12,23}
2.30 QUOTE: "If I hadn't launched it that day, I was about to just can it and go on to the next thing I was about to do." -- Mark Zuckerberg, Harvard Crimson, 2004 {11}
2.31 p: That night he went to Noch's with his friend KX. He told KX he was excited to connect Harvard, but that one day someone would connect the whole world. It never crossed his mind that the someone might be them. "We were just college kids," he said later. {13}
2.32 note: He was 19, out with a friend, and didn't think he'd be the one.
2.33 p: Within days, more than ==650 students== had signed up, and he expected about 900 by the next morning. He told the Crimson he could build in a week what the university had not managed in years. He added privacy controls so users could choose who saw their profile, and promised he would not sell anyone's email address. {12}
2.34 QUOTE: "I think it's kind of silly that it would take the University a couple of years to get around to it. I can do it better than they can, and I can do it in a week." -- Mark Zuckerberg, Harvard Crimson, 9 Feb 2004 {12}
2.35 p: He also explained, in one sentence, why the site would grow on its own: "The nature of the site is that each user's experience improves if they can get their friends to join it." __That is the network effect, described by a 19-year-old five days after launch.__ Every new user made the site more useful for everyone already on it. {12}
2.36 example: For example: by June 2004, four months after launch, thefacebook.com had almost 160,000 members at colleges across the US. His roommate Dustin Moskovitz helped him open it to more schools. {11,33}
2.37 h: Not a company yet
2.38 p: In those first months Mark kept saying he was not in it for money. A few ads ran on the site, he said, only because "servers don't grow on trees." When the Crimson asked about his ambitions, he gave a very student answer: "My goal is to not have a job." Eight years later, his letter to Facebook's first public investors began: "Facebook was not originally created to be a company." {11,36}
2.39 p: One odd footnote: the Crimson reported that Mark got a C+ in a Harvard course on artificial intelligence. And TIME reported that one of his AI instructors at Harvard was Andrew "Boz" Bosworth, who later joined Facebook and rose to become one of its top engineering leaders. {33,23}
2.40 note: A C+ in AI. Grades are not destiny.
2.41 h: The summer that changed the plan
2.42 p: In June 2004 Mark and a few friends rented a summer sublet in Palo Alto, California, near Stanford and the big venture capital firms. The plan was a summer of work on their projects, with school due to start again in the autumn. Silicon Valley had a pull on him. "Palo Alto was kind of like this mythical place where all the techs used to come from," he said. {33,38}
2.43 p: He never went back to class. Looking back, he said it was "never a formal decision"; the team simply did not return. Harvard's most famous dropout had just begun. Years later he was honest about the safety net that made the risk possible, as you will read in the chapter on his beliefs. {33,38}
2.44 photo: https://commons.wikimedia.org/wiki/File:MarkZuckerberg.jpg :: Mark Zuckerberg in November 2005, aged 21, about a year after the summer move to California.
2.45 timeline: (Autumn 2002) Starts at Harvard, class of 2006; first class is CS 121. {29,34} | (Autumn 2003) Builds CourseMatch at the start of sophomore year. {29} | (Late Oct 2003) Builds Facemash overnight; shuts it down after protests. {29,30} | (Nov 2003) Ad Board hearing on security, copyright and privacy; he is not forced to leave. {31} | (Dec 2003) Harvard's own campus-wide directory is stalled. {32} | (4 Feb 2004) Launches thefacebook.com from Kirkland House, aged 19. {33,11} | (Jun 2004) Moves to Palo Alto for the summer; never returns to classes. {33,38}
2.46 p: Put these years together and a clear pattern shows. Mark did not have one lucky idea. He built ZuckNet, games, Synapse, CourseMatch and Facemash before Facebook, and each one taught him something about people and software. The real company, and the real fights, were about to begin. {24,26,11,29}
2.47 note: Six projects before the big one. Keep building.

#### [3] every-company | Every venture, win or lose | Everything He Ever Built
3.0 p: Most people think Mark Zuckerberg built one thing. He actually built, bought or bet on about twenty. The list includes a music plug-in from high school, a hot-or-not site that got him hauled before a Harvard discipline board, a file-sharing tool, an ad system that told your friends what you bought, a phone launcher with terrible reviews, a solar drone, a digital currency and a personal AI agent. {11,31,41,54,57,60,77}
3.1 p: This chapter goes through them in order. For each one you get the problem, the decision, where the first money came from and how it ended. __Notice how often the flops and the giant wins come from the same habit: building fast, shipping early and buying what he could not build.__ {36,47,50,73}
3.2 note: Count the failures. Then count the wins. Both lists are long.
3.3 VENTURE Synapse Media Player [2002–2003 (built in high school)] failed
  idea: A plug-in for the popular Winamp music player that learned what you liked to listen to and built matching playlists for you. In plain words, it was a tiny, early version of the "recommended for you" idea that later ran Spotify and YouTube.
  decision: Mark built it with his school friend Adam D'Angelo as an independent project, and they put it online for free instead of selling it. When companies came knocking, the two first said no, then later changed their minds.
  how: Two teenagers, a school project and a free download. A link on the tech site Slashdot brought a wave of attention.
  funding: No outside money at all. It was built with their own time and released free. The two did split the $12,000 cost of a patent, sure that future profits would cover it.
  what: After the Slashdot link, AOL, WinAmp and Microsoft all showed interest. In 2003 Mark said one company had offered $950,000 plus a three-year job. In 2004 he recalled offers of up to about $1 million, then one of about $2 million. By his own account they decided to accept an offer only after reaching college, and by then the buyer had lost interest. He told The Crimson he had been naive and now kept lawyers for his products.
  lesson: Interest is not an offer, and an offer is not money in the bank. When a real buyer is at the table, decide quickly or be ready to walk away for good. {10,11}
3.4 VENTURE CourseMatch [2003 (Harvard)] merged
  idea: A small website that let Harvard students see who else was taking the same courses, so they could pick classes with friends.
  decision: He built it because the information already existed but nobody had connected it. The idea of linking people through their classes later became one of the first features of thefacebook.
  how: Built alone in his dorm during his sophomore year.
  funding: None. It was a student side project.
  what: CourseMatch did not become a company. Its core idea was folded into thefacebook.com a few months later, where profiles listed the classes you were taking.
  lesson: Small tools are cheap experiments. Keep the part people loved and move it into your next product. {11}
3.5 VENTURE Facemash [Oct–Nov 2003] shut
  idea: A copy of the site hotornot.com for Harvard. It showed two students' photos side by side and asked visitors to vote on who was "hotter".
  decision: He built it over the last week of October 2003, pulling ID photos from the online "facebooks" of nine Harvard houses, partly by hacking into house websites. He did not ask anyone's permission.
  how: One student and less than a week of work. The link spread through campus email lists and traffic quadrupled in a day.
  funding: None.
  what: By 10 p.m. on its busiest day it had 450 visitors who cast at least 22,000 votes. Student groups, including Fuerza Latina and the Association of Black Harvard Women, criticised it on their email lists. He took it down, and was then called before Harvard's Administrative Board on charges of breaching security, violating copyrights and violating individual privacy. He was allowed to stay at Harvard; he would not say if he got a lesser punishment.
  lesson: Growth that hurts people is not growth. He saw how fast a social site could spread, but he also learned that privacy mistakes follow you. {31,11,29}
3.6 note: The privacy theme starts here, in 2003. It never goes away.
3.7 VENTURE Thefacebook / Facebook / Meta Platforms [2004–today] public
  idea: An online directory of Harvard students with profiles, photos, interests, classes and relationship status, so you could look up and connect with people you actually knew. Harvard was planning its own version, and Mark thought it would take the university years.
  decision: The site was built around real people you already knew, not strangers. It grew college by college instead of opening to everyone at once. Early on he resisted venture capital and turned down buyers, including a $1 billion offer from Yahoo in 2006.
  how: He launched it from his room in Kirkland House on 4 February 2004 and said he built it in about a week. More than 650 students signed up within days. #### [7] how-they-work | How he runs it | Thirty People, No One-on-Ones
7.0 p: Most mornings Mark Zuckerberg wakes between ==7:00 and 7:30 a.m.==, usually because one of his three daughters has woken him. He reaches for his phone straight away, and he says the news on it is mostly bad. Then he does something few chief executives do. He goes and fights. {88}
7.1 QUOTE: "I compose myself and go fight for two hours, recenter myself, and it's like, now I can go deal with this stuff." -- Mark Zuckerberg, on Theo Von's podcast, 2025 {88}
7.2 p: It started as a way to quiet his head. He told Joe Rogan he used to run, "but the problem with running is you can think a lot." So he moved to jiu-jitsu and mixed martial arts, where a wandering mind gets you choked. He also told Rogan why the phone hurts so much first thing: "People reserve the good stuff to tell me in person." {89}
7.3 note: Find the one hour when your brain cannot check the phone.
7.4 p: The rest of the day has been described in pieces over the years. In a 2023 profile he aimed for eight hours of sleep and said "I don't stay up super late at night." Priscilla Chan has said he tucks the children in unless he is travelling or has a board meeting. He told Lex Fridman he goes through what matters most in life with his daughters: health, friends, family and something to look forward to. {89}
7.5 h: Clearing out the small decisions
7.6 p: In November 2014, at Facebook's first public town hall, someone asked why he wore the same gray T-shirt every day. His answer became one of the most copied habits in the startup world. Quartz pointed out the idea behind it, which psychologists call decision fatigue: even small choices, like breakfast or clothes, use up energy. {90}
7.7 QUOTE: "I really want to clear my life to make it so that I have to make as few decisions as possible about anything except how to best serve the community." -- Mark Zuckerberg, Facebook town hall, November 2014 {90}
7.8 h: The "small group"
7.9 p: Meta has tens of thousands of staff, but Mark directly oversees only ==25 to 30 people==. He calls them the "small group", and he knows the name is a joke. He described them to Stripe's John Collison in May 2025 as a "core army that can go get anything done across the company". He spends real time making sure they are connected to each other and all know what is going on. {91,78}
7.10 p: Here is the surprising part. He has no recurring one-on-one meetings with any of them. He does keep only two standing weekly meetings with the group: an open-ended strategy talk and an operating review of company priorities. __The rest of his calendar stays loose on purpose, so he can jump on whatever matters that day.__ {91}
7.11 QUOTE: "I think if you're going to report to me, you need to be able to manage yourself." -- Mark Zuckerberg, Stripe Sessions, May 2025 {91}
7.12 p: He was careful about how far this goes. "I talk to all these people constantly, more than they want to talk to me, I'm sure," he said. He also said that for the rest of the company, management is important and is how people grow. So this rule is for one room, not the whole building. {91}
7.13 note: No one-on-ones only works if every person can manage themselves.
7.14 facts: (25–30) people who report directly to him {91} | (2) standing weekly meetings with that group {91} | (~15) product divisions under them {91} | (66,775) Meta employees, 30 September 2026 {78}
7.15 example: For example: he told Forbes, "For every hour of meeting that I have, the team sends out the pre-reads in advance." He wants at least an hour to prepare and another hour to follow up. So one hour in a room can cost him three hours of his day, which is why he keeps so few standing meetings. {89}
7.16 h: Running a company with memos and open questions
7.17 p: The best description of how he wanted Facebook to work is a document he wrote at 27: the letter in the 2012 IPO filing, which he called "The Hacker Way". He explained why the company holds hackathons every few months, and said Timeline, chat and video all came out of them. He also made every new engineer go through a training program called Bootcamp, even managers whose main job was not to write code. Investors were reading a stock offering, and it told them to expect engineers to win arguments by building things. {92}
7.18 p: Since then, the big decisions have reached staff as written memos. There was the November 2022 layoff message, the March 2023 "Year of Efficiency" note, the January 2025 note on low performers, and a June 2025 memo naming the first 11 people in his new AI lab. Several were leaked or obtained by reporters soon after, so anything he writes to staff can end up in the news. {72,73,93,94}
7.19 p: He also takes questions directly from employees. In 2019 The Verge published two hours of leaked audio from two internal meetings in July of that year. Staff asked him how worried he was about being broken up and what the plan was against TikTok, and he answered in plain, unpolished language. __You learn more about a founder from the questions his own people dare to ask than from any keynote.__ {95}
7.20 note: Would your team dare to ask you the hard question?
7.21 p: Debate inside can get rough. In 2016 a senior executive, Andrew Bosworth, posted an internal memo called "The Ugly" arguing that "all the work we do in growth is justified", even if people died because of what happened on the platform. When it leaked in 2018, Bosworth said he had written it to provoke debate and did not agree with it. Mark called him a "talented leader who says many provocative things", and called the memo something "that most people at Facebook including myself disagreed with strongly". {96}
7.22 h: Flatter is faster
7.23 p: In March 2023, after laying off thousands, Mark set out a rule for how the company should be shaped. He removed several layers of management and asked many managers to go back to hands-on work. He also said no manager should have more than 10 direct reports, since many had only a few. His reasoning fits in one sentence: "every layer of a hierarchy adds latency and risk aversion in information flow and decision-making." {73}
7.24 QUOTE: "In retrospect, I underestimated the indirect costs of lower priority projects." -- Mark Zuckerberg, "Update on Meta's Year of Efficiency", March 2023 {73}
7.25 note: Very important for first-time founders! Side projects cost more than their budget.
7.26 h: How he hires
7.27 p: At Mobile World Congress in Barcelona in 2015, he gave his hiring test in one line. At the same event he said the top priority is to keep a team as small as you can, and pointed out that Facebook served more than a billion people with fewer than 10,000 staff. He also warned the room that "Facebook is not a company for everyone in the world." {97}
7.28 QUOTE: "I will only hire someone to work directly for me if I would work for that person." -- Mark Zuckerberg, Mobile World Congress, 2015 {97}
7.29 p: He told Sam Altman in 2016 that Facebook looks for raw talent and is willing to hand people jobs they have never done before. The early days could be brutal. Noah Kagan, who joined in 2005, later described what it was like in a public post: "My boss was fired the day I started. My next boss was fired a month later. I got fired in 9 months." That is one former employee's memory, and he added the reason as he saw it: "Mark was intense about keeping A players only." {98,99}
7.30 note: Fired after nine months. Still telling the story nearly twenty years later.
7.31 p: In 2025 the most powerful person at Meta became its top recruiter. Bloomberg reported he was frustrated by Meta's AI shortfalls and was meeting researchers at his own homes, aiming to build a team of about 50. Reports described a target list, and said he often sent the first message himself. He also rearranged the office so the new hires would sit close to him. {100,94,101,175}
7.32 p: Rivals noticed. Sam Altman said Meta had dangled $100 million signing bonuses at OpenAI staff. OpenAI's research chief, Mark Chen, wrote in a leaked memo: "I feel a visceral feeling right now, as if someone has broken into our home and stolen something." Whether huge offers build a great team is still open. One critic quoted by Fortune asked what happens when someone quits after a week and whether they would have to return the bonus. {94}
7.33 h: How he fires
7.34 p: In January 2025 he told staff to expect an "intense year" and said he had "decided to raise the bar on performance management and move out low performers faster". Meta planned to exit roughly ==5%== of its lowest performers and fill those roles again. Fortune noted the difference from earlier rounds: this one was about performance, not cost. The company promised what it called generous severance. {93}
7.35 note: Letting people go is part of the job. Doing it decently is too.
7.36 h: How big calls get made
7.37 p: For everyday product choices, he says, the answer is to get out of the way. He told Altman that engineers were running tens of thousands of versions of Facebook at any moment, each shown to perhaps 10,000 or 100,000 people, with results compared to the normal version. The company could not move that fast, he said, "if every change had to be approved by me". His method is data and user feedback to pick the problem, then intuition to guess a solution, then a test. {98}
7.38 p: For the biggest choices, he decides, and he is hard to move. In 2012 Facebook was so weak on phones that its IPO filing warned it made no meaningful mobile revenue. Nicola Mendelsohn, Meta's global head of advertising, later told a story from that period. When product teams kept working on the desktop site, she said, he had no meetings with them for two weeks because nobody had mobile work to show him. {102,103}
7.39 example: For example: mobile was about 25% of Facebook's revenue at the end of 2012 and about 50% at the end of 2013. In 2015, Fortune reported, mobile brought in more than $4 billion and 80% of advertising revenue, less than four years after that warning to investors. {102}
7.40 note: Silence can be a very loud instruction.
7.41 h: What it is like to work for him
7.42 p: The people around him describe very different bosses. Mendelsohn called him "bold" and "an extraordinary human being", and said he is "always right", which is high praise and also a small warning. Brian Acton, the WhatsApp co-founder, walked away from about $850 million in unvested stock after disagreeing over how to make money from WhatsApp. Instagram's founders left in 2018 too, though Kevin Systrom told WSJ Magazine there were no "philosophical misalignments". {103,104}
7.43 p: Some accounts are harsher. Yann LeCun, Meta's departing chief AI scientist, told the Financial Times that after the Llama 4 AI model's published test results were, in his words, "fudged a little bit", Mark "basically lost confidence in everyone who was involved" and sidelined the whole group. Former product manager Antonio Garcia Martinez wrote in his book that during a 2011 company lockdown Mark seemed "aloof and disconnected". These are one-sided accounts from people who left, so read them that way. {105,106}
7.44 p: Taken together, the on-record stories point one way. You get real freedom on small things: you can test an idea on 100,000 people without his approval. On the few things he cares about most, he decides, and he holds the votes to make it stick. __Founders often copy the speed and forget that it depends on hiring people who need no managing.__ {98,91}
7.45 note: Great place to learn. Hard place to win an argument about the big calls.

#### [8] mindset | What he believes | Eight Beliefs, Eight Stories
8.0 p: Mark rarely explains his beliefs in the abstract. He tells stories, usually about something that hurt. Here are eight ideas he keeps returning to, each with the moment that shows it in real life. Some of them made him rich. A couple of them also landed him in court. {13,98}
8.1 h: 1. Start with the problem, not the company
8.2 p: On the night he launched the first version of Facebook, he went to Noch's with his friend KX. He remembers saying that someone would one day connect the whole world. "The thing is, it never even occurred to me that someone might be us," he said at Harvard in 2017. He assumed one of the big technology companies would do it. {13}
8.3 p: So when young founders ask for advice, he tells them to do it in that order. He told Sam Altman it feels "really backwards" that people decide to start a company before deciding what they want to do. He even told Peter Thiel, Facebook's first outside investor, that he and Dustin Moskovitz planned to go back to school. They took one semester off, then another, and never returned. {98,107}
8.4 QUOTE: "The most important thing that entrepreneurs should do is pick something they care about, work on it, but don't actually commit to turning it into a company until it actually works." -- Mark Zuckerberg, to Sam Altman, 2016 {107}
8.5 note: Read this twice if you have a pitch deck but no product.
8.6 h: 2. Say the mission out loud
8.7 p: When Yahoo offered ==$1 billion== in 2006, Mark did not want to sell. Nearly everyone else did. An advisor told him that if he refused he would regret it for the rest of his life. He refused, and within about a year every person on the management team had gone. {13,98}
8.8 QUOTE: "I believed in what we were doing, but I felt alone. And worse, it was my fault." -- Mark Zuckerberg, Harvard Commencement, 2017 {13}
8.9 p: Why his fault? He told Altman that turning down the money was not the painful part. __The painful part was that he had never explained the mission, so people who joined expecting a big payday had every right to be angry.__ He was 22, and he wondered whether he was "an imposter". The lesson he took is that purpose has to be said out loud, because nobody can read it off your face. {98,13}
8.10 p: In that same rough patch he took some odd advice. In 2015, with Prime Minister Narendra Modi beside him on stage, he said Steve Jobs had told him to visit a temple in India that Jobs himself had gone to early in Apple's life. Mark went, and spent almost a month travelling around the country. He said seeing how people connected there reinforced why the work mattered. {108}
8.11 p: By the IPO he had turned this idea into a one-line creed. His 2012 letter told investors that the company did not wake up each morning with making money as its main goal. Readers can judge for themselves how that promise has held up, and our chapter on what went wrong gives you the evidence. Still, the line tells you how he wants to be understood. {92}
8.12 QUOTE: "Simply put: we don't build services to make money; we make money to build better services." -- Mark Zuckerberg, letter to investors, 2012 {92}
8.13 h: 3. Shipping beats arguing
8.14 p: The 2012 letter says Facebook had "Done is better than perfect" painted on its walls. It describes a mantra you would hear around the offices, "Code wins arguments", which meant that instead of debating for days you built a prototype and looked at it. The point was that the best idea should win, not the person who lobbies best or manages the most people. Five years later he gave students the same idea in plainer words. {92,13}
8.15 QUOTE: "Ideas don't come out fully formed. They only become clear as you work on them. You just have to get started." -- Mark Zuckerberg, Harvard Commencement, 2017 {13}
8.16 p: But he changed the most famous line of all. The letter said: "Move fast and break things." In April 2014 he stood on stage at the F8 developer conference and joked that the motto was now "Move Fast With Stable Infra", meaning stable infrastructure. He also promised outside developers a two-year guarantee that core tools would not break without warning. __A rule that helps at 10 people can hurt thousands of developers who rely on you.__ {92,109}
8.17 note: Your rules should change when your company size changes.
8.18 h: 4. A company is a learning machine
8.19 p: Altman asked him how Facebook keeps innovating. His answer: "companies are learning organisms and you can make decisions that either make it so that you learn faster or you learn slower." He compares building a company to the scientific method, where you try hypotheses and let the experiments teach you. Even News Feed was less of a leap than it looked, he said. People were already clicking through hundreds of profiles to see what had changed, so the product just saved them the clicks. {98}
8.20 p: He is honest about when this breaks. He said Facebook paid a lot for Oculus partly because it had not built that expertise inside earlier. Big dramatic moves, in his view, are often a sign that you stopped learning a few years ago. Yet he also says it is better to make the big move than to let pride stop you. {98,107}
8.21 QUOTE: "I actually think when you do things well, you shouldn't have to do big crazy things." -- Mark Zuckerberg, to Sam Altman, 2016 {107}
8.22 h: 5. Not taking risks is the real risk
8.23 p: Altman asked him for the best advice Peter Thiel ever gave him, and the answer is below. A version of it also appears in his IPO letter as a company saying: "The riskiest thing is to take no risks." He wrote that the company encourages bold decisions "even if that means being wrong some of the time." {107,92}
8.24 QUOTE: "In a world that's changing so quickly, the biggest risk you can take is not taking any risk." -- Mark Zuckerberg, recalling Peter Thiel's advice, 2016 {107}
8.25 p: He has lived by it at a scale that scares people. Reality Labs, the virtual reality unit, lost $10.2 billion in 2021 and $13.72 billion in 2022, and he renamed the whole company for that bet. Asked in 2024 whether he regretted the name, he said only: "Meta is a good name." Then he made an even bigger bet on AI. {67,68,110,78}
8.26 note: He can bet like this because no one can outvote him. You can't.
8.27 h: 6. Treat competition like war
8.28 p: Former Facebook product manager Antonio Garcia Martinez has described the day Google+ launched in 2011. He says it set off a "lockdown", which his book calls a "state of war that dated to Facebook's earliest days", during which nobody could leave the building. Mark finished his speech with a Latin line from ancient Rome: "Carthago delenda est", meaning Carthage must be destroyed. That is one man's account, but Mark's love of Roman history is well known. {106,111}
8.29 p: The same instinct drove his deals. Emails shown at the FTC trial in 2025 had him agreeing, in 2012, that buying Instagram might help "neutralise a competitor". He told the court the real question had been whether to build a better camera or buy one. And in one line from those emails he said he wanted to use acquisitions to protect the business. {112,113,114}
8.30 QUOTE: "use M&A to build a competitive moat around us on mobile and ads" -- Mark Zuckerberg, email shown at the FTC trial, as reported by the Financial Times {112}
8.31 p: He ended up winning that case, as you will read later. But note how he thinks: he told the court Instagram "was better, so I thought it was better to buy them." Most founders try hard not to admit a rival is better. He admits it and then acts. {113,114,115}
8.32 h: 7. Keep the votes
8.33 p: Asked in a 2019 internal meeting about Senator Elizabeth Warren's plan to break up Facebook, he bet that a legal challenge would follow and that Facebook would win it. He said the fight would still "suck" for the company, and he did not want a major lawsuit against his own government. Then came the line reporters quoted for years. {95}
8.34 QUOTE: "But look, at the end of the day, if someone's going to try to threaten something that existential, you go to the mat and you fight." -- Mark Zuckerberg, leaked internal Q&A, July 2019 {95}
8.35 p: His control is the reason he can talk this way. As of October 2026 he held about 13.4% of Meta's economic value but 60.6% of the votes. A filing that month says a special board committee first proposed a date when his extra voting power would end, and he would not accept it. __Control let him bet billions on ideas that outsiders mocked. It also means nobody inside can stop him when he is wrong.__ {80}
8.36 p: Whistleblower Frances Haugen put that downside bluntly to US senators in 2021. "In the end, the buck stops with Mark," she said. "There is no one currently holding Mark accountable but himself." {170}
8.37 h: 8. Luck is real
8.38 p: This may be the belief that readers in India should hold on to most. In the same Harvard speech he reminded students that Facebook was not the first thing he built. He had also made games, chat systems, study tools and music players. And he said what many rich founders never say: that he could afford to fail. {13}
8.39 QUOTE: "If I had to support my family growing up instead of having time to code, if I didn't know I'd be fine if Facebook didn't work out, I wouldn't be standing here today." -- Mark Zuckerberg, Harvard Commencement, 2017 {13}
8.40 note: If you don't have that cushion, build yours first. No shame in it.

9.15 h: Giving it away: the $100 million lesson

9.19 h: The 99% pledge
9.20 p: When their first daughter, Max, was born in December 2015, the couple published a letter to her and pledged ==99%== of their Facebook shares, then worth about $45 billion, during their lifetimes. The first wave of praise turned to questions when reporters saw that the money would go through the Chan Zuckerberg Initiative, a limited liability company, rather than a charity. #### [11] what-went-wrong | The hard chapter | What Not to Copy
11.0 p: Much of what follows is serious, and some of it involves real harm to real people. We have tried to say exactly what was proved, what was settled and what is still under appeal. Mark and Meta dispute much of it, and we say that too. Read this as the most expensive business education you will ever get for free. {84,115}
11.1 h: The wrong bet on phones
11.2 p: For about two years Facebook tried to build its mobile apps with HTML5, a web technology, instead of writing proper apps for each phone. The apps could not reach the quality people expected. In September 2012 he stood up at TechCrunch Disrupt and called it "one of the biggest mistakes if not the biggest strategic mistakes we've ever made". He then fixed it, and as you read earlier, mobile became 80% of advertising revenue by 2015. {147,148,102}
11.3 note: Copy this one: say it plainly, then change course fast.
11.4 h: "A pretty crazy idea"
11.5 p: Two days after the 2016 US election he told a conference that the notion of fake news on Facebook swaying the vote was "a pretty crazy idea". Investigations into Russian operations made the remark look badly judged. Ten months later he took it back: "Calling that crazy was dismissive and I regret it." By April 2018 he was telling the Senate that "One of my greatest regrets in running the company is that we were slow in identifying the Russian information operations in 2016." {149,150,134}
11.6 h: Myanmar
11.7 p: This is the most serious chapter in Facebook's history. In March 2018, UN investigators said social media had played a "determining role" in the crisis in Myanmar, where hundreds of thousands of Rohingya Muslims fled military violence. A report that Facebook itself commissioned, from the nonprofit Business for Social Responsibility, found the platform had been used to spread hate tied to offline violence. The company's response was written by a product policy manager: "prior to this year, we weren't doing enough to help prevent our platform from being used to foment division and incite offline violence." {151}
11.8 note: Launching in a new country means you are responsible there too.
11.9 h: Cambridge Analytica and the $5 billion question
11.10 p: In 2018 it emerged that data on about 87 million people had ended up with a political consulting firm. The detail to remember is how small the original mistake was. Facebook heard back from the firm that it was not using the data and had deleted it. He told senators, "we considered it a closed case. In retrospect, that was clearly a mistake. We shouldn't have taken their word for it." {134}
11.11 p: The bill took years to arrive. The FTC fined Facebook $5 billion, saying it had broken its earlier privacy agreement. Shareholders then sued him and other directors in Delaware for more than $8 billion, alleging that the deal shielded him personally. A former director testified the company did not pay to protect him, and the case settled just before the second day of trial in July 2025, on undisclosed terms and with no ruling on who was at fault. __One law professor said what was lost: a full public account of how it happened.__ {152}
11.12 p: It was not over even then. In late September 2026 a New Mexico jury found that Facebook had broken state law almost 44 million times with misleading statements. One category covered its commitment to investigate thoroughly after Cambridge Analytica. In 2018 he had told senators, "We're investigating every single app that had access to a large amount of information in the past." We cannot say that exact sentence was one of those counted, but it shows why promises made in a crisis matter. {84,134}

11.32 h: What a young founder should NOT copy
11.33 p: Do not copy the habit of fixing privacy problems only after they blow up. Each shortcut came back with interest, sometimes over a decade later.
