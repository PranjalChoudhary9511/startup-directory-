# 04 — Craft Research: Story, Retention, Visuals, Voice

**Track 1: "Everyone Saw It. Few Decoded It."** (Free flagship, 15–20 min, Hinglish/English, host on camera with motion graphics, B-roll, photos and captions)
**Stories:** Tata Nano (primary; tribute to Ratan Tata) → Airbnb (short) → Amul / Verghese Kurien (hopeful climax)
**Framework:** The Problem Iceberg: (1) what people say, (2) what people do (the workaround), (3) why it really happens (the root cause), (4) what people actually want (the hidden desire: status, trust, dignity, control, time)

## A. Retention rules for a 15–20 minute video

### A1. The 25 rules

**Foundation**

1. **The story is the retention hack. Editing tricks are not.** Paddy Galloway, the strategist behind many of the biggest channels, puts it bluntly: everything traces back to the story, and fast cutting cannot rescue a weak idea [S1]. *Apply:* lock the Iceberg argument and the three story spines (Rule 11) before you think about B-roll.
2. **Pre-production matters more than production.** In the edX study of 6.9 million viewing sessions, producers said planning had the largest effect on engagement [S2]. Johnny Harris spends about 3–4 weeks of researcher time and a 60–80 page research digest per video before a 3–4 day writing sprint [S3]. *Apply:* budget about 60% of the effort for research, structure and visual scripting, and about 40% for the shoot and edit.
3. **Treat the first 60 seconds as the most important minute.** The leaked MrBeast production guide (2024; its authenticity was never confirmed, and MrBeast's team declined to comment) says the first minute is where most viewers leave, so it deserves the most planning [S4][S5]. *Apply:* script the first 60 seconds word by word, shoot several takes, and edit it last, once you know the whole video.
4. **Confirm the click within 5–15 seconds.** Viewers leave when the opening does not match what the title and thumbnail promised [S4]. Galloway's intro rules are to restate the title/thumbnail promise, go straight into a storyline, cut context, put the best clip first, set stakes, and make sure it doesn't feel like an intro [S6]. *Apply:* no logo sting, no "Hi guys, welcome back," and no channel pitch. Start inside the story, for example on Nano launch day.
5. **Open on a misconception and say it out loud.** Derek Muller's (Veritasium) PhD research found that clear, polished science explainers produced almost no learning. Students felt more confident in their wrong ideas. Videos that first stated the common misconception and then took it apart roughly doubled post-test scores [S7][S8]. *Apply:* say the belief everyone holds ("Sab kehte hain Nano isliye fail hui kyunki woh sasti thi"), let it sit for a moment, then break it.
6. **Build a curiosity gap the viewer can close.** Loewenstein's information-gap theory says curiosity is a feeling of deprivation that comes from noticing a specific gap in what you know. It needs some prior knowledge, and the gap must feel bridgeable [S9][S10]. *Apply:* the Nano is perfect for this, because everyone has half-knowledge of it. Frame the gap precisely: "The right problem. The right man. So why did it fail?" Don't ask "Want to learn about innovation?"
7. **Use "but" and "therefore" between beats, never "and then."** Trey Parker and Matt Stone's rule (NYU guest lecture, usually dated 2011) is that if "and then" fits between two beats, the story is boring. Each beat should cause the next one ("therefore") or reverse it ("but") [S11][S12]. *Apply:* read your outline aloud with only "BUT" and "THEREFORE" between beats. Rewrite every "and then."
8. **Package honestly: legit bait, not click traps.** Muller argues that titles and thumbnails decide whether good work reaches people. He separates enticing-but-accurate packaging from misleading packaging [S13] *(secondary summaries)*. *Apply:* "Ratan Tata ne sahi problem pakdi. Phir bhi Nano fail hui. Kyun?" is legit bait. "The SHOCKING truth Tata hid" is a click trap, and it would be disrespectful in a tribute.

**Structure**

9. **Make chapters of 3–5 minutes, each a mini-episode.** In the edX data, median engagement topped out at about 6 minutes whatever the video's length, and viewers often watched less than half of videos longer than 9 minutes [S2]. A 15–20 minute video works only as 4–5 linked mini-episodes. *Apply:* each chapter opens a question, pays it off, and ends on a new question.
10. **Plan big re-engagements at about the 3 and 6 minute marks, and every 3–4 minutes after that.** The MrBeast guide calls for a strong "re-engagement" moment at about 3 minutes and another at about 6 minutes, plus quick progress in minutes 1–3 [S4]. Practitioner guides suggest a smaller "interrupt" every 60–90 seconds: a change of visual, voice, proof, question or framing [S14] *(secondary)*. *Apply:* see the beat sheet in A2.
11. **Give every story a spine.** Pixar's Story Spine, which Emma Coats listed as rule #4 of Pixar's storytelling rules and which came from improv teacher Kenn Adams, runs: *Once upon a time… Every day… One day… Because of that… Because of that… Until finally…* [S15][S16]. Adams warns that the spine is a skeleton, not the finished story [S16]. *Apply:* write one spine each for Nano, Airbnb and Amul on a single index card before you write any dialogue.
12. **Shape the whole video as "Man in Hole."** Vonnegut's most popular story shape: someone is doing fine, falls into trouble, climbs out, and ends up better off [S17][S18]. *Apply:* the **Nano is the fall** (hope → launch → slide). **Airbnb is the hand reaching down** (a small proof that going deeper works). **Amul is the climb out** (a nation-scale triumph that ends higher than where we started). The viewer should feel this shape without being told.
13. **Use expectation vs reality: make the viewer commit before you reveal.** Huron's theory of musical expectation, which also works for story, says emotion comes from predicting and then having the prediction confirmed or broken. A confident prediction that gets broken produces the strongest reaction [S19][S20]. Muller's misconception work (Rule 5) is the teaching version of the same idea. *Apply:* "Pause karo. Guess karo: Nano kyun fail hui? Price? Safety? Factory?" Wait a beat, then: "Teeno galat nahi hain. Par teeno *surface* hain." It is a soft reversal, but it gets the viewer to commit.
14. **Nest your open loops.** **Rule of thumb** built on Rules 6 and 10: keep one main loop open for the whole video ("What did Nano miss that Amul got right?"). Open and close 4–6 smaller loops inside it, and always open the next small loop before closing the current one. Close the main loop only at the climax.
15. **Write visual-first.** Johnny Harris says he learned "visual-first writing" at Vox: he writes the image and the line together instead of decorating narration with pictures afterwards [S21][S3]. *Apply:* use a two-column script, with **what we SEE** on the left and **what we HEAR** on the right. Any line with an empty left column gets rewritten or cut.
16. **Pay off the promise, then end quickly.** The MrBeast guide warns against signalling the ending early and says to end soon after a satisfying payoff, because people leave once they sense the wrap-up [S4]. *Apply:* no "So in conclusion…" After the Amul climax and the Iceberg handover, end within 45–60 seconds. Put any platform CTA in one line, ideally inside the final story beat.
17. **Keep the back half simple and the pace honest.** The same guide warns against dull stretches and against fake intensity [S4]. *Apply:* explanations of the Iceberg depths go in the middle, but each one is attached to a person, a photo or a number. Never present a framework as a bare list.
18. **Set personal stakes for a 17-year-old in the first minute.** Galloway: give viewers a reason to keep watching [S6]. *Apply:* "Agar aap kabhi kuch banana chahte ho, chahe startup ho, YouTube channel ho ya college fest, yeh galti aap bhi karoge. Sabse smart log bhi karte hain."
19. **Keep a promise ladder: tell them what's coming, in one line, twice.** **Rule of thumb** (a broadcast "tease" convention): at about 1:00 and at about the halfway point, preview the climax without giving it away. "Aur end mein ek kahani hai, jahan 5 lakh kisaanon ne ₹2-₹2 jodke film banayi." (That Manthan fact is sourced in [S62].)

**Delivery and measurement**

20. **Be conversational and personal, not formal.** Mayer's personalization principle, from multimedia-learning research covering 200+ experiments, says conversational wording beats formal wording for learning [S22][S23]. *Apply:* use "aap" or "tum" and "hum," not "one must consider."
21. **Bring energy, and don't slow down on purpose.** In the edX study, faster-speaking instructors drew more engagement, up to about 2× within the same length bucket. The authors traced this to enthusiasm, and the least energetic speakers were in the 145–165 wpm middle band. Their advice is to coach enthusiasm, reassure presenters that a fast pace is fine, and edit out dead pauses and fillers [S2]. *Apply:* see section E for when to *deliberately* slow down.
22. **Every visual must earn its place (coherence).** Mayer's coherence principle: extra words, pictures and sounds hurt learning [S22]. *Apply:* no decorative stock clips of "people walking in a city." Each cutaway should show a fact, a feeling or a place.
23. **Signal the structure.** Mayer's signalling principle: cues that show how material is organised help learning [S22]. *Apply:* the Iceberg depth-meter HUD (section C) is your signal. Viewers always know which depth they're at.
24. **Put the sponsor or CTA where it costs least.** The MrBeast guide notes that standard ad reads produce a visible retention drop [S4]. *Apply:* this is a free flagship, so don't put a platform pitch in the first 70% of the video.
25. **Use the retention graph as an editor.** Track CTR, average view duration and average view percentage, and compare like with like [S4][S6]. *Apply:* after publishing, mark every dip steeper than the curve around it. Those timestamps are next track's lessons.

## B. Emotional arc and goosebump engineering

### B1. What the research says triggers chills (frisson)

| Trigger | Evidence | How it maps to this video |
|---|---|---|
| **Expectation violation** (unexpected harmony, rhythm or melody; surprise in story) | Huron's contrastive valence: a negative or tense state followed by a positive resolution feels stronger *because of* the contrast [S19][S25]. Sloboda (1991) linked shivers to sudden changes in harmony or texture, and tears to appoggiaturas and sequences [S26] *(secondary)*. | The Depth 4 reversal at ~6:00. The Amul pivot from despair to "farmers own it." |
| **Crescendo, and a new voice or instrument entering** | Grewe et al. (2007) found chills correlate with new instruments or voices entering and with rising loudness and sharpness. Panksepp (1995) recorded chills at a crescendo [S27]. | At the Amul climax, add a new instrument (choir, sarangi or shehnai) on top of the pulse as the key line lands. |
| **Meaning and coherence** | Schoeller & Perlovsky (2016): an incoherent setup *suppressed* chills, a meaningful setup made the experience more pleasurable, and narrative chills clustered at the story's peak alongside strong empathy for characters [S28]. | The Iceberg must be crystal clear *before* the climax. Chills come from understanding, so the depth-4 payoff for Amul only hits if the viewer already holds the framework. |
| **Sudden intensification of togetherness (kama muta, "being moved")** | Fiske, Seibt and Schubert: when a communal bond suddenly intensifies (reunion, sacrifice, people becoming one), people report tears, goosebumps and warmth in the chest. Moment-by-moment ratings of "moved" tracked closeness, tears and goosebumps [S24][S29][S30]. It lasts about a minute or two [S30]. | **This is your strongest lever.** Five lakh farmers chipping in ₹2 each to fund a film about themselves. Farmers standing as owners. Tata's empathy for one family on one scooter. Show *collective* shots and faces, not logos. |
| **Peak experience and self-transcendence** | ChillsDB validated chill-inducing music, film and speech clips. Chills were linked to self-transcendence (feeling connected to something bigger) across 2,937 participants [S31][S32]. | End by connecting the viewer to something bigger: "Yeh desh aise hi bana hai." |
| **Individual difference** | Sachs et al. (2016): people prone to chills have more white-matter connections between auditory and emotional regions (a small sample of 10 vs 10) [S33]. | Not everyone will get chills. Design for "moved" in everyone and goosebumps in some. |

### B2. The goosebump recipe: five steps, used at most 2–3 times in the video

1. **Plant meaning early.** The viewer needs to know the framework and care about a person (Schoeller [S28]).
2. **Build tension.** Use an ostinato pulse, rising stakes, shorter sentences and faster cuts.
3. **Drop to silence for 1–3 seconds.** Cut music and SFX entirely, hold the host's face or a still photo, and let the line breathe. Bernardi et al. (2006) found that a pause after music lowered heart rate, blood pressure and breathing, even *below* the resting baseline. Silence resets the body, so the next swell lands on a calmer, more receptive viewer [S34].
4. **Reveal on a single line, low and slow** (see E3), with the key word in Hindi if that is the more emotional word: *izzat*, *bharosa*, *apna*.
5. **Swell with something new entering**, such as a new instrument, choir, higher register or wider shot [S27]. Show a collective image: many faces, a crowd, hands (kama muta [S24]).

**Where to put them:** (a) a mini chill in the cold open at the launch-day applause; (b) a "moved" beat in the Ratan Tata scooter-origin moment and the tribute at ~7:30; (c) **the main chill at ~80–85% runtime**, the Amul collective triumph. Don't put two big chills within 3 minutes of each other. The second one feels manipulative. **Rule of thumb**, consistent with kama muta lasting only a minute or two [S30].

### B3. Designing the up-and-down arc (failure → triumph)

- **Contrast creates intensity.** Huron's contrastive valence [S19] means the Amul triumph needs a real low point first. Spend 30–45 seconds on the farmers' situation before Kurien: middlemen, milk spoiling, no bargaining power. Let it hurt.
- **Alternate the energy every 2–4 minutes.** The edX chunking evidence [S2] and the MrBeast re-engagement timing [S4] both point to regular resets. Plot your script on the emotion column in A2. If three chapters in a row sit at the same level, rewrite one.
- **Honour, don't mock.** The Nano is a tribute. The arc should feel like "a great man saw the right problem, and here is the one depth he missed," so the sadness is *respectful* and never cynical. That makes the viewer trust the host enough to feel the later triumph.
- **Silence is punctuation, so give it a hierarchy.** **Rule of thumb:** a 0.5 s micro-pause marks a comma-level thought; a 1–1.5 s beat lets a fact land; a 2–3 s total silence (music out) marks a turning point. Use the 2–3 s level no more than 3–4 times in the whole video.
- **Change the music at the turning point; don't just swell it.** At a reversal ("Problem price nahi tha"), hard-stop the current cue, hold a beat of silence or room tone, then start a *new* cue in a different key or instrumentation. The listener's prediction breaks, and that break *is* the expectation-violation trigger [S19][S26].

## C. Visual system recommendation

### C1. The style: "Field Notebook, Decoded"

The brand promise is *decoding what everyone already saw*, so the visual system should have two layers:

- **The SEEN layer:** real archival photos and footage, news clippings, maps and products as everyone saw them. Paper-textured, slightly desaturated, with real grain.
- **The DECODED layer:** a hand-annotation overlay in one signal colour that circles, underlines, draws arrows, and writes margin notes on top of the SEEN layer. This is the brand's signature move, and it is "decoding" made literal.

This is coherent with the Iceberg, where above the waterline is what's seen and below is what's decoded. It is achievable for a small team, and it draws on proven references: Johnny Harris-style paper maps (built with GEOlayers in After Effects; a texture laid on with Multiply blend plus displacement [S35][S36][S37]), Vox-style explainers that mix data visualisation, animation, sound design and narration [S38], and Kurzgesagt's lesson that a consistent, *owned* illustration style is a moat. They draw most scenes from scratch to keep the look distinctive [S39].

### C2. Palette guidance (India-first, tied to the Iceberg)

| Role | Suggested colour | Use |
|---|---|---|
| Base / paper | Warm off-white "khadi / newsprint" (~#F2ECE1) | Backgrounds, cards |
| Ink | Near-black (~#1A1A1A) | Type, line art |
| **Signal / Decode** | Haldi-marigold (~#F2A900) | ONLY for annotations, highlights and the "decoded" word. Never decorative. |
| Failure / warning | Sindoor red (~#C1272D) | Falling charts, "wrong depth" stamps |
| Depth 1 → 4 | Light aqua → teal → indigo → near-black indigo (~#BFE3E6 → #3A8FA0 → #24356B → #0E1433) | The Iceberg bands and the depth HUD |
| Hidden desire | Gold glow (~#E8B547) on the darkest indigo | Depth 4 only: "the treasure at the bottom" |

**Rules:** one signal colour, used sparingly, so its meaning stays sharp. Pair colour with number and icon at every depth so it works for colour-blind viewers and in thumbnails. Keep skin tones natural. Grade archival photos warm, not teal-orange.

**Type:** one sturdy sans with a matching Devanagari family (e.g. Noto Sans plus Noto Sans Devanagari, or Mukta/Hind, which are free on Google Fonts and have Latin and Devanagari together), plus one handwritten face for annotations. Kinetic typography only for 3–5 "thesis lines" per video, so each one lands.

### C3. Motion vocabulary: 10 named moves, reused in every track

| Move | What it is | When |
|---|---|---|
| **The Pin** | An archival photo drops onto paper with a soft shadow and a paper-rustle SFX | Introducing a person or moment |
| **The Breath (2.5D parallax)** | A photo split into foreground, mid and background layers with a slow camera push. The "Kid Stays in the Picture" effect, an evolution of the Ken Burns pan-and-zoom [S40][S41] | Every emotional archival photo. Max 6–8 s, slow; never wobble. |
| **The Circle** | A hand-drawn marigold circle or underline writes on | Decoding a detail |
| **The Split** | A split screen, "What they SAID" vs "What they DID" | Depth 1 vs Depth 2, every time |
| **The Route** | A map line animating between places (GEOlayers or manual masks [S35][S36]) | Singur → Sanand; villages → Anand; SF apartment → NYC hosts |
| **The Counter** | A number rolling up or down in lakh/crore, with a dual-label tooltip | Prices, sales, farmers, litres |
| **The Fall / Rise line** | A line chart drawing on in ink, red if falling and marigold if rising, always with a zero baseline | Sales slide; milk output |
| **The Dive** | The camera descends through the Iceberg with a depth counter and a sonar ping | Moving to a deeper depth |
| **The Freeze** | Freeze frame plus desaturation plus a record-stop | The reversal moment ("BUT…") |
| **The Stamp** | A rubber-stamp verdict ("SOLVED AT DEPTH 2") | Chapter endings, scoring each story |

**Evidence board:** use it **once**, for the Nano post-mortem ("Case file: why did it fail?") with a pin, string and clues ruled out one by one: price → safety → factory → **status**. Used more than once, it reads like a true-crime cliché.

**Reconstructions:** use stylised illustration (flat or ink-wash, clearly not a photograph) for moments with no archive, such as the family on the scooter in the rain, Kurien in early Anand, or the Airbnb founders' air mattresses. **Don't use photoreal AI images of real people.** See D5.

### C4. The Problem Iceberg graphic: a spec for reuse across 50 tracks

**Concept:** an iceberg in water seen in cross-section, with a **depth gauge** (a sonar or depth-meter ruler) down the left side reading −1, −2, −3, −4. At the very bottom there is a small gold glow, *the hidden desire*. This is the unique twist: **in our iceberg, the bottom isn't danger, it's treasure.**

| Element | Spec |
|---|---|
| **Waterline** | A bold horizontal line labelled "EVERYONE SEES ↑ / FEW DECODE ↓". It carries the brand line every time. |
| **Depth 1: What people SAY** | Above the water, white ice. Icon: speech bubble. Label EN/HI: *What they say / Kya bolte hain* |
| **Depth 2: What people DO** | Just below the water, light aqua. Icon: hand with a tool (the workaround, *jugaad*). *What they do / Kya karte hain* |
| **Depth 3: WHY it happens** | Teal to indigo. Icon: roots or gear. *Why it happens / Asli wajah* |
| **Depth 4: What they WANT** | Darkest indigo with a gold glow. Icon: a key, plus five sub-icons that can be swapped per story: **Status** (crown), **Trust** (handshake), **Dignity** (raised head / *izzat*), **Control** (steering wheel), **Time** (clock). *What they really want / Asli chahat* |
| **Anchor marker** | A small anchor icon that drops to the depth where a company *actually* built its solution. Nano: the anchor stops at Depth 3 (affordable family transport) and the desire glow (status/dignity) stays unlit. Amul: the anchor reaches Depth 4 (control/dignity through ownership) and the glow lights up. This makes the framework *scoreable*, which makes it memorable and shareable. |
| **Depth HUD** | A mini version (the depth gauge only, about 6% of frame width, top-left) stays on screen whenever the story is "inside" a depth. It works as a constant signal (Mayer [S22]). |
| **Sound signature** | A soft sonar ping for each depth descended, lower in pitch at each level. Depth 4 gets a warm chime. This becomes the brand's audio logo. |
| **Motion** | Descent = the camera dives with bubbles rising. Ascent (summary) = a pull-out to the full iceberg. Always 1.0–1.5 s. Consistent easing. |
| **Build** | An After Effects template (.mogrt) or a Premiere/Resolve equivalent, with editable text fields, swappable icons, the anchor position as a slider, and EN/HI label toggles. Export a still version for thumbnails and a vertical version for Shorts or Reels. |
| **Thumbnail rule** | A simplified iceberg with **two** depths visible, one product photo, and the anchor. Three elements or fewer, bright, and readable in dark mode (Galloway: three or fewer elements, bright [S6]). |

### C5. Positioning honestly: how our iceberg differs from the systems-thinking iceberg

**Prior art exists, so acknowledge it.**

- **Systems-thinking iceberg:** Events → Patterns → Structures → Mental Models. Events are produced by patterns, patterns are held in place by structures, and structures grow out of mental models. It is commonly credited to Donella Meadows, Peter Senge and the Academy for Systems Change. Some trace the iceberg metaphor further back, to Edward T. Hall's 1976 cultural iceberg [S42][S43][S44]. Its origin is contested and none of the sources I found is primary.
- Other well-known icebergs: Hall's cultural iceberg [S43], and the general "tip of the iceberg" metaphor.
- Our depths 1–2 restate a well-established user-research principle: **watch what people do, not what they say** (Nielsen Norman Group's "First Rule of Usability? Don't Listen to Users") [S45]. Depth 4 matches **Jobs-to-be-Done**, where people "hire" products for functional, social and emotional jobs (Christensen et al., HBR 2016) [S46]. Depth 3 is the root-cause logic of the "5 Whys."

| | Systems-thinking iceberg | **Problem Iceberg (ours)** |
|---|---|---|
| Object of study | A *system* (why events keep recurring) | A *person's* problem (why customers behave as they do) |
| Who uses it | Policy makers, organisations, analysts | Founders, makers, students building something |
| Bottom layer | Mental models (beliefs that hold the system in place) | **Hidden desire** (status, trust, dignity, control, time): an *opportunity* |
| Feeling at the bottom | Diagnosis | **Treasure**: where the business is |
| Output | Leverage points for systemic change | **Where to anchor your solution**, and a score (the anchor marker) |
| Signature move | Moving from reaction to redesign | Moving from "what they said" to "what they want" |

**Suggested on-screen credit, once in Track 1:** *"The iceberg is an old idea, used in systems thinking and user research. Ours asks a narrower question: how deep does your solution go?"* It is honest, it builds credibility, and it pre-empts "this is just the Meadows iceberg" comments.

## D. Legal visual sourcing in India

> **This is not legal advice.** Have an IP lawyer review the final asset list for a flagship that sits on a commercial platform. Keep a **clearance log** for every asset, recording its source, licence, date, scope and a screenshot of the terms.

### D1. What Indian law allows (and doesn't)

- **"Fair dealing," not US "fair use."** Section 52(1)(a) of the Copyright Act, 1957 permits fair dealing with any work for (i) private or personal use including research, (ii) **criticism or review**, of that work or any other work, and (iii) **reporting of current events and current affairs**. The use must fall within one of these listed *purposes*, and it must also be *fair* [S47][S48].
- **Fairness is judged qualitatively.** Courts look at purpose and character (is it transformative?), the nature of the work, the amount and substantiality (a few seconds can still be the "heart" of a work), and the market effect. **There is no safe clip length** [S47][S48].
- **Helpful precedents:** *TV Today v Newslaundry* (Delhi HC) found that very short news clips used for critique, which did not substitute for the original, supported fair dealing. *India TV v Yashraj* applied *de minimis* to brief musical excerpts. **Cautionary ones:** *Super Cassettes v Chintamani Rao* held that whole songs dressed up as "news" were not fair. *Shemaroo v News Nation* (Bombay HC 2022) rejected fair dealing where a channel used films after its licence ended [S48][S49].
- **The platform reality:** YouTube's Content ID cannot assess Section 52. News agencies have sent strikes over short clips; in the ANI vs YouTubers dispute, Mohak Mangal alleged ANI demanded ₹45–50 lakh in licence fees, and the matter was pending in the Delhi HC as of mid-2025 [S47]. **Even a valid legal defence can cost you the channel's standing before it is ever tested.**
- **Trademarks (logos):** Section 30(1) of the Trade Marks Act, 1999 allows using a registered mark to identify the owner's goods, provided the use is "in accordance with honest practices" and does not take unfair advantage of, or harm, the mark's reputation [S50]. **Cautionary case:** in *Dabur v Dhruv Rathee* (Calcutta HC, 2023), a juice-analysis video that showed Dabur's ads and an allegedly distorted "Real" pack was ordered edited and later taken down. It settled in 2024 with all "Real" references removed or blurred [S51][S52].
- **Personality and publicity rights:** a Commons licence covers copyright only. Personality and privacy rights may still apply to real people [S53].

### D3. AI imagery: the risks and the rules

- **Platform:** YouTube requires disclosure when realistic content is made with altered or synthetic media, including generative AI. Clearly unrealistic or animated content is exempt, and YouTube may apply a label creators can't remove [S64][S65].
- **Indian law:** the IT (Intermediary) Amendment Rules 2026, notified 10 Feb 2026 and in force from 20 Feb 2026, define "synthetically generated information" as content that is artificially created or altered and *appears real*, and they require labelling and provenance metadata. A further draft (April 2026) proposes continuous visible labels; its status is unconfirmed [S66][S67].
- **Credibility:** Netflix's *What Jennifer Did* (2024) was publicly criticised for apparently AI-generated or AI-altered photos of a real person, with no disclosure in the credits [S68]. In a tribute to Ratan Tata, a single "AI-looking" frame could hijack the comments.
- **Industry standard:** the Archival Producers Alliance's *Best Practices for Use of Generative AI in Documentaries* (Sept 2024) recommends being transparent with audiences (on-screen labels, narration or credits) and keeping cue sheets of all AI elements [S69].
- **Our rule:** **illustration that is obviously illustration.** If AI tools are used at all, use them only for non-human textures or backgrounds, never for the likeness of a real person, and label them on screen ("Illustration" or "Reconstruction") and in the credits.

### D4. Recommended approach

1. **Build 70% of the visuals from owned assets:** host footage, the Iceberg system, maps, charts, illustrated reconstructions and kinetic type. They are legally clean, they become brand IP, and they are reusable across 50 tracks.
2. **Licence 15–20% of the visuals, the "hero" archival moments:** about 6–10 editorial photos (Getty/PTI/Reuters) for the Nano launch, Ratan Tata, Kurien, Anand and Airbnb's early days, plus a short *Manthan* clip if GCMMF/FHF agree.
3. **Fill the remaining 10–15% with stock B-roll** (Storyblocks or Artgrid): Indian roads, two-wheelers in rain, milk collection and dairy.
4. **Send permission emails on day 1** (Tata Motors comms, Amul/GCMMF, Airbnb newsroom, PIB Photo Division, NFDC). Answers take weeks.
5. **Credits:** use an on-screen source tag on every archival image (small, bottom-left, e.g. "Photo: Getty Images / Bloomberg") plus a full credit list in the description.

## E. Voice and delivery coaching guide

### E1. Core techniques

| Technique | What to do | Source |
|---|---|---|
| **Pace as a dial, not a setting** | Explanations at about **150–170 wpm** (conversational). Tension and montage at **170–190 wpm** in short bursts. Reveals and sad beats at **110–130 wpm**, with pauses. Online viewers tolerate faster speech than live audiences. Engagement rose with pace in the edX data, driven by enthusiasm, and the flattest speakers sat at 145–165 wpm. | [S2][S70][S71] |
| **The power pause** | Pause **before** a key point (anticipation) and **after** it (lets it land). Major pauses run about 2 s, roughly "three beats." It feels endless to you and deliberate to them. Replace every "um" with silence. | [S70][S72][S73] |
| **Pitch drop on key lines** | Authority lives in the lower-middle of *your natural* range, with a downward inflection at the end of statements. Kill uptalk, where statements rise like questions. Don't force a fake-deep voice; it tires quickly and sounds performed. | [S74][S70][S75] |
| **Volume drop to make people lean in** | Dropping slightly for an important point pulls listeners in. Raise it for passion. Watch out for the common fault of letting volume trail off at the *end* of sentences, exactly where the key word usually sits. | [S76][S73] |
| **Stress words** | One stressed word per sentence, two at most. If everything is stressed, nothing is. Mark them in the script (bold or underline). | [S77] |
| **Sentence-length variation** | Short for impact. Longer for context and detail, building a picture. Then short again. Read the script aloud to catch lines that look fine on paper but trip the tongue. | [S71][S78] |
| **Chunking** | Speak in 3–5-word phrases with micro-pauses between them. | [S71] |
| **Talk to one person through the lens** | Broadcasters picture a *single* listener. Say "you/aap," never "hi viewers/dosto sab log." Each viewer should feel addressed personally. | [S79] |
| **Breath** | Diaphragmatic breathing: the belly moves out on the inhale, not the chest. Breath carries emotion; NHK narrators teach that putting a deep breath into a word like "nostalgic" makes it *felt*. Use a deep breath only at key moments. | [S71][S80] |
| **Write for the ear** | One thought per sentence. Say numbers in a speakable way ("lagbhag ek-tihaai," not "31.7%"). Front-load the subject. | [S78][S81] |

### E2. Teleprompter: making it not sound read

1. **Put the text right next to the lens.** Use big type and short lines [S82].
2. **Read one line ahead of your voice** (the "eye-lead"). This is the single biggest skill and takes 3–4 sessions to become automatic [S83].
3. **Mark the script** (Kerry Barrett, former news anchor): an ellipsis for a slow-down, bold or asterisks for stress, "x" for a one-second pause and "xx" for two, parentheses to keep a thought together, and one sentence per paragraph [S77].
4. **Talk it before you read it.** Explain the section to your phone without the script, then read the script and compare. Copy the places where you naturally sped up, slowed down or got excited [S77].
5. **Don't slow down because it's a prompter.** New presenters slow down and go flat when focused on the words [S77]. The edX data agrees: energy beats caution [S2].
6. **Move your head slightly and gesture.** Natural movement hides eye-tracking [S77].
7. **Write names and Hindi words phonetically** in the prompter [S84].
8. **Shoot in chapters, not in one take.** Do 2–3 takes per paragraph and keep the best. Use a B-camera at 45° or a 110–120% punch-in to hide cuts.
9. **Hybrid mode:** for the emotional beats (the tribute, the Amul climax), try bullet points on the prompter instead of full text, then pick whichever take sounds more alive. **Rule of thumb.**

### E3. How to deliver each moment

| Moment | Pace | Pitch | Volume | Pauses | Face and body | Music under it | Hinglish delivery tip |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| **Reveal / reversal** ("Problem price nahi tha.") | Slow, 110–130 wpm. Decelerate *into* the line. | Lower-middle; falling finish on the key word | Drop slightly, then land the key word at normal volume | 1.5–2 s **before**, 1–2 s **after** | Still. Eyes on the lens. ### E4. Drills: a two-week program, about 20 minutes a day

**Warm-up (5–7 minutes, before every session and shoot):**
- Hum gently on "mmm" for 1 minute, gliding low to high. Then 1 minute of lip trills. Then 2 minutes of straw phonation: hum or glide through a narrow straw, optionally into a glass of water. These are semi-occluded vocal tract (SOVT) exercises; partly closing the mouth builds back-pressure that helps the vocal folds vibrate efficiently [S88][S71].
- Tongue twisters, slow then fast: "Red leather, yellow leather," "Unique New York," and a Hindi one such as "Kachcha papad, pakka papad" [S71].
- Articulation: read one paragraph with a pencil held between your teeth, then without it [S89].
- Hydrate. Avoid shooting immediately after a heavy meal or dairy. **Rule of thumb.**

| Drill | How | Fixes |
|---|---|---|
| **1. WPM calibration** | Record 60 s of explanation and count the words. Do it again for a "sad" paragraph. The target gap between your explanation pace and your sad pace is at least 40 wpm. | No pace variation [S70][S75] |
| **2. Stress shift** | Say one sentence 5 times, stressing a different word each time ("*Nano* sasti thi" / "Nano *sasti* thi" / …). Notice how the meaning changes. | Robotic stress [S89] |
| **3. Period drill** | Read your 10 key lines, dropping pitch on the final two words. Record and listen. | Uptalk [S75] |
| **4. Pause counting** | Mark x/xx pauses in the script and count "one-Mississippi" silently. Then perform without counting and check whether the pauses survived. | Rushing [S77][S72] |
| **5. Talk-then-read** | Ad-lib a section into your phone, read the scripted version, and compare. Rewrite any scripted line that sounds less natural than your ad-lib. | Sounding read [S77] |
| **6. Caption reading** | Mute a dialogue-heavy show with captions on and read the characters' lines aloud as they scroll. | Prompter flatness [S77] |
| **7. Lean-in** | Deliver one paragraph three ways: normal, 30% quieter, and quieter with slower pace. Record and pick the most compelling. | Volume monotony [S76] |
| **8. One-person photo** | Tape a photo of one real 17-year-old (a cousin, a student) beside the lens and talk only to them. | Announcer voice [S79] |
| **9. Speak-singing** | Read a children's story aloud, exaggerating pitch by about 50%, then bring it down to 70% of that. | Monotone [S71] |
| **10. Emotion ladder** | Say "Aur phir… Nano band ho gayi" at five intensities from 1 (flat fact) to 5 (crying). Find the 2–3 that feels true. That's your level for sad beats. | Over- or under-acting |
| **11. Breath placement** | Mark breath points (/) in the script every 1–2 lines. Practise one deep breath *before* each emotional line. | Gasping, running out of air [S80] |
| **12. Full-chapter run** | From day 8, perform one whole chapter on camera daily. Watch it back muted (check face and energy) and then audio-only (check voice). | Integration |

### E5. Hinglish register tips for a 16–28 audience

- **Choose the base language per section and stay consistent.** Code-mixing among Indian youth follows rules and builds in-group solidarity and emotional nuance [S90]. A useful split: **Hindi carries emotion** (*izzat, bharosa, sapna, jugaad, apna*). **English carries the framework and business terms** (problem, root cause, customer, depth). Keep the Iceberg labels bilingual and *identical in every track*.
- **Switch codes at the punchline.** Research on Indian media code-switching notes that younger audiences follow fast English-Hindi switching well, and that prosodic marking of switches affects engagement [S85]. Set up in one language and land in the other.
- **Pick "aap" or "tum" and keep it.** "Aap" is warm and respectful, and works across Class 10 students and their parents watching alongside. "Tum" feels more peer-like but can sound casual in a tribute. **Rule of thumb:** use "aap" for Track 1.
- **Say numbers the Indian way** ("ek lakh," "paanch lakh kisaan") and put the dual format on screen (₹1,00,000 ≈ $X at the time).
- **Avoid forced slang.** Phrases like "no cap" or "it's giving" date fast and read as an adult trying too hard. Authentic warmth and the conversational register Mayer recommends [S22] beat trend words.
- **Pronounce names right.** Write *Verghese* (Var-geez) and *Kurien* (Koo-ree-en) phonetically in the prompter [S84].

### E6. Common mistakes of new presenters (and the fix)

| Mistake | Fix |
|---|---|
| Going slower and flatter on the prompter | Read one line ahead; bring the energy; talk-then-read drill [S77][S83][S2] |
| Uptalk on statements | Period drill [S75] |
| Volume fading at sentence ends | Land the last word; mark it in bold [S73] |
| Filler words ("toh basically," "matlab," "um") | Pause instead; edit out the leftovers [S72][S2] |
| Stressing every word | One stress word per sentence [S77] |
| Performing sadness (a quivering voice, a frown) | Understate it and let the facts and silence work [S86][S87] |
| Shouting the triumph | Energy from the breath and face, not volume; pause before the line |
| Smiling through serious lines (nervous habit) | Watch the muted playback; the emotion ladder drill |
| Monotone because the eyes are glued to the text | Mark the script; move your head; gesture [S77] |
| Generic "Hey guys, welcome back" intro | Start inside the story [S6] |
| Shooting the entire script in one marathon take | Shoot paragraph by paragraph; take breaks every 20 minutes |

**On-camera presence:** frame a medium close-up (chest up) for explanation, and **punch in to a tight close-up for sad and reveal lines**; the face carries trust. Keep your hands in frame for the "depth" gesture. Use a lav or shotgun mic about 20–30 cm from the mouth. Record room tone for 30 s at every setup so silences can be edited cleanly.

## F. Music and sound design

### F1. Scoring each emotional phase

| Phase | Musical ingredients | India-first signature instrument | Where in Track 1 |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Tension** | Low drones, a pulsing ostinato, ticking, minor key, a slowly rising pitch (Shepard-style riser) | Low tanpura drone; muted tabla pulse | Nano's slide; the farmers' plight |
| **Loss** | Sparse solo instrument, lots of space, or silence | Sarangi or solo cello; a single piano | Tribute beat (~7:30); the death of the Nano |
| **Wonder / curiosity** | Airy pads, arpeggios, gentle plucks, major-7 colours | Bansuri (bamboo flute); santoor | Iceberg dives; maps; the Airbnb idea |
| **Triumph** | Build from the pulse, then add percussion, then a new voice or choir, plus a key lift. Crescendo plus a new entry is the chills recipe [S27]. | Dhol or tabla build; shehnai or choir on top | Amul climax (~14–16 min) |
| **Light / hustle** | Plucky, bouncy, staccato | Ukulele-style pluck or a light tabla groove | Airbnb's cereal-box hustle |

**Rules:**
- **Spot the score from the script, not the edit.** Mark every emotional shift in the script's "SEE" column (Rule 15) and assign a cue. Kurzgesagt commissions an original soundtrack for each video [S39]. Vox explainers treat music as part of the storytelling and balance it with writing and visuals [S38].
- **Change, don't just swell.** At every BUT/THEREFORE turn, end the cue (a button ending or hard stop), hold 0.5–2 s, and start a new cue [S19][S26].
- **Silence is a cue too.** Two to four total silences (music *and* SFX out, room tone only) at the biggest lines [S34].
- **Mixing:** **rule of thumb:** duck music about 10–15 dB under the voice while the host speaks, and let it rise to full level in gaps and on visual-only beats. Deliver the final mix at about −14 LUFS integrated with a true-peak ceiling of about −1 dBTP. YouTube turns loud mixes down but won't raise quiet ones [S91] *(secondary)*.
- **Build a sonic brand:** commission a 3–5 s theme, a "decode motif," from an Indian composer. Use it in the title card, the Iceberg dives and the end card of every track. Owning it also avoids Content ID problems.

## G. Editing and captions

### G1. Cut frequency and pace

- **The context:** James Cutting's analysis of 150–160 Hollywood films found average shot length fell from about 12 s (1930s) to about 2.5–4 s (2000s). Shot lengths increasingly follow a 1/f pattern resembling the rhythm of human attention [S93][S94]. Shorter shots mean more control over attention, but this is a *rhythm*, not a speed limit.
- **Guidance for Track 1:** **rule of thumb**, informed by the evidence above and practitioner guides suggesting a visual change every 10–15 s [S14] *(secondary)*:
  - **Explanatory sections:** a visual change (new shot, graphic build, punch-in or annotation) every **4–8 s**.
  - **Tension and montage:** **1–3 s** cuts, synced to the music pulse.
  - **Emotional beats (sad, reveal, tribute):** **hold 6–15 s** on a face or a parallax photo. The *slowdown itself* signals importance.
  - **The host on camera** should run no more than 20–30 s without a cutaway or punch-in, except in deliberate intimate moments.
- **Alternate the host and the visuals.** The edX data found more engagement in videos that alternated the instructor's talking head with slides than with either alone [S2].
- **Keep deliberate pauses and cut accidental ones.** Edit out fillers and dead air [S2], but **protect the marked power pauses** (x/xx) in the edit.
- **Use J- and L-cuts** so the audio of the next scene leads the picture (J), or the current audio carries over the next picture (L). It smooths the BUT/THEREFORE flow.
- **Use punch-ins** (a 110–120% digital zoom) on key words for emphasis, at most once every 30–45 s. Overused, they jitter.

### G2. Captions that help retention

- **The evidence:** in a Verizon Media and Publicis survey (2019, 5,616 US adults), 80% said they are more likely to watch a whole video when captions are available, 69% watch with the sound off in public, and 80% of caption users have no hearing impairment [S95] *(secondary reporting of a survey)*. Indian mobile viewing in public places makes this even more relevant. **Rule of thumb.**
- **The learning nuance:** Mayer's redundancy principle says full on-screen text *plus* narration *plus* graphics can hurt learning, *except* when the text is short, highlights key words, and sits near the relevant graphic [S22][S23].
- **The two-track caption strategy:**
  1. **Closed captions (SRT) for 100% of the video,** uploaded in English and in Hinglish written in Roman script (and Hindi in Devanagari if resources allow). Don't rely on auto-captions for Hinglish; fix names and numbers by hand.
  2. **Burned-in "emphasis captions" only for key lines:** 2–5 words, the stressed word in marigold, positioned in the lower third and clear of graphics. Use them for thesis lines, reveals, numbers and Iceberg labels. **Not** full-sentence karaoke captions over a 16:9 explainer; that clutters the graphics and fights the redundancy principle.
- **Caption style:** at most 2 lines, about 32–42 characters per line, a sentence-case sans with a Devanagari companion, white text with a subtle shadow or 60% black box, and the bottom 15% of the frame kept clear for the YouTube player UI. **Rule of thumb**, aligned with broadcast subtitle conventions.
- **Kinetic typography:** reserve animated full-screen type for 3–5 thesis lines ("RIGHT PROBLEM. WRONG DEPTH."). Rarity is what makes them land.

### G3. Chapters

- **YouTube requirements** (Help Center, as described by secondary sources): put the timestamps in the description, start the first at **00:00**, list **at least 3** in ascending order, and make each chapter **at least 10 s** long [S96].
- **Chapter titles are curiosity hooks, not labels.** For example: `00:00 Launch day, 2008` · `01:00 The family on the scooter` · `04:00 Why it slid` · `06:00 The depth everyone missed` · `08:30 Strangers' beds (Airbnb)` · `12:00 5 lakh farmers, ₹2 each` · `16:00 The Problem Iceberg`.
- **Match chapters to the 3–5 minute mini-episode structure** (Rule 9), so a viewer who skims still meets the Iceberg.
